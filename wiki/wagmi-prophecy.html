The WAGMI Prophecy

🌙 Community Lore 📅 Last updated: October 2026 📂 Lore

The WAGMI Prophecy carries the HODL Wars’ promise that “We’re All Gonna Make It.” It is a declaration of hope whose hardest word is all. Across the Year 3008 world, people use it to welcome, encourage, joke and rally. Its value is tested when someone is tired, doubtful, unsuccessful or unable to follow the path that a confident speaker imagined for them.

Origins and the meaning of a shared future

WAGMI expands to “We’re All Gonna Make It,” a phrase used in online crypto and NFT culture as encouragement, greeting, joke and sometimes promotion. Its fictional role is larger: W81’s subject register calls the WAGMI Prophecy a guiding declaration of optimism for the HODL Wars and its Diamond Hands tradition. The phrase’s cultural circulation does not establish one documented moment when a universal doctrine began.

Within the Year 3008 world, the promise is attractive because survival so often seems organised around somebody being left outside. A protected room has limited space; a transport has limited capacity; a repair can serve only one urgent job first. To say “all” is to refuse to treat those limits as a satisfactory moral conclusion. It does not make the limits disappear. The prophecy becomes demanding at the moment somebody asks how the next person will be included.

The prophecy text and its disputed force

The older wiki preserves this declaration: WAGMI. Diamond Hands forever. The moon awaits all true believers. It belongs to the fictional chronicle tradition. It is not a quotation from Bitcoin’s historical whitepaper, a verified ancient prediction or a promise about real investment returns. The Scroll tradition places such declarations among later interpretations and supplements.

The wording contains its own argument. “We’re all” sounds unconditional; “all true believers” can make belonging depend on whoever decides what belief should look like. Some readers hear endurance and solidarity. Others hear a test that a tired, frightened or dissenting person can fail. The prophecy’s history is therefore also a struggle over whether its promise widens the circle or gives a confident speaker permission to draw its boundary.

Lantern Recess

Lantern Recess is a small gathering place attached to an outside repair and lodging route. Its name comes from a sheltered space where a lamp can remain visible without standing in the path of people carrying loads. Travellers leave short hopeful messages there, and regular workers read them when the day’s useful light has gone.

Pella Dawn, who helps organise the evening room, dislikes declarations that demand an immediate reply. Ren Latchwood, a letter carrier with a taste for ceremony, likes a spoken chorus. Neither position makes either of them the keeper of the prophecy. Their disagreement begins with ordinary circumstances: a room full of people whose work ends at different times, some ready for company and others unable to bear another request.

Pella keeps the lamp lit when no chorus is planned. Ren initially finds that wasteful. She answers that a welcome which depends on joining the song has already become a narrower thing.

No Chorus Night

No Chorus Night began after a carrier returned with a damaged load and news of a household whose onward journey remained uncertain. Ren proposed a WAGMI reading to lift the room’s spirits. One traveller asked whether they could eat first. Another asked that the missing news not be turned into a lesson about belief.

The meal continued without a speech. Pella took requests for messages that could be carried the next day; Ren helped record them. The silence was not harmonious. Several regulars felt that the room had abandoned its custom exactly when people needed reassurance. Others were relieved that they did not have to perform optimism while deciding what to do next.

At the following gathering, Ren described the night without naming it a failure. He had learned that people could act on hope without saying the familiar words together. No Chorus Night became a local story about listening before offering comfort, rather than a prohibition on public joy.

The Long Tomorrow cloth

The Long Tomorrow is a repaired cloth hung inside the recess. Its first inscription promised that everyone would arrive together. Later hands added small marks naming tasks: a message carried, a roof patched, a room prepared, a return still awaited. Pella permits additions only with the consent of people named, because the cloth is visible to strangers as well as friends.

Ren once tried to arrange the marks into a rising path toward a bright final panel. The design would have looked coherent from across the room. It also required moving an unresolved absence into the middle as if it were merely an obstacle already passed. The affected household asked him to leave the space open.

The cloth now has an awkward unfinished area. Visitors sometimes assume it is damage and offer to repair it. Pella explains that the space is being kept, not neglected. The Long Tomorrow’s hope depends on allowing an unfinished life to remain unfinished in the story.

WAGMI and NGMI

The NGMI Chronicles carry the opposite phrase: “Not Gonna Make It.” In the older market allegory, the contrast divided steadfast holders from people who sold, panicked or failed to learn. Used without care, that division mistakes one visible decision for a whole life and treats hardship as proof of inadequate conviction.

The more useful fictional tension concerns what a community does after disappointment. Does it preserve the person’s place, listen to the reasons for their decision and help with consequences? Or does it protect its own optimistic story by declaring that the person never truly belonged? Redemption cannot be meaningful if the only permitted return is agreement with those who pronounced the original judgement.

At Lantern Recess, a traveller who withdraws from a collective undertaking can still receive a meal. That practice has costs and boundaries; the room cannot support every request. It does reject the idea that care must first be earned by repeating an approved account of success.

The work behind a hopeful room

A lamp needs attention. A room needs cleaning and fuel. Someone must collect bedding, answer late arrivals and settle disagreements over noise. WAGMI meetings depend on this work even when their public images show only smiling faces and a shining phrase.

Pella changes the gathering list so that preparing and clearing the room receive the same space as speaking. Ren worries that the change will make a celebration look like a duty roster. A worker asks whose duty had kept the celebration looking effortless before. The new list remains informal, but it makes substitution possible when a regular helper is ill or simply needs a night elsewhere.

The first substitute misunderstands the order of preparation and leaves the room briefly cold. Nobody calls the error evidence that shared labour cannot work. They alter the instructions. Hope acquires a practical form in that willingness to revise an arrangement without making the person who struggled carry its entire failure.

Greetings, jokes and public declarations

WAGMI can be whispered to one person, painted on a wall or shouted by a crowd. The scale changes what it does. A private reassurance can leave room for an answer. A crowd’s declaration can make uncertainty difficult to voice. A joke can release pressure or disguise contempt, depending on who is expected to laugh.

Ren collects variants from travellers, including the familiar hesitant “WAGMI, probably.” He likes the way humour admits that the speaker cannot command the future. Pella asks him to note where he heard a phrase without treating the traveller as its inventor. The distinction matters when a popular line becomes attached to one charismatic speaker and the people who kept it alive disappear from the story.

One message arrives with permission to be read once and then returned. Ren wants to retain it because it expresses the room’s purpose unusually well. Pella points to the condition. They read it, return it and leave a short record that an account was shared under a limited agreement. The absence frustrates later visitors who have heard about the beautiful words. It also preserves the speaker’s ability to offer something without having it become permanent communal property. A hopeful room must sometimes accept that a valuable story is not its story to keep.

Their small collection has no approved final wording. It preserves the circumstances of use: a departure, a difficult repair, an apology after an overconfident promise. The same letters can carry different obligations in each setting.

Different factions, different hopes

A Chain Scribe may hear WAGMI as a commitment to preserve a name beyond the current crisis. A Salvager may ask whether the promise includes the workers who rebuild after the celebrated defence. NICE & EASY BOIS mediators may seek a smaller agreement that allows rivals to share a route. An AllCity Bulls competitor may hear a future victory. None of these readings exhausts the phrase.

Rugpull Miners can exploit optimistic language, and powerful patrons can demand gratitude for sponsoring it. This does not make every hopeful gathering deceptive. It makes the relation between words and obligations worth examining. A speaker who offers everybody a future while leaving present costs with a few people has narrowed the promise through practice, even if the slogan remains unchanged.

Lantern Recess sometimes accepts supplies from visitors with faction ties. Pella records the gift and any stated condition. The lamp does not acquire a permanent owner because someone paid for one evening’s fuel.

The Prophecy Arc and the Bonnet traditions

W81’s register connects WAGMI with a wider Prophecy Arc, the Bonnet Prophecy and Convergence narratives. Other archive accounts stage dramatic reconciliations, universal transformations or completed peace. They are narrative traditions whose images matter; they are not all consecutive settled events in one chronology.

First Witness’s 2030 text does not name Alfie. Reading a later Child of Fire interpretation back into that early text would turn reception into prediction. Likewise, the WAGMI declaration does not prove that every later champion, Games outcome or unification was destined from the beginning.

At Lantern Recess, Ren sometimes reads an ending that imagines everyone arriving. Pella asks him to introduce it as a hoped-for ending. The distinction lets the room enjoy the story without requiring those still waiting for news to pretend that their waiting has already been answered.

Hope inside and outside Block Topia

The escaped Bitcoin Kids and the inside Bitcoin X Kids inhabit different circumstances. Outside, an appeal to make it together may mean finding shelter or preserving a route without surrendering local choice. Inside, it may mean maintaining a household, defending a useful vocation or finding room for a difficult question within an orderly institution.

Neither life is adequately described as pure freedom or pure passivity. The same hopeful phrase can support someone who stays as well as someone who leaves. This page does not make every inside child a secret Army recruit, nor does it give an outside storyteller authority to decide what counts as another person’s successful life.

The HODL Warriors’ collective defence and HODL X Warriors’ champion tradition also remain distinct. Shared optimism does not erase different obligations, histories or command arrangements. A chorus can bring people into the same room without making them one institution.

An unresolved future

The shared history places the Great Concord in 2030, the World Chain Triple Fork in 2880 and the survival of fewer than twelve secure regions by 2930. Queens became the basis of Block Topia. The current setting is 3008, with the Final Fork ahead and unresolved. Sacred or Aether-Chain, World Chain and True Bitcoin Fork are distinct; a prophecy is not a fourth system that silently merges them.

The WAGMI tradition does not supply an exact Kernel date, repair the Sacred Fork’s uncertain chronology or guarantee victory over NULL. Its force is ethical and imaginative: people can organise toward a future they cannot yet see. They can also revise a promise when they discover whom its first wording excluded.

The Long Tomorrow cloth remains unfinished. Ren still likes a chorus. Pella still keeps the room open on nights when nobody wants one. Their differences give the local promise something to do tomorrow.
