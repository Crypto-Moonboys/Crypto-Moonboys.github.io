Web3

✅ Verified📅 Last updated: October 2026📂 Technology

Web3 is a broad term for applications that use blockchains, cryptographic accounts or decentralised infrastructure to organise assets and participation. Its advocates emphasise user control and portability. Those are design goals to assess in each system, not properties automatically supplied by a token or wallet connection.

History and origins

Earlier web models

“Web 1.0” and “Web 2.0” are retrospective labels rather than precise technical eras. The early web included personal publishing and interactive services as well as static pages. Later platforms made uploading, social interaction and large-scale hosting easier, often concentrating discovery and account control.

A platform’s terms may grant it a broad licence to host and distribute work, but users do not universally surrender copyright in everything they post. Ownership, permission to use content, data processing and control of an account are different questions. Centralised services can restrict access without owning every user’s creations.

The blockchain-oriented Web3 vision

Gavin Wood helped popularise a blockchain-oriented meaning of Web3 in 2014. The term “Web 3.0” had earlier uses, so it should not be attributed as an entirely new phrase. Ethereum’s 2015 launch supplied a shared contract platform on which many subsequent applications developed.

Wallets, names and storage

IPFS, MetaMask and the Ethereum Name Service became prominent infrastructure projects during the mid-to-late 2010s. They address different problems: locating content by its identifier, authorising activity with accounts, and resolving human-readable names. A wallet, name and stored file do not together constitute an infallible identity system.

DeFi, NFTs and collective organisation

The 2020 DeFi expansion and the 2021 NFT market brought wider attention to blockchain applications. ConstitutionDAO’s attempt to buy a historical document in 2021 illustrated rapid collective fundraising as well as the difficulty of converting online governance into a successful real-world purchase.

The Ethereum Merge in 2022 changed its consensus energy and issuance model. It did not by itself solve application usability, fees or governance. “Metaverse” branding during the same period also referred to systems with many different levels of decentralisation.

Later experiments

Projects such as Lens and Farcaster explored portable or shared social infrastructure. Identity credentials, decentralised computation and Layer 2 networks developed alongside them. These are areas of experimentation; a product’s use of a blockchain does not prove mass adoption, private data ownership or independence from its operator.

Core concepts

Identity and credentials

A wallet can prove control of a key, but not automatically the holder’s real identity, humanity, reputation or entitlement. Credentials may be issued by other parties, presented by a holder and checked by a verifier. Self-sovereign identity seeks greater control over these relationships; it does not mean everyone can issue every authoritative credential for themselves.

Recovery, privacy and revocation matter as much as initial control. A publicly traceable account can expose activities that a person would prefer to keep separate. Names can expire or be transferred, and providers can control parts of the experience even when the key remains with the user.

Decentralised storage

IPFS identifies content through hashes and helps retrieve it from available peers. A content identifier supports integrity checks; it does not guarantee that someone will keep serving the file. Pinning, replication and continued operation are necessary parts of availability.

Filecoin adds a storage market with its own incentives and obligations. Arweave is designed for long-term data retention under a different economic model. Describing storage as decentralised or permanent does not remove dependence on software, operators and continuing network function.

ENS names

ENS provides name resolution for addresses and other records. It can make an account easier to find, but a recognisable name is not proof that its controller is trustworthy. Registration, renewal, resolver control and the exact destination still matter. A name is a useful identifier rather than a universal certificate of identity.

DAOs and governance

A decentralised autonomous organisation coordinates some decisions through contracts, voting or other shared procedures. Actual arrangements may include foundations, companies, committees, delegated voting and administrator keys. A DAO can therefore have identifiable leaders and legal responsibilities.

Public votes do not guarantee equal influence. Token concentration, participation thresholds and the power to execute or block a decision determine how governance works in practice. A treasury’s displayed assets are not a complete account of every obligation.

Applications and smart contracts

A dApp commonly combines contracts with websites, hosted interfaces, RPC services, oracles and storage. Contracts follow their programmed rules, which may include upgrades or pause controls. Networks and interfaces can suffer outages or impose restrictions. Decentralisation must be assessed component by component instead of assumed from the application’s name.

Assets and ownership claims

A token can provide a transferable identifier, specified protocol powers or evidence of a particular claim. Copyright, commercial licences, governance rights and legal ownership follow their own terms. A token holder does not automatically own the artwork, website or business associated with an asset.

Likewise, direct creator distribution can still involve marketplace fees, royalties, hosting costs and intermediaries. Reducing one platform’s control does not make a service costless or exempt it from law.

Assessing adoption and control

Wallet addresses, registered names, downloads and daily active people are different measures. One person can control several accounts, while a custodial address can represent many users. Funding totals and token prices do not establish successful use.

A useful assessment identifies what runs on-chain, who operates the remaining services, how users can leave or recover access, and what rights can be exercised without the original interface. Historical adoption snapshots should be dated and defined rather than displayed as timeless evidence.

Potential benefits and practical limits

Shared standards can make assets and application state easier to inspect and reuse. Cryptographic authorisation may reduce dependence on a single account provider, and open interfaces can let others build compatible tools. These are practical opportunities rather than a prediction that every centralised service will disappear.

Composability can also spread failures: many applications may depend on one bridge, oracle or issuer. Portability is valuable only when another functioning service can interpret the asset or record. A stored token without its required artwork, terms or compatible application may preserve less than its owner expects.

Risks and limitations

  • Key recovery, confusing signatures and malicious approvals can make onboarding and everyday use difficult.
  • Centralised websites, RPC endpoints, storage providers and administrator keys can remain important dependencies.
  • Public records may expose personal activity; pseudonymous does not mean anonymous.
  • Concentrated voting or ownership can undermine a community-governance claim.
  • Contract faults, scams and changing service terms can affect access and assets.
  • Legal responsibilities differ by jurisdiction and activity; decentralised branding does not decide them.

Web3 and the creator umbrella

Crypto Moonboys brings together Graffiti Kings culture, character identities, artwork and creator work. Blockchain can document selected assets and actions. The current project also supports creators keeping their existing identity without requiring a Moonboy, NFT, faction or Arcade participation. SWARMSY is the private creator environment supporting both routes.

A qualifying unique 1/1 character follows its published ownership and licence terms. Shared-canon approval remains a separate process. Older multi-chain manifestos provide historical context, not proof that every promised reward, application or ownership model is currently available.
