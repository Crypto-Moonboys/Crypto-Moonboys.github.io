Chain Archaeology and Historical Forensics

First WitnessHistorical method · Section 67Last updated: October 2026

By 3008, historians often work like forensic engineers. They do not merely read old records; they compare damaged systems that disagree about what existed.

Hash before story

Chain archaeologists begin with provenance where possible: hashes, signatures, block positions, storage media, replication history and known custody. Only then do they interpret narrative meaning.

Cross-chain triangulation

After the Triple Fork, the same event may exist in three incompatible versions. Shared details across hostile forks can become especially valuable because coordinated fabrication is less likely.

Wall evidence

Murals, photographs, physical wear, city plans and graffiti layers can contradict digital records. A wall may preserve an event the official chain removed, while a chain may preserve a date the wall mythologised.

Ghost identities

Some people exist in records without reliable evidence they ever lived physically. Others lived but vanished from surviving identity systems. Chain archaeologists distinguish record existence from biological existence.

The provenance confidence scale

Findings are often published with confidence levels rather than binary true/false labels. The practice influenced the First Witness Concordance.

The seductive artefact

The more perfectly an artefact confirms what scholars hoped to find, the more aggressively it is tested. Convenient evidence attracts fraud.
