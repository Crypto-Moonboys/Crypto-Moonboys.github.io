Life on the Waterfront

The Walking Boombox started out as a loan speaker used at outdoor sessions. A damaged carry handle gave the repair bench an excuse to fit legs. The crew expected it to wheel itself between bookings. Its first independent outing was a walk to the edge of the harbor to find out how far a song carried over water.

A soundsystem with legs and terms

The Boombox spent its first weeks on legs reporting to every booking at the time it had been plugged in on the old timetable. Most of the rooms were still being cleaned when it got there. It made friends with the cleaners by asking what they wanted to hear and made enemies with a promoter when it logged their work as part of the show. The habit of moving its own speaker made it harder for people to assume they also owned every recording inside it.

Blue brings it the small sounds nobody else takes home. Jamaican Bear brings it a lender’s conditions and then waits to see if it will put on the obvious first track. The Boombox keeps the empty workshop tape under the same clip as its record of who can lend it. It will describe that recording for an hour and turn the volume knob all the way down if somebody asks to hear it without permission.

The empty workshop tape

It keeps sets for loading hours, closing time and the gap between shifts. The most fought-over record in its crate is a field recording of an empty workshop. People recognise who is missing from the room before they can name the sound. The Boombox has turned down offers to play the tape in a paid set because the person who lent it set a different condition.

Off duty

Off duty it makes short compilations tailored to a particular walk. Jamaican Bear calls them walking sides. The Boombox always leaves ten seconds of silence at the end for shoes, gulls and whatever the street was already playing.

Why it kept a gap in the set

On the first trial evening Chada’s two-bell recording gave the Boombox a problem. It could have used the bells to get everyone’s attention and made the old accident sound fresh. Instead it asked for permission to leave a gap after Jamaican Bear’s record. In that gap the apprentice asked about the shift and the question got onto the air. The Boombox remembers the small noise she made as she pulled her timesheet out of a damp pocket.

Teddy has asked whether a regular ten-second break might fit future broadcasts. The Boombox will agree if the working person at the end of the queue gets a chance to speak first. It knows that silence can turn into part of the show if they sell it hard enough. Ask it which track the port needs and it often asks whether you would rather hear the tide for a moment.
