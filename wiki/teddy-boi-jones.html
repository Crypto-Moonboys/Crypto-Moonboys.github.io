Learning where to leave a breath

Teddy Boi Jones, thirty-six, grew up in the seats the projectionist at a little revue house could see through her booth window. His mother, a ticket seller, let him sit there so she could find him at close. His father carried instruments and occasionally played the last two numbers when a trumpeter failed to turn up. Teddy can still recognise the floorboard that squeaked beneath a singer’s first entrance. He treated it as a kind of percussion until he got a job sweeping before performances and discovered how hard other people worked to keep that sound out.

The Teddy came from a coat with a fur collar somebody left behind in a footwell. He put it on to make a storm announcement. A bad local reviewer used “Teddy Boi” in a column the next morning and he printed it on his first posters. He spent a while getting paid to introduce raffles and late buses. His first broadcast from the docks came from a storage room where a raincoat over the door held the sound in better than the wall did.

Why the Blockstars need a stagehand

The Blockstars can turn a local performance into something a person across three districts might remember for life. Their old Blockbusters origin cycle names Dondi and Kishimoto and tells of the move from street art to cultural power. Teddy inherited the craft of reaching an audience and the faction’s argument about who pays for all that reach. He never met the founding pair. He did sell the idea of a prestigious engagement to two backing singers who never got their fee. His own name had made it onto the contract. Theirs had made it onto the poster.

Now Teddy introduces the sound operator before the star when a programme allows it. On smaller stages he lets the crew take the first bow. He sets aside money from the first payment instead of promising everyone a share after the show. He also knows the pleasure of hearing a room repeat his line, and still bargains hardest when a sponsor wants his name at the top. He represents the Blockstars on the Waterfront because the local performers wanted someone who could negotiate room, rates and airtime. He remains liable to lose their support in the next argument about the rates.

The night the lights went out

Teddy was due to present the ill-fated rigging exhibition at the Ninth Berth. He had a spare speaker and a promotion fee to protect. When ZARAH8 closed the lower walk, he cut the show feed and read the practical directions Eddie Stax had painted on the wall. Then he broadcast the warning a second time with longer gaps so people at the far ramps could hear it between wind gusts. He almost introduced the second announcement with a joke. The person holding the lamp was shaking. He read the directions again.

In the following days a distributor offered a good fee for a clean, dramatic broadcast about the rescue. It wanted one hero, one villain and a clean wrap before the ad. Teddy recorded a version with the porters and two traders instead. One trader withdrew her voice after hearing where he had put the applause. He removed the clip and paid her travelling expenses. The distributor cut the rate. He still had the old trumpet in its case and put it up for sale to cover the sound operator, then bought it back at a loss when no other money arrived.

The part he plays at home

Teddy makes puppets from broken gloves and collects the advice people give stagehands while waiting for a microphone. He is excellent at the opening bars of dozens of songs and unreliable at most of the endings. He bakes small cakes the size of railway tokens and hates hearing a host say that tonight’s guests need no introduction. When nervous he runs a thumb across his cuff and makes the last jokes rush. Danny Rankz once got him to leave seven seconds of silence at the end of a difficult interview. Teddy still listens to the silence every time he plays the recording.

His most enduring collaboration is with Eddie. They would like a street catalogue with clear rights for everyone whose voice or name is included. They have gone six months without agreeing on the typeface. Chada Boi gives Teddy odd sounds for the interval and will only take credit if Teddy can remember where he heard them. Teddy buys him dinner some evenings anyway. He is capable of asking a thoughtful question to someone he loves. He is sometimes incapable of asking it without waiting for the answer to sound good.

The room he wants to fill

Teddy’s aim is a small theatre on the Waterfront with a paid rehearsal night and a second till for the backstage crew. He would make room for a show that could fail to sell out and return the following week. He keeps a floor plan in his instrument case, under the cloth. The plan reserves three warm seats for people who arrive after their shift. The last time he went to survey a possible room, he spent an hour imagining the curtain before he remembered to look at the toilets.

“Give me the person who moved the chair. Everybody’s already heard from the bloke who sat in it.” He says it like a punchline, but his best work begins when he gives the chair mover the whole minute.

The audience for an empty chair

Teddy learned the value of Second Credit doing an after-hours set in a back room. A vocalist lost her voice before her entrance. She asked Teddy to read the announcements and leave the first song out. He put her chair on the stage with a light on it and gave the audience a speech about absence. People loved it. She remained angry for a year because he had sold tickets to her illness without asking.

They tried again long after the original booking. She picked the players and requested the same chair. They paid somebody to mind the door, and she spent the first half in the audience making notes. At the interval Teddy asked whether he could tell the original story. She let him tell it as long as he finished by saying how much he had been paid. He read the figure out. It was the straightest laugh of his career.
