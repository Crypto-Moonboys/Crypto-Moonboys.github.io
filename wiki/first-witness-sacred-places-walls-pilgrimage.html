Sacred Places, Walls and Pilgrimage

First WitnessPlace and pilgrimage · Section 39Last updated: October 2026

A religion with no headquarters still developed places people travelled to. The difference is that no one location could claim to be the only legitimate centre.

The First Witness sites

The exact physical location of every 2030 Concord session is not locked in the current canon. Later traditions nevertheless identified archives, meeting rooms, data centres and public squares associated with the process.

The Source Register treats specific site claims cautiously because pilgrimage economies create strong incentives for places to become “the original room.”

Witness Walls

Some cities maintain one public wall where corrections, names, disputes and remembrance marks are legally protected for a fixed period before being painted over. A complete photographic archive preserves each layer.

The ritual captures Book IV's central tension: public memory must survive, but public space must also remain available to the living.

Chainfire ruins

Post-2880 ruins associated with contradictory identity systems became sites of mourning and education. Visitors compare surviving records from different forks and see how ordinary lives were split by incompatible ledgers.

Some factions commercialised the ruins aggressively. Others prohibit merchandise entirely.

The pilgrimage without destination

Open Block schools developed an unusual pilgrimage: travel to a place important to your own family or community, verify one story about it and correct one mistake in the record.

The destination is therefore different for every person. The pilgrimage centres method rather than geography.

The forbidden centre

Several attempts to build a universal First Witness capital failed politically or were opposed on theological grounds. The most quoted objection says, “Build a library, not a throne.”

Large libraries and archive temples exist. Their legitimacy depends on acknowledging that another archive can contradict them.

The street pilgrimage

GraffPUNK traditions map routes through layered murals, erased tags, memorial walls and surviving pre-Chainfire marks. Participants are expected to learn the local history rather than merely photograph the art.

A tourist who collects images without learning names is sometimes called a “wall miner.”
