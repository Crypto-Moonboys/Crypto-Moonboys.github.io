Sects, Schools and Schisms

First WitnessReligious history · Section 37Last updated: October 2026

The Forty are cultures and factions. Sects are arguments. A person can belong to one faction and still follow a completely different school of First Witness interpretation.

The Literal Witnesses

Literal Witnesses insist the nine Books should be interpreted as close to their 2030 language as possible. They distrust later prophecy, relic cults and faction commentary.

Their strength is textual discipline. Their weakness is pretending a thousand years of changed language can be escaped simply by saying “read it literally.”

A Literal Witness teaching room commonly puts the source, a working translation and its disputed words within the same view. Learners may be asked to explain a term without borrowing the teacher's preferred conclusion. The Second Gloss dispute is uncomfortable for this school: its discipline helped expose an administrative addition, but some respected literal readers had earlier defended that addition as the obvious meaning. Their reputation did not relieve later students of checking the comparison.

Teachers gain recognition through readers willing to attend, maintain the room and recommend their instruction. A neighbouring class can withdraw those recommendations without revoking access to the source. The practical sanction is loss of teaching work and support. It can remove a negligent teacher and can also silence someone whose accurate reading offends the patrons supplying the chairs.

The Open Block School

Open Block thinkers centre Book IX. They treat the Covenant as intentionally incomplete and argue that new moral problems require new commentary rather than forced answers from old sentences.

Critics accuse them of turning an unfinished scripture into permission to rewrite anything.

Open Block classes distinguish a proposed later rule from a claim about what the witnessed text already says. The distinction becomes physical in some rooms: new commentary goes on a movable board, while the source copy stays separately marked. A proposal must name the present problem, the people expected to perform the work and a way to recognise failure. An eloquent suggestion unsupported by a usable arrangement remains a suggestion.

Dara Ilex's challenge travels well here, although some Open Block teachers broaden it beyond the access office the hearing actually changed. Their opponents call that habit gloss inflation. The school's strength lies in asking a new question openly. Its weakness returns whenever a teacher labels a desired answer new necessity and treats disagreement as refusal to join the future.

The Keepers' Communion

This school sees stewardship as the centre of the entire tradition. Environmental care, archives, repair, succession, social duty and institutional maintenance all flow from custody rather than ownership.

Their failure mode is paternalism: the keeper begins deciding what others need without asking them.

Hollow Bell is taught as an institutional failure precisely because its stores were competently maintained. Rell Aven knew the equipment, seed cycles and repair needs; there was no simple replacement who could make all responsibilities compatible. The later Low Bell arrangements make household representation part of ordinary custody rather than a favour offered only when desperate neighbours reach the gate.

Communion apprentices learn budgets, repairs, records and the consequences of closing a service. Sponsorship by two working keepers can open a placement, but it does not compel a household to trust the apprentice. Dismissed workers may carry documented competence to another site. Refusing to surrender tools or records can make dismissal itself dangerous; some houses ask an outside witness to attend the handover rather than let the keeper evaluate their own departure.

The Ash School

Born after periods of catastrophic loss, the Ash School emphasises impermanence. Nothing survives unchanged; therefore attachment to institutions, leaders and even interpretations must remain light.

Critics say Ash teachers can become emotionally detached and too willing to let damaged things die rather than repair them.

Low Bell migrants included people who adopted Ash readings after losing homes they had helped preserve. They carried songs, seed samples and a few names instead of trying to move an entire institution. Their practice gave departure a language other than defeat. It also angered neighbours still attempting repair, who heard permission to leave in words they wanted to hear as a promise to remain.

Ash teachers disagree over that cost. Some require a final maintenance shift before leaving a shared house; others argue that exhausted people must be able to depart without earning freedom through more service. Neither rule fits every case. A student's recognised Ash teaching can survive the loss of a building, while a dependent person left inside that building can reasonably ask what the teaching preserved for them.

The Crownless

Crownless communities developed around anti-messianic readings of Book VIII. They reject permanent religious offices and are suspicious of anybody declared uniquely chosen.

Ironically, several famous Crownless teachers became personality cults despite preaching against personality cults. Their biographies became favourite examples in later satire.

The Blue Stair split supplies the most familiar local example. Eda Senn's followers preserved her voice so aggressively that the living Eda could no longer interrupt it. Leth Orr's smaller class rotated introductions and let a different recorder explain why each lesson had changed. That made the teaching less recognisable to visitors looking for a beloved performance; it did not make the class immune to new personal loyalties.

Crownless recognition usually rests on a room's willingness to host somebody for a stated series rather than grant a lifelong office. A teacher can be asked to finish the series without being invited back. Learners may copy the material and leave. The existence of that right does not supply a new room, food or work references, which is why the Blue Stair record counts the practical losses of the people who exercised it.

The Chain Devotion

At the opposite extreme, Chain Devotion traditions came close to treating authenticated continuity itself as sacred. They argued that preserving the record was humanity's highest religious duty.

The mainstream First Witness tradition considers this a serious distortion because Book V explicitly refuses to make the chain the object of worship.

Why schism never ended the religion

The First Witness survived schism because no school could revoke another school's access to the founding text. They could excommunicate, insult, ban or ignore one another. They could not make the Bitcoin witness disappear.

That technical fact became a theological one: disagreement could become severe without requiring one side to own the original.

Belonging between schools

A family can share a Keeper repair shift, attend a Crownless discussion and retain an older religious observance without those acts proving one exclusive identity. The Concord created a common Covenant rather than abolishing older faiths. Schools disagree about ceremonies, revelation and the dead; ordinary households can disagree internally without forming a new sect every time someone declines a prayer.

Migration changes those relationships. A Low Bell house may welcome an Ash teacher because the teacher can conduct a funeral without promising institutional permanence, then prefer a Literal Witness reader for a translation dispute. A child apprenticed to the store hears both. Later faction labels cannot neatly recover every earlier household's belief. Institutional history must follow rooms, work and departure as well as the names teachers give their positions.

Sources and the later commentary

The local history and scenes added here are newly authored canon expansion. Sources, limits and their shared sequence are recorded in the institutions and everyday culture evidence ledger.

These accounts develop later institutions and interpretations. They do not alter the nine witnessed Books, establish an exact date for the Sacred Fork, or turn one community's practice into law for all Forty paths.
