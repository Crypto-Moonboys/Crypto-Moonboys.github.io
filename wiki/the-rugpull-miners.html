⚔️ The RUGPULL MINERS

⚔️ Lore 📅 Last updated: October 2026 📂 Lore

The Rugpull Miners are Year 3008 profiteers who turn shared trust into private extraction. Their history is told through vanished ventures, unpaid work, rival factions and disputed restitution, with each betrayal leaving consequences long after its promoters have moved on.

Adult lore — 21+: The Red Ledger Night sections contain strong language, adult sexual relationships, sex work, drugs, coercion and lethal violence.

A faction built around betrayed trust

The Rugpull Miners are a predatory faction in the Year 3008 world. Their name describes the value they extract from other people's confidence, labour and expectations. The dedicated Digital Parasites of the Grid account places them among temporary ventures that leave contributors bearing the losses while their organisers depart with the gains. Their territory is therefore as much social as geographical: the broken promise, the missing payment and the agreement nobody can now explain.

They are one of the evidenced cultures within the forty-faction civilisation. That recognition is descriptive, not absolution. The First Witness commentary says they quote Fork, freedom and voluntary exchange while violating Witness and Keeper through deception. They turn the right to leave a system into a claim that leaving cancels obligations to everyone still inside it.

Independent is the least misleading label for their allegiance, but it does not imply equality among them. A successful promoter, a frightened clerk and a porter awaiting wages may inhabit the same venture under very different conditions. Understanding those differences helps explain how the faction persists. It does not make its victims responsible for the choices made above them.

Rafe and the red case that keeps coming back

Rafe's red case remains a local Miner emblem after the porter-door killing. Some adult imitators carry empty copies because looking like a person with money can attract the next person's money. Others paint the case crossed out beside a lost payment. Rafe treats both as proof that his name matters. An injury and a discredited story have changed his body and invitations without making him stop selling.

The Miners' distinctive talent is temporary belonging: a venture that feels like a crew, a crew that feels like an ascent, an ascent that leaves the organiser with the gain. They are often excellent company. They remember a stranger's aspiration, make a tired worker feel clever and know how to turn an awkward supper into an evening somebody wants to buy again. Those skills are part of the extraction, not decorative evil pasted over a transaction.

At a later adult gathering, Rafe admits a minor failed payment before anyone asks. The admission makes the larger proposal seem candid. An attendee who knows about the killing walks out; another stays because his previous venture paid her promptly. A worker accepts promotional work with an advance and tries to keep her own exposure small. The account establishes the recruitment and advance, not the outcome of a new universal collapse. Rafe is dangerous because the first benefit can be real, the company pleasurable and the warning insufficient to make everyone leave.

Origins, curses and competing accounts

The main archive account traces the Miners to pre-Datapocalypse grifters whose minds supposedly survived the Genesis Kernel's Self-Fork with their appetite for exploitation intact. This is an origin claim within a broader disputed rescue mythology. It does not settle the Kernel's nature, prove an inherited criminal essence or mean that every later member was once a particular pre-collapse fraudster.

The catastrophe used by the current chronology is the World Chain's Triple Fork in 2880. The ensuing Great Unravelling disrupted institutions that had made promises enforceable. By 2930 fewer than a dozen secure regions remained, including the Queens foundation of Block Topia. The Miners' 3008 opportunities arise in that fractured landscape; their story cannot be compressed into one corruption hidden inside the city's walls.

The older wiki instead tells of cursed code struck during a Great Hash Fracture. It names the False Bull Run, Cycle 13 Collapse and a forbidden Trust Fracture Code. Those tales remain part of the faction's feared reputation, with uncertain dates and scope. A curse is not established as the cause of betrayal, and no supposed code is accepted as able to defeat every form of verification.

Freedom used as an excuse

Miner rhetoric celebrates the person who notices that a grand institution is less solid than it looks. That suspicion can expose real hypocrisy: an elite sponsor may demand honesty while hiding its own losses, and a ruler may call an extraction a public service. The Miners' corruption of this insight is the conclusion that another person's dishonesty licenses their own.

Their favourite argument treats every exchange as if both sides possessed the same knowledge and power. A worker who accepts a promise under pressure is said to have chosen the outcome freely. A family that trusted a familiar intermediary is mocked for wanting certainty. The missing facts disappear behind a sermon about personal responsibility, while responsibility for the misrepresentation is carefully left unnamed.

The Covenant's Witness and Keeper readings cut through that account. Freedom to refuse an agreement does not make a false description truthful. Custody does not become ownership because recovering it is difficult. These disputes belong to the fictional society's moral language; the faction's existence is not an endorsement of deception or a claim about how present-day financial systems ought to operate.

The Winter Counter

The Winter Counter is a cramped meeting room used by one Miner company between ventures. Its benches, heating fittings and patched table are ordinary, and much of the work done there looks mundane: arguing over unpaid bills, mending clothes, checking who has returned and finding someone to collect supplies. The room's existence undercuts the fantasy that a faction can live forever by vanishing.

Nella Tarn once kept settlement notes for this company. She began by believing she was recording risky but legitimate work. Over time, explanations for missing payments became harder to reconcile with what workers had actually delivered. Nella's hesitation was not a sudden discovery that dishonesty existed. It was the recognition that her careful handwriting was being used to make another person's refusal look like a completed agreement.

Rafe Quill, a senior promoter in the same company, maintained that the records were merely optimistic. He praised Nella's discipline while resisting her requests to correct them. Their conflict shows a local hierarchy based on who can define the story after something fails. Neither person commands the whole faction, and the Winter Counter is not a secret capital or the location of every stolen asset.

The labour underneath the spectacle

Miner ventures require work beyond their public promises. Porters carry equipment, cooks feed gatherings, repairers keep rooms usable and clerks answer people who return asking for what they were owed. Some workers know the venture's purpose; others learn too late. A faction label alone does not explain which decision a person made or what evidence they could see.

Companies are often short of the very resources their leaders claim to possess in abundance. Displays of success can coexist with delayed wages and borrowed tools. Rafe argued that visible confidence would bring the company through a difficult period. The people repairing his display were still expected to wait for payment. Their work made his confidence look more substantial than their own circumstances allowed.

Everyday dependence creates pressure that the rhetoric of pure freedom obscures. A worker who leaves may lose a bed, a meal or the only person prepared to vouch for them. A person who stays can become involved in harms they first meant only to survive. The faction exploits that ambiguity, but an account of its labour must not turn every dependent into either an innocent or a mastermind.

The Empty Dividend

The Empty Dividend is the name survivors gave to a failed local venture associated with the Winter Counter company. Workers had supplied a venue and its fittings on the understanding that the completed venture would cover their agreed payments. When the venture ended, the money available did not match what they had been told. The room was full of objects that testified to work, and almost empty of anyone willing to accept responsibility for it.

A porter named Bex Orla lost the payment intended to repair a household heater. The immediate consequence was not a dramatic fall from wealth. It was a longer walk to a shared warm room, less time available for paid work, and an argument with a neighbour who had advanced supplies. Others lost more valuable goods, but Bex refused the suggestion that a smaller sum made the harm negligible.

Nella preserved notes showing that the obligations had remained open. Rafe claimed the venture's failure had released everyone from its original expectations. The dispute did not reveal one miraculous hidden reserve. A few usable items were recovered and sold, leaving substantial losses. The Empty Dividend became an account of incomplete restitution, not a comforting tale in which exposure restored everything.

The Bent Receipt hearing

The Bent Receipt hearing followed the Empty Dividend. Its name came from a folded work receipt Bex had carried long enough for the crease to obscure part of the writing. The hearing brought surviving notes together with workers' testimony. What looked like a small documentary defect mattered because each side wanted the missing part to support its preferred story.

Nella could explain how the company's entries were made, but her involvement also made her evidence contestable. She had benefited from the same room and supplies as the promoters, and some workers remembered her repeating assurances she no longer defended. She acknowledged those statements rather than claiming that later honesty erased them. The hearing accepted parts of her account while leaving other claims unresolved.

Bex received a partial payment and a written recognition of the remainder. Neither guaranteed collection. The outcome reduced one uncertainty without repairing the household's lost time or the neighbour's confidence. The case remains significant because it treated the receipt, testimony and responsibility as separate questions. A damaged record did not automatically make the worker a liar, and a legible record did not automatically make the company truthful.

The AllCity Bulls and the price of humiliation

The dedicated Miner and AllCity Bulls accounts agree that competitive pride makes the Bulls recurrent targets. A loss threatens not only their holdings but their belief that they understand the contest better than everyone else. Some Bulls therefore prefer a story about an unbeatable trick to the admission that their own certainty went unexamined.

This gives the False Bull Run legend lasting power even though its precise scale is unresolved. It condenses a wider fear: the public scoreboard might be measuring confidence in a promise rather than a completed result. The legend is not a verified account of every speculative collapse, and the Miners do not receive credit for every market failure merely because their name offers a familiar explanation.

Bulls seeking redress can be useful allies to smaller victims, bringing attention and resources. They can also dominate a hearing so thoroughly that a porter's missing wages disappear beneath a champion's lost prestige. Bex's supporters use the Empty Dividend story to challenge that hierarchy. The size of an audience should not decide whose obligation remains worth remembering.

The Furies, the Guild and the danger of retaliation

The Blockchain Furies are the Miners' principal enemies in the archive. They pursue evidence of deception and favour forceful public reckoning. Their account claims formidable forensic and Reversal Protocol abilities; the Finance Guild account describes funding their efforts because false ledgers threaten its own authority. Those claims establish a conflict, not universal detection or guaranteed recovery.

The factions want different outcomes. A victim may want a missing tool returned, the Guild may want a debt recognised, and a Fury may want an offender made into a warning. Destroying an asset can frustrate repayment. Exposing a company without distinguishing its workers can create a second set of victims. None of those risks makes investigation unnecessary; they explain why people harmed by Miners sometimes distrust the people offering vengeance.

Nella's decision to give evidence brought no automatic protection from suspicion. Some former associates called her a traitor, while some opponents assumed her testimony was another performance. She could not answer either accusation by demanding immediate trust. Her later work became slower and more closely questioned. Accountability offered a possible route out, not a clean new identity without obligations.

Trade rivals and uncomfortable customers

The Bally Boys consider the Miners destructive competitors because a market damaged by betrayal becomes harder for any broker to use. The Salvagers despise promises that consume resources without producing power, coolant or usable materials. These positions can lead to cooperation against a particular Miner company, but shared opposition does not turn every broker or supplier into a disinterested defender of the public.

The dedicated Nomad account says quiet wanderers are hard to track, while the Miner account names their desire for untouched caches as a vulnerability. Both can be true in limited cases. Independence does not confer perfect judgement. A Bear may avoid public speculation and still believe a story about a lost place that speaks directly to a cherished private hope.

Relations with Alfie's escaped Bitcoin Kids are similarly inconsistent. One archive text depicts them as targets; another describes them trading information with Miners for necessities; a later summary says their volatility makes them unattractive prey. The page preserves those differences. Need can produce a transaction without an alliance, and an unreliable customer can still become a victim. The engineered Bitcoin X Kids remain the distinct population inside Block Topia.

Digital Disguise and borrowed credibility

Digital Disguise is the dedicated account’s name for the Miners’ imitation of another faction’s appearance and credibility. The resulting harm extends beyond a missing payment: a familiar insignia or endorsement becomes harder to trust even after the impersonator has departed. The account’s claim that such disguises are almost impossible to trace remains faction mythology, not a demonstrated universal ability.

The same source describes Miners approaching the GraffPUNKS’ Punk Net as apparent anti-establishment defectors and supplying compromised material represented as Spraycode. This is a distinct relationship from the false sponsorship surrounding the Games. It explains why an outside rebel network can distrust an offer made in its own political language. The allegation belongs to w47; it does not make all defectors suspect or establish that every supplied fragment is hostile.

Games traffic and false sponsorship

The Hard Fork Games and Feast Week concentrate ambition, travel and spending around Block Topia. The dedicated Miner account places false sponsors and broken post-Games promises in that environment. Those are particular archive allegations. They do not prove that every sponsor is fraudulent, that every champion shares the same fate or that the city grants the Miners unrestricted access.

Other sources emphasise strong protection around inside incomes and describe the Squeaky Pinks as constrained by rigid enforcement rules. The tension is institutional: a system can be strict about entry while responding poorly to a promise that was misrepresented elsewhere. Neither the city's confidence nor an opponent's accusation provides a universal map of what its defences can prevent.

Returning candidates can carry losses they are ashamed to explain. Their households may already be waiting for gear or payment the candidate expected to bring home. A false promise therefore outlives the public event. The Games' departure and return histories are essential context for this damage; the bright week itself is only the most visible portion.

NULL, emergency defence and the Final Pull

The dedicated account says the Miners exploit fear of NULL with supposed defences and promised refuge. It also recognises the contradiction: an antichain that erases meaning and records threatens the very gains they hope to keep. The faction can profit from fear only while enough shared reality remains for anything to be valued or remembered.

Its imagined Final Pull is a sovereign refuge for perpetual exploitation. That is an ambition in the account, not an accomplished chain or a guaranteed escape from the unresolved Final Fork. The old claim of untouchable hidden vaults is similarly a reputation, not a complete inventory of reserves. Stories of unlimited stolen wealth can distract victims from the smaller, traceable obligations still before them.

Other archive traditions place the Miners inside collective HODL mobilisation, temporarily sharing danger with enemies. This does not contradict opportunism so much as expose its limits: even an extractor needs a world left to extract from. Emergency service does not settle old losses. HODL Warriors and HODL X Warriors remain distinct, and neither title grants a universal pardon to anyone appearing in a battle account.

Leaving the company

After the Bent Receipt hearing, Nella took ordinary copy work under terms that required another person to check her entries. The pay was modest and the questions could be humiliating. She objected when an employer treated every uncertainty as proof of deliberate deceit, but accepted that her old assurances had helped sustain the company. Leaving was a continuing argument about responsibility rather than a single moment of redemption.

Rafe continued to describe the Empty Dividend as a temporary setback exaggerated by enemies. Some listeners believed him because they needed their earlier confidence to have meant something. Others wanted a new opportunity to replace an old loss. His continued audience is not proof of irresistible charisma; it shows how hard it can be to accept an ending without compensation.

Bex kept the bent receipt after the hearing. Its value was no longer the expectation that one piece of paper would recover everything. It connected the unpaid balance to work that had actually been done. Against a faction that turns every ending into permission to forget, an unfinished obligation can itself become a form of resistance.

Rafe’s red case and the debt he calls old news

Rafe Quill, 49 in the Red Ledger Night account, is still associated with the Winter Counter company. Exposure of the Empty Dividend has cost him willing workers, not his ability to speak well in a room with money. He attends the supper as a guest of a private backer, wearing a repaired coat so skilfully altered that it looks like an expensive decision. Nella Tarn, 35, knows the lining and remembers which seam she helped mend.

The returned sound case reaches Rafe through a mistaken handover. He hears enough of Ione and Perr's private argument to recognise leverage. Instead of returning it, he approaches Third Ear with a story about hidden accounts. The office's narrower interpretation frustrates him until he learns what Daro will pay for the private matter alone. He takes an advance for the case. He has not broken a city protocol or earned the truth he is selling.

Rafe gives Nella a small payment against an old obligation after the sale. She asks where it came from. He tells her a new buyer recognised value. When she learns about the private voices, she puts the amount aside rather than pretending that money never passed through her hand. She still needs to eat. Refusing to spend it leaves another debt open; spending it would not make the original loss disappear. Rafe understands this pressure and calls her hesitation a luxury.

At the supper he tells Bex Orla, 39, that the Empty Dividend is old news. Bex has spent months living with the bill for the missing heater money. 'Old to you,' Bex says. Rafe looks toward a patron before answering. 'I can't run a company inside everybody's feelings.' The company has never stopped using those feelings when it needs faith, patience or one more unpaid shift. This time the line finds a listener with nothing left to postpone.

The porter-door killing

The porter-door confrontation occurs at a later small hire in Borrowed Dawn, after the packet sale has become known. Bex comes to demand the rest of the recognised Empty Dividend payment. Nella is present to challenge Rafe's settlement version. Neither has agreed an armed raid, and Bex has not been sent by the Ash Desk. Their grievance is real. What Bex does next is also his own act.

Rafe calls Bex a paid-up nuisance. Bex throws a drink, then strikes at him with the bottle while Rafe tries to get behind the table. Jex Sable, the 41-year-old hired porter, intervenes to stop the attack. Bex swings again and hits Jex at the side of the head. The room loses its clever voices all at once. Jex collapses. Workers call for help and remain with him; he dies before the attending medic can restore him. The account gives no resurrection through a remembered voice, avatar or copied record.

Rafe survives with a cut face and later a visible scar. He says Bex arrived intending murder; the surviving room accounts do not establish that intention. They do establish Bex's violent blows and Jex's death. Bex says he wanted Rafe to feel what had been done to him. That desire does not make Jex the right person to suffer, or his death an accidental footnote to a righteous attack.

Nella tries to get between Bex and another door worker as people close around them. She does not remove the body, conceal the bottle or rewrite whom Bex struck. Outside, she says, 'He was owed. He killed somebody. Both, you fucking cowards.' Several listeners want one of those sentences omitted. She loses a Winter Counter lodging arrangement after refusing Rafe's demand for a statement that clears the company's earlier accounts. The lodging loss hurts; it is not evidence that she has been forgiven for her own past assurances.

After the death: a wounded promoter still selling

Rafe makes his scar part of the next introduction. He does not openly say that the Empty Dividend has been settled. He lets buyers infer that the violent claimant was never credible. The tactic wins two new conversations and loses one client who asks to see the original obligations. He continues with a smaller company, less trustworthy labour and enough patronage to avoid Bex's shortages. The killing has damaged him and improved one of his sales stories.

Bex is held pending a locally recognised hearing into Jex's death and the room violence. This account does not decide a sentence or import Tor Pell's separate Dry Step result. Bex remembers the unfinished heater job between accounts of the blow. His old neighbour refuses a visit. Nella brings a limited bundle of clothing and tells him she will describe what she saw, including what he did. He calls her disloyal before asking her not to leave yet.

Jex's partner, Dala Sable, 43, rejects a proposed public reconciliation supper. She wants his earned wages, the names of those handling the case and his coat back. The coat has been cleaned by a worker who thought that would help. Dala holds it for a long time and says she wanted it as it was. Nobody finds a good answer in the room. Her refusal is not turned into a new faction oath or a demand that all mourning remain permanent rage.

The Winter Counter has a spare place where Jex never sat. Some Miners speak of him as though he was part of their company because a shared dead man makes a more useful defence than a hired worker they scarcely knew. Nella corrects the claim when she hears it. Rafe stops inviting her to gatherings. The company carries on; so does the unpaid remainder from the venture that began the grievance. Exposure, revenge and restitution remain three different unfinished things.

Sources for the Red Ledger Night account
