⚔️ The TUSKON OGS

⚔️ Lore 📅 Last updated: October 2026 📂 Lore

The TUSKON OGS are veteran loyalists whose reputation rests on staying after others move on. In the year 3008, they hold ground for the Aztec Raiders, guard supplies, repair worn equipment and carry the burdens that conquest leaves behind. Their old street codes promise that people will not be abandoned. Their history also shows how easily that promise can be used to demand obedience from someone who has already given too much.

Adult lore — 21+: These fictional accounts include strong language, adult relationships, intoxication, coercion, violence and lasting loss.

Faction and allegiance

The TUSKON OGS belong to the setting’s forty-faction landscape. Their most substantial archive account calls them the Digital Loyalists: the force that secures what the Aztec Raiders conquer. The association is more consequential than a shared enemy. Raiders require ground held, supplies protected and defeated populations kept under control after the celebrated advance has passed. Tuskon service makes that occupation endure.

The older street-lord image survives in their treatment of seniority, local memory and promises made face to face. It cannot support an unqualified claim that they answer to nobody. A veteran can possess considerable authority over a yard while serving a larger campaign whose purposes that yard did not choose. This tension shapes their pride and their failures.

Old Iron and the seat nobody borrows

Brak Sennel, 62, is a Tuskon veteran called Old Iron around several outside watches. He sits in a broad chair repaired so many times that its original frame is difficult to find. A plain club stands beside it; a polished new weapon would, he says, make him look desperate. Younger adults repeat the line and copy his heavy, unadorned coat. His appeal is permanence: he has stood through changes other people describe from second-hand accounts. His threat is the conviction that standing longest entitles him to decide who stands next.

He can organise a watch, recognise a failing supply promise and make frightened soldiers feel less alone. He can also turn a meal into a test of obedience. An adult recruit called Iska Ren, 27, leaves a local watch after its work becomes support for a Raider occupation she opposes. Brak keeps her previous help from the next toast and calls her departure softness. Her brother remains in the watch, proud of a service she now hates. The family continues borrowing the same cart and refusing to discuss who last used it.

Toma's repair-minded authority remains different from Brak's social discipline. Some veterans recognise the distinction; others protect Old Iron because challenging him would expose compromises in their own histories. Tuskon power is the power to make an acquisition endure after the conqueror has moved on. Their idols are people who have never left. Their ugliest choice is making somebody else's right to leave sound like betrayal of the dead.

Known facts and disputed honours

Their characteristic equipment is the Data-Club, a blunt fictional weapon associated with attrition and holding space. Plain forms and terse reports distinguish them from the Raiders’ more elaborate displays of rank. They require repair materials, rest, replacement equipment and dependable deliveries. Loyalty supplies none of these by itself.

The faction’s enemies include the Squeaky Pinks and Crypto Moongirls defending Block Topia, and outside communities threatened by Raider expansion. The Salvagers’ account also describes Tuskon protection work, showing that their reputation circulates through practical contracts as well as campaigns. Neither a hired escort nor an honoured promise makes every Tuskon unit independent of the Raiders. Claims of invulnerability, incorruptibility or universal obedience belong to faction rhetoric and require the same scrutiny as their opponents’ boasts.

What an old name carries

The archive imagines Tuskon origins in the devotion of bodyguards, followers and trusted seconds who survived catastrophe by holding to a person or purpose. The First Witness commentaries give their lineage another emphasis: memory and authority earned by enduring work, with the danger that age becomes an answer to every criticism. These strands meet around a deceptively simple question. When someone says they have always stood beside their people, which people are allowed to speak?

A younger member may inherit a name, a place at the table and an obligation before learning what earlier holders did to acquire them. An elder remembers the winter when the same obligations prevented abandonment. Both experiences can be true. Tuskon custom grows harsh when one is permitted to erase the other, especially when the person asking for an explanation lacks a prestigious history.

Long Step Yard

Long Step Yard is a Tuskon gathering and repair place in contested territory beyond Block Topia’s protected interior. Its name comes from a worn raised threshold: a visitor carrying a heavy load must slow down and take one deliberate step. The threshold is not a defensive marvel. It is an inconvenience that generations of workers have learned to warn strangers about.

Toma Flintlock oversees the yard’s stores and watches. Senn Bracken, a younger keeper of its repair records, counts the work that victory speeches usually omit. They share a table scarred by years of fittings being tested against it. Toma dislikes lists that substitute for knowing the people on them; Senn dislikes being told that a respected face makes a missing entry unnecessary. Their arguments are familiar enough that other workers sometimes continue eating through them.

The yard’s daily authority is tangible. Someone decides which damaged carrier gets repaired first, whether an exhausted guard returns to duty and who must explain an incomplete delivery. Those choices distribute hardship. A solemn account of loyalty becomes useful only when it can answer for them.

The labour of holding ground

A campaign leaves the Tuskon with dull necessities and ugly responsibilities. Stores need shelter. Routes that were passable yesterday may no longer carry a loaded cart. Damaged gates need hinges before they need insignia. The guarded population still requires water, food, heat and places to mourn, even when the occupying command records these needs chiefly as threats to stability.

At Long Step Yard, Senn separates repairs to household equipment from repairs requested for the next advance. The categories do not create neutrality; both take place under an occupation. They do make it harder to describe every hour of labour as support for a single glorious purpose. A replacement wheel for a resident’s cart is remembered differently from a replacement fitting for a patrol.

Toma regards this distinction as necessary bookkeeping and occasionally as an irritation. Commanders want readiness expressed in one confident number. Senn brings the broken parts to the meeting. Readiness, she says, has an owner waiting for each of them.

The Borrowed Banner

The Borrowed Banner incident began when a returning group hung a damaged Raider banner over the yard’s repair table. They intended to honour the workers whose labour had kept their equipment usable. For two days the gesture pleased almost everyone. Then a resident who had lost access to a workshop under the occupation came to collect a repaired carrier and refused to stand beneath it.

Toma first treated the refusal as an insult to the workers. Senn asked whether an honour could still be called a gift if every visitor had to accept it. The disagreement divided the yard along lines that rank alone did not predict. Some of the oldest guards wanted the banner displayed outside; some younger members feared that moving it would be read as disloyalty.

They eventually returned the banner to the returning group and kept a small record of the workers’ names on the table. The resident received the carrier. Nobody recovered the lost workshop through this settlement. The story matters locally because the yard accepted that gratitude to its labourers did not entitle it to dictate the meaning of the occupation to someone who had suffered from it.

The Late Watch

The Late Watch is a different kind of yard story, remembered with less satisfaction. A delivery arrived after its expected window, and an exhausted guard was ordered to remain until its load had been checked. The order seemed minor beside a campaign. By morning the guard had made mistakes, the check had to be repeated and the next shift began with an argument about who had failed whom.

Toma initially defended the order as ordinary service. Senn laid out the successive extensions that had made the supposedly short duty last through the night. No single extension looked exceptional. Together they revealed how a person who always agreed could be used beyond their capacity while everyone involved still praised their reliability.

The yard changed the way it recorded extended watches and gave another member responsibility for checking the burden. The change did not become a law for every Tuskon force. Toma continued to believe that difficult service was sometimes unavoidable. He also began asking who would take over before calling a task nearly finished.

Promises, seniority and the table

A promise at Long Step Yard is normally repeated in the presence of someone expected to remember it. The custom gives speech weight without making memory perfect. Arguments arise when one party recalls a fixed undertaking and the other recalls an undertaking conditional on supplies or safety. Senn’s records preserve those qualifications, although some elders still regard writing them down as an accusation.

The table has places associated with long service, but a newcomer carrying a report is expected to be heard before the meal resumes. In practice, the quality of that hearing varies. A veteran can interrupt without seeming to interrupt, merely by telling an older story that absorbs all the available time. Younger members have learned to bring short accounts; they have also learned how much a short account can leave out.

The strongest version of Tuskon memory makes room for those omissions. The weakest version demands gratitude for being allowed to speak at all. Both are present in the yard.

Supplies and the price of dependence

The Bally Boys court the Tuskon because conquest consumes supplies and promises future spoils. The dedicated account calls the loyalists difficult to bribe. That does not make them free of economic pressure. A repair cannot be completed with an assertion of honour, and a supplier who advances scarce materials expects something to return.

Toma has a habit of placing a damaged fitting beside a proposed supply agreement. It reminds everyone what the agreement is supposed to solve. Senn asks what must be surrendered if the expected next campaign does not happen. Neither habit removes the unequal power between creditor, commander, worker and conquered resident, but both make a distant promise harder to confuse with a present resource.

Salvagers sometimes work under Tuskon protection while distrusting the military purpose behind the job. Their practical cooperation is fragile. A carrier can be delivered safely and still end at a place its maker would not have chosen. Completing a contract does not settle that moral argument.

Neighbours who do not call them guardians

The Tuskon account celebrates protection of captured assets from Blockchain Furies audits and Rugpull Miners’ predation. These are different threats. A fraudster seeking someone else’s supplies is not equivalent to an investigator asking who was dispossessed. Tuskon commanders can benefit from treating both as interference. The difference matters most to residents whose testimony an audit might preserve.

Against the Squeaky Pinks, Tuskon loyalty meets an institution claiming that obedience belongs to Block Topia’s law. Against the Crypto Moongirls, it meets an organised defence of territory the Raiders covet. None of these forces becomes harmless because its opponents are coercive. People caught between them can fear the arrival of every uniform.

The Nomad Bears and other travellers judge a held road through access, delay and treatment, rather than the age of the emblem above it. Being known as a guardian at home does not mean a stranger experiences the same gate as shelter.

The younger generation and the Games

The archive says Tuskon forces raid the escaped Bitcoin Kids’ networks and regard their improvisation with contempt. This records a hostile military perspective. It does not establish that the outside generation lacks discipline, work or durable loyalties. The inside Bitcoin X Kids are a distinct generation living within Block Topia, with households, duties and relationships of their own; an invading faction’s indifference cannot define those lives.

The Hard Fork Games offer the Raiders opportunities to recruit and observe. Tuskon service gives those offers their less glamorous future: a place in a watch, an expectation of endurance, and orders issued by someone else. A champion seeking purpose may see belonging in that future. A veteran may see another exhausted newcomer requiring boots, instruction and time.

At Long Step Yard, Senn asks new arrivals what work they thought they were accepting. Their answers are kept with the record of their first assignment. The discrepancy is often more revealing than the recruitment speech.

Holding ground under the Echo Fork

The dedicated Tuskon account claims that simple purpose resists the contradictions of the Echo Fork. It describes loyalists holding Raider positions marked by the Eye Without Hash and remaining useful where more elaborate reasoning fails. That is an attributed theory of resistance, not proof that obedience grants immunity to NULL, the Antichain or every form of corruption.

The Raiders’ wish to conquer the source of the Antichain makes this claim especially dangerous. A commander who believes their followers cannot be affected has less reason to heed reports of fear, confusion or loss. The resulting silence may look like confidence from a distance. At the yard, Senn insists that an unfamiliar condition be recorded in the speaker’s own words before anyone explains it away as poor discipline.

A held position is also a limited achievement. It can remain occupied while becoming uninhabitable, unusable or incomprehensible. The command to stay does not answer how the people staying will live.

Collective defence and a returning obligation

W81’s mobilisation account pictures the Tuskon becoming zealots for the Chain when the HODL Warriors assemble. In the wider continuity, their participation belongs to a collective defence state that crosses faction boundaries; it does not create an additional faction or demonstrate that previous allegiances and harms have vanished. The HODL Warriors remain distinct from the city-linked HODL X Warriors.

A common danger can bring people who have harmed one another into the same line. What happens after it passes is harder. The defeated resident still remembers the missing workshop; the worker still wants a promised rest; a commander still wants to recover the ground temporarily set aside. Long Step Yard’s stories keep these smaller obligations visible beside the language of universal survival. A defence of the Chain has not yet answered every question asked at the repair table.

History, legends and open dates

The shared chronology fixes the Great Concord or Covenant in 2030, the World Chain’s Triple Fork in 2880, fewer than twelve secure regions by 2930 and Queens as the basis of Block Topia. These provide the setting’s scale; they do not date every Tuskon lineage. In 3008, the outside world remains larger than the city and its territorial claims.

The older page preserves a First Data Schism vault said to hold original street codes and a Neon Riot of Cycle 32 in which the Tuskon repel corporate invaders. These are legacy traditions, including the claim that the vault could rewrite boundaries. Their cycle labels are not securely mapped to the calendar, and their heroic victories do not establish that Tuskon domains are untouchable.

Sacred or Aether-Chain, World Chain and True Bitcoin Fork remain distinct. The Kernel’s exact dating, the Sacred Fork’s chronology and the outcome of the Final Fork remain open. No loyalty oath on this page settles them.

Real-world basis

The TUSKON OGS are a fictional faction exploring service, veteran authority, occupation and the difficulty of refusing an inherited obligation. Their name does not establish a factual connection to a real city, ethnic community, military organisation or person. Data-Clubs and consciousness-origin stories belong to the invented world. Long Step Yard, its workers and its local incidents are newly authored lore, not recovered testimony about real lives.

The Black Salt table

Long Step Yard's Black Salt supper began after a badly supplied watch in the earlier 3008 season. The salt was ordinary seasoning darkened by its container, not a sacred substance. The exhausted adults at the first table ate whatever could be made hot. Brak Sennel remembered that they had stayed together and made the supper into a test of belonging. Later guests received better food and were told that the first meal had tasted better because loyalty was hungrier then.

An adult cook named Dovra Pan, 54, finally told him the original meal had been fucking dreadful. The veterans roared with laughter. Brak laughed too, then objected when an outsider quoted her sentence. Inside the yard, mocking a miserable night could prove that somebody had earned the right to remember it. Outside, the same words supposedly insulted the people who endured it. Dovra refused to pretend the ingredients changed when the audience did.

The table acquires one empty place for a watch member who has died and another, less comfortably explained place when somebody leaves. Brak calls the latter temporary. Iska Ren's departure shows how that description can become a demand disguised as welcome: her place is kept while her decision to leave is treated as an illness the yard expects her to recover from. Dovra uses the spare setting for a working guest. Brak moves it back when he next notices.

Love that expects you to stay

Tuskon affection is material and intrusive. A repaired coat may arrive without being requested; advice about a partner arrives with it. A person who brings dinner to a tired watch can find that the yard starts discussing their relationship as common property. Veterans enjoy the warmth of being known and the authority of having known one another too long. New adults can experience the same attention as a room in which their departure has already been voted down.

Brak dislikes lovers who ask a watch member to choose a private life over the yard. Dovra dislikes the assumption that every private invitation is a recruitment attempt by somebody else's faction. Their arguments are loud enough for new guests to understand the cost of taking a side. The Black Salt table offers belonging that outsiders cannot simply purchase. It also teaches that a gift can become a debt whose creditor insists he has never kept accounts.

The Tuskon appetite differs from Raider expansion. The Raiders want the next door; the Tuskon want the people behind the door to keep remembering who stayed. Both desires can sustain a community and prolong an occupation. Old Iron's most seductive promise is permanence. His most frightening habit is hearing 'I am leaving' as the opening of a negotiation he intends to outlast.
