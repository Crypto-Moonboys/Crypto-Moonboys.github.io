The pieces under the paint

Eddie Stax is a thirty-eight-year-old GraffPUNK whose tag refers to the layers he used to find on rental shutters. His aunt ran a sign shop above a tea stall where other painters left damaged boards for repair. Eddie inherited the habit of turning every board over. On the back of a restaurant sign he once found the inscription for a dance band that had been thrown out of the same building decades earlier. He copied it down and brought the rubbing to the band’s remaining drummer. She made him bring a second copy for the drummer who had left under a cloud. According to her, they had both paid for the fucking sign.

Eddie’s dad drove a press cart and took the long way home past the walls the cart crews had commissioned. He taught Eddie which corners turned vicious around payday and which cafe would give you a towel after a soaking. He did not teach him to paint. Eddie learned from the men and women who painted family parties, shop fronts, eviction notices and the names of people whose deaths the official books reduced to a time and location.

What a wall owes the street

The GraffPUNKS carry public memory through layered murals, Tagtalk and the Paint Path. Their authority is scattered. A person can paint something that changes a district’s mind without winning the right to speak for its next victim. Eddie is the crew’s Waterfront representative because the local wall crews asked him to do the job for a season. At the end of it they can ask for somebody else. He likes to talk as if everybody already knows that detail. In practice he enjoys having his name across the top of a meeting notice.

He earned a difficult lesson painting the Held Door, a tribute to a woman who kept a stairwell open while the tenants upstairs cleared out. He used her full name without asking. Her employer could identify her from the wall, and docked two shifts for abandoning a post. Eddie first defended the painting on the grounds that everyone in the street had seen what she did. She asked him whether everyone in the street was going to make up her rent. He ran collection tins at four shops and paid the difference himself. The repaired picture carries the words she chose, and the corners of his old lettering are still visible from the pavement.

The paint that counted feet

On the night of the double tide, people leaving the lower walk found two paper arrows pointing in opposite directions. One was an obsolete service notice. Cold rain had pasted it to the newer one. Eddie painted a trail on the brickwork toward the uphill exit, then put a second mark at the turn so a crowd could see whether it was still on the right route. ZARAH8 gave him an estimate of how far the crowd could move at once. Nine Toes Joe made him shift a mark that would have sent loaded carts up the narrow stairs.

The following afternoon a news crew asked him to stage the painting again. The wall was dry by then and a new camera angle carried a sponsor’s banner beautifully. He turned the crew down. Two nights later, short of money, he accepted a different commission to illustrate the same disaster from memory. It took him a month to recognise that he had sneaked through his own rule by changing the medium. He gave the fee to the relief kitchen and kept the commission receipt on his wall.

The sound of a good room

Eddie collects drum breaks and reverses old shop jingles into dance loops. Teddy Boi Jones has the only surviving recording of Eddie trying to play a bass line from Billy’s Launderette on a broken organ. Teddy uses ten seconds of it as his exit music whenever Eddie has been talking for too long. Eddie makes his own wall rubbings on thin wrapping paper and eats salt-and-vinegar crisps with gloves on after a paint job. He is fussier about boiling water for tea than he is about the state of his sink.

If he trusts you, he will show you the part of a wall that embarrassed him. He can be sharp with new painters who want a clear patch at a site he has looked after for years. His closest friend outside the crew, Chada Boi, asks for one wall a month to be free of explanation. Eddie laughed the first time. Now he puts the kettle on while Chada watches people supply their own stories.

Whose lettering survives

Eddie and Teddy are working on a disputed wall catalogue. Teddy wants voices alongside the photographs. Eddie wants a contribution to be withdrawable after a person has heard the recording. Their first volunteer asked to keep the photo and erase the credit. A sponsor then threatened to cut the catalogue’s budget because the name was valuable. Eddie chose the volunteer. He still owes the sign shop for paper, and in a bad month he wonders if choosing the right thing ought to feel so much like another debt to his aunt.

At the Ninth Berth he looks after the public board and the wall behind it. He saves space under every caption for a correction. His ordinary ambition is a two-room print shop with a properly lit sink and a sound system strong enough to shake dust from the ceiling. Ask him who owns a wall and he’ll ask who has to see it on the way home. “Hold up. Who painted the bit everybody’s talking over?”

Five credits at a funeral

A painter from Eddie’s first crew died owing money for a scaffold and several favours involving the scaffold. The crew wanted one great name at the centre of the funeral piece. The painter’s brother brought five claims: the dead man’s name, the friend who drew the outline, the woman who mixed the paint, a shop that paid for the wall and two lads who held up the canvas between deliveries. They counted again. The shop wanted its name bigger than the dead man’s. It had brought sandwiches to the family for a week and assumed that put it in a useful bargaining position.

Eddie made a second panel where the entire group could record their contribution at the same size. The brother used his space to describe the months his sister had spent getting the painter to appointments. Nobody knew how to put that on a wall that was supposed to be about the art. They let it stay. Eddie likes to point visitors at the two panels and ask which one makes them feel they met anybody.
