The people beyond the wall

The Bitcoin Kids are the escaped and outside rebel lineage led by Alfie “The Bitcoin KiD” Blaze. In Year 3008, their name belongs to a community with beds to find, work to do and relatives they cannot safely visit. The Bitcoin Kid Army is its organised defensive expression. A person can belong to the Kids without serving on a patrol, and an ally can work beside the Army without acquiring a new ancestry.

The Bitcoin X Kids are the distinct engineered generation inside Block Topia. Older songs call almost every child of the Bitcoin Bloodline a Bitcoin Kid. That loose usage survives in speech, but it cannot identify which generation a record describes. An escaped adult remembering an inside nursery and an X Kid attending a city lesson are not automatically the same person at different points in a universal life cycle.

The name “Kids” survives growing older. It carries the insult once levelled at people said to be too immature to understand the city's bargain, and the stubborn familiarity of people who knew one another before they had public names. Some dislike it. A fitter who has spent years supporting a household can find it difficult to negotiate with a landlord who hears “Kid” and sees an unpaid apprentice. Others keep the name precisely because it irritates people who expect rank to accumulate into a title.

History before the escape

The world they inherit was damaged long before their rebellion. The Great Concord produced the Covenant in 2030, witnessed on Bitcoin. Its later transmission into the Sacred/Aether Chain has no established exact date. The World Chain became a different, civilisation-scale dependency and broke in the Triple Fork of 2880. By 2930 fewer than a dozen secure regions remained; the Queens stronghold became the basis of Block Topia. These events explain the city's obsession with continuity. They are not battles fought by Alfie's present Army.

Block Topia offered a formidable answer to that history: food systems that worked, identities a gate could recognise, shelter that did not depend on winning a street dispute every night. The Bloodline programme joined that promise to controlled reproduction and managed upbringing. Its claim was that a new generation could inherit security without inheriting the impulses that had ruined the old world. The escapees grew up inside versions of that promise. What they rejected was the city's right to turn its success at keeping people alive into permanent authority over their lives.

Their accounts disagree about when recognition became refusal. One remembers a reassigned parent. Another remembers a lesson in which a documented absence was changed into a voluntary departure. A third admits there was no revelation: she left because someone she loved was leaving. Later organisers prefer a clean conversion story. Actual recollection contains resentment, boredom, fear, loyalty and plans that changed at the last moment.

The one per cent claim

The “1% Rebels” and “99% Compliant” belong to a powerful archive tradition. They express the claim that a small minority escaped a programme designed to make departure unthinkable. They are not a verified census of every generation inside Block Topia. A faction banner can carry a percentage long after nobody can reconstruct the population from which it was calculated.

Outside, the small number became a source of pride and a reason to distrust newcomers. People who had risked everything began asking whether later arrivals had suffered enough to belong. Inside, the same number served a different purpose: rebellion could be presented as a rare defect in an otherwise successful design. Both uses hid ordinary differences. Remaining inside might mean loyalty, dependence, fear for a relative or the absence of a workable exit.

The Kids' worst arguments begin when these slogans become diagnoses. “Compliant” is an accusation, not a description of a person's entire inner life. Nor does having escaped establish courage in every later situation. The community includes people who crossed the wall and then could not face a hospital queue, a debt hearing or an apology. The outside world gives them more choices; it does not make every choice easy.

Alfie and the work of being followed

Alfie Blaze leads the escaped Kids and carries their signal across faction boundaries. His name opens doors that would remain shut to an unknown courier. It also makes a shelter conspicuous. A visit can bring food donations in the morning and a hostile search before nightfall. Hosts sometimes refuse the honour of housing him because they cannot afford the attention.

He is most useful when he can make rivals stay in the same room long enough to identify a common problem. He is least useful when followers treat an unfinished remark as an order. A suggestion to keep a route available can become a promise that the route is safe. A promise to seek a missing person can become a rumour that the person has already been recovered. The Army has to correct those claims without making every correction sound like a betrayal of its leader.

The Child of Fire reading connects him to Book VIII of the Covenant. The 2030 text does not name him. Some Kids paint its imagery beside his tag; others refuse portraits because a wall full of one face feels too much like the city they left. This disagreement is part of the movement. Alfie's significance does not settle his disputed origins, establish immortality or make his judgement infallible.

The Chain Break and its uneven aftermath

The Chain Break is the name carried by the escape tradition: the moment Block Topia's claim to contain the Bloodline failed. Accounts describe a breached wall, a broken psychological system, a signal that made departure imaginable, or several escapes later compressed into one night. The Army's historical companion preserves those differences. No reliable exact year has been established for the founding escape.

The first outside problem was recognition. A city identity could prove that someone had existed inside a system the host district hated. It could not prove that the person would pay rent, share water or leave when asked. Some refugees expected gratitude for rejecting the Authority. Their hosts had been living without its protection for generations and found the expectation insulting. Escapees brought useful knowledge, but they also brought habits formed by services that had always appeared when required.

Long after a crossing, the body can keep the city's timetable. People wake before an absent announcement or wait for permission to take a second portion. Others make a performance of refusing any schedule at all and become impossible to share a room with. Settling is less dramatic than escape and often more divisive. It requires accepting dependence on people who do not think your story is the centre of the world.

The Borrowed Rooms

The Borrowed Rooms are a loose arrangement of lodging offered by outside households, workshops and small halls. The name describes an obligation, not a hidden capital or a new faction. Each host decides how many people the space can carry. A room above a repair shop may have heat and no privacy; a dry platform may have privacy and no water. A place is listed as usable only for the conditions its host actually offers.

Arrivals are given something practical before they are asked for a public account: a place to sit, a cup, an explanation of the toilet and the sleeping arrangements. This custom developed because frightened people agreed to almost anything if they thought a bed depended on it. It remains uneven. A generous host may still use a guest's escape story to draw customers, and a guest may resent doing ordinary cleaning after surviving an extraordinary ordeal.

Mina Patch, an escaped fittings apprentice, became known for repairing the rooms nobody wanted to show visitors. She could stop a loose tap and mend a bed frame, but she could not make an overcrowded room less crowded. Her early notices listed capacities in blunt terms: two sleepers, one person needing quiet, no cooking after the downstairs shift. The notices were unpopular because they made hospitality look small. They also made it possible to keep a promise.

Mina Patch and the second door

Mina kept her work name after leaving the city. “Patch” had begun as a sneer at repairs too modest to impress a supervisor. Outside, a repair that lasted until the next proper supply run was often exactly what a household needed. Her first paid job was a door that swelled in damp weather. She spent half the fee replacing a tool she had borrowed and damaged, then concealed the cost because she wanted the next customer to think she was established.

The door became an awkward measure of her standing. People pointed to it as proof that escapees could be useful; Mina wanted them to notice the second door, which she had measured incorrectly. She had to take that one apart while the family waited in the cold. In the Borrowed Rooms, her rule that mistakes must be written beside successful repairs began as an attempt to stop herself repeating an expensive error, not as a grand theory of truth.

She does not run the Kids. She has authority when a joint is failing, less when a room's residents argue over guests, and none to send someone on an Army task. Her friendship with the courier Tavi Rill is strained whenever Tavi returns with a new arrival and assumes she will find space. They have saved one another trouble often enough to know exactly which trouble the other habitually creates.

Food, heat and the Three Pump stoppage

Three Pump is an outside service yard named for its surviving pump housings, not a numbered city district. Refugee lodgings, a washroom and several workshops depend on its water and heat arrangements. During a local stoppage in 3008, two housings failed while the third continued intermittently. The immediate quarrel was over which users would lose their normal share. Every household could explain why its own use was essential.

Mina helped inspect the damaged fittings. Cal Vetch, the Army quartermaster, arrived with an allocation sheet that counted patrol needs and missed the laundry used by carers. Jalen Rusk, who cooked for the yard, refused to treat that omission as a minor clerical problem. Clean bedding had become a hidden military supply because sick and exhausted people were recovering in rooms the Army did not list as its own.

The compromise reduced washroom hours, pooled some cooking and delayed an Army equipment wash. It kept the yard functioning, but residents still lost wages while carrying water and waiting for access. No one discovered an unlimited reserve. The repair bill outlasted the emergency, and Jalen continued to charge the Army for meals it had assumed were donations. Three Pump is remembered because survival produced a debt, not because everyone became friends.

Work beyond rebellion

Many Kids make a living from the unglamorous difference between knowing how something should function and being able to keep it functioning with poor parts. They sort fittings, mend clothing, carry parcels, clean storage rooms, restore damaged recordings and help customers interpret old city equipment. Others have skills the outside economy cannot readily use. A perfect record-management qualification buys little when a market has no reliable power and settles most exchanges face to face.

Work introduces dependencies that patrol stories omit. A repairer may need permission from a landlord who dislikes Alfie. A courier may have to wait for a trader who deals with the Authority. A performer may earn more from an audience that wants the escape story exaggerated. Refusing every compromise can mean transferring the cost to a housemate who still has to buy dinner.

Apprenticeship is therefore a political institution as well as a way to learn a trade. Hosts want assurance that a trainee will remain long enough to repay teaching with useful labour. Trainees fear finding another system that calls permanent obligation an opportunity. The more workable agreements name a period, a wage or food arrangement, and the tools the trainee can take away. They fail when either party treats affection as a substitute for terms.

MemeCoin and the price of a joke

The older rebel account describes MemeCoin as a currency made from remixed cultural relics and resistant to the city's financial tracking. In practice, the tradition gives the Kids an economy of jokes, scarce copies, favours and reputation alongside ordinary barter and whatever money a particular market accepts. Its boast of being untraceable is not a universal technical guarantee. A distinctive joke can identify its maker more clearly than a name.

A loop of old music may be valuable to a collector and worthless to the person selling a filter. A newly popular image can finance a week of food, then become impossible to trade when everyone has a copy. Cal Vetch accepts cultural receipts for some discretionary exchanges but does not write the next month's staple purchases against yesterday's fashionable price. This makes him unpopular when a new issue is rising and indispensable after it falls.

Counterfeits complicate the argument that all copying is liberation. A copied joke is one thing; a copied mark falsely claiming that a maker approved a debt is another. The Kids have learned to separate permission to circulate a piece from permission to promise labour in its maker's name. Their practice resembles the Nexus Copy Ledger, though the Nexus is not their central bank and cannot enforce every bargain made on the road.

Tavi Rill and the travelling household

Tavi Rill carries parcels between Borrowed Rooms, host workshops and Army meeting points. She is fast at remembering who needs a parcel delivered privately and poor at admitting that a journey has become too much for her. Her reputation began with reliable ordinary deliveries, then became distorted by a rescue story that credited her with carrying three people. She had carried one person's bag while other people did the harder work.

The correction cost her invitations to two paid appearances. It also made her a more useful witness at later returns: families understood that she would say when she had not seen someone. She now writes the end of a journey before its beginning in her notebook—what actually arrived, who accepted it, what remained unresolved—because departure speeches had been taking up pages needed for delivery problems.

Her household travels in pieces. A blanket stays with Mina, a tin of letters with a market friend, work clothes at whichever room currently has a dry shelf. This dispersal helps her move and makes loss difficult to count. She can survive one room closing, but she cannot simply “go home” when tired. The romantic image of the road hides the labour of keeping several partial homes in other people's patience.

Names, clothes and earned appearance

A new arrival is not obliged to discard a city name. Some choose a work name immediately; others keep several names for different relationships. The awkward cases are shared names, names copied from a dead friend, and names that an outside patron has already printed on posters. A person can discover that the name chosen in private has become someone else's product before it has become familiar in their own mouth.

Clothing is adjusted for weather, repairability and the need to belong without becoming conspicuous everywhere. A visible mark can secure a welcome in one district and a surcharge in the next. Borrowed coats carry several histories. Etta Reed's patched coat, preserved in the Sacred Chain account, is admired because its repairs remain attributed to the hands that made them. It is not an official Bitcoin Kids uniform or proof that its wearer belongs to their lineage.

Bonnets produce more argument than almost any other object. Some escapees keep one as a record of work they refuse to let the city erase. Others will not wear anything resembling a rank. The bonnet traditions disagree over rights by birth and rights earned in trials. The Kids do not settle that history by declaring every bare head oppressed or every decorated one compromised.

Lessons at the Backstep School

The Backstep School began as lessons held on the broad rear steps of a host workshop. The name now follows the practice when the class moves. It is a small teaching arrangement within the outside community, not one of the forty factions. Lessons combine reading, sums, route descriptions, craft knowledge and the ability to ask what a witness actually saw. Older escapees attend when they need to learn something the city never required them to do.

One recurring exercise gives learners a broken household budget. The dramatic answer is usually to confront the trader. The useful answer often starts by finding the missing cost of fuel, care or a borrowed cart. Another exercise compares two accounts of a street incident without announcing which is right. Pupils dislike discovering that both witnesses can be accurate about different parts and still unable to settle the whole case.

Children living among the Kids are not automatically soldiers. When patrol organisers began treating quick learners as a reserve of unpaid messengers, hosts pushed back by making lesson time unavailable for routine Army errands. The boundary remains contested under pressure. A young person can be proud of being needed and still be used beyond what they understand. School offers a place to be temporarily bad at something without risking everyone else's journey.

Nights, music and small luxuries

Evenings are not a continuous rebel gathering. People wash, argue over noise, mend things badly and try to have a conversation that is not about the city. Music travels through cheap speakers, remembered lyrics and loops traded so often their original names have fallen away. A host may tolerate a political broadcast and still ban a favourite song after hearing it every night for a week.

The most prized luxuries are often choices the city made unnecessary: a flavour chosen badly, a wall painted an awkward colour, an hour spent without a useful result. These freedoms can become performances. New arrivals sometimes feel obliged to like every harsh sound and uncomfortable room to prove they are no longer compliant. Older residents recognise the strain and occasionally admit that they too miss a particular city meal.

Romance and friendship have the same material limits as everything else. Two people may be close and unable to share a room. An argument can threaten a whole sleeping arrangement. A breakup may require friends to redistribute storage, rent and childcare before anyone can enjoy the dignity of leaving. The Kids have no single ceremony that resolves this. They have households learning, unevenly, how to stop a personal quarrel becoming a public loyalty test.

GraffPUNKS, paint and local permission

The GraffPUNKS give the Kids access to traditions in which walls preserve routes, warnings, names and disagreement. The Kids carry those traditions between places that cannot rely on a single working network. This is an alliance of needs and skills. It does not make every escapee a master of Spraycode or every painted mark a weapon.

A wall has users before a courier arrives. Its owner may want rent, its neighbours may want daylight, and the family whose missing relative is painted there may not want the portrait covered by a mobilisation notice. Early Army crews damaged several local relationships by treating a useful surface as empty infrastructure. Backstep teachers now make learners describe what is already on a wall before asking what they would add.

Paint itself imposes limits. Smart pigment, ordinary colour and temporary chalk are not interchangeable. Expensive materials invite theft and exaggerated claims. A modest mark regularly renewed by people who know its meaning can be more dependable than a celebrated mural nobody has the equipment to maintain. That does not make the mural worthless; it means the community must decide what labour it can afford to put behind admiration.

The Nomad Bears and unwelcome attention

W81 describes the Nomad Bears as observers of dead zones and intermediaries in the delivery of Mnemonic Whispers. Their connection to the Kids is useful and uneasy. A courier who wants a guide can experience solitude as selfishness; a Bear whose quiet route becomes crowded can experience the request as a slow occupation. Being tracked for protection can still feel like being tracked.

The Kids cannot demand every route a Bear knows or treat an old exchange as lifelong permission. Some relationships last because they are narrow: one parcel, one warning, a recording in return for a night's shelter. Others become friendships that are harder to explain to officials on either side. The absence of a public contract does not mean the absence of an obligation; it means outsiders may not know what the obligation actually is.

The Bears' supposed Antichain immunity remains a claim of their archive tradition. The Kids' own anomalies are equally poor grounds for declaring anyone safe from every form of corruption. Neither quiet contemplation nor restless improvisation replaces rest, observation and the admission that a frightening symptom may have more than one cause.

Kin still inside

Contact with relatives in Block Topia is one of the movement's least visible pressures. Some families stop writing to protect an inside member's position. Others maintain a thin exchange of ordinary facts: a meal remembered, a repair completed, an elder's condition. A small message can matter precisely because it carries no demand to choose a side.

Outside organisers sometimes want these contacts turned into intelligence. The relative becomes “access,” and a letter that once belonged to a family becomes evidence for a campaign. Tavi refuses routine requests to copy private messages into an Army report. She is not always thanked. Someone who fears a patrol is missing may find the distinction between private correspondence and urgent information unbearable.

The X Kids inside remain a distinct generation. Helping an individual does not let an outside leader declare that generation secretly enrolled in his Army. Nor does an X Kid's curiosity prove a wish to leave. Any later change in a person's situation needs its own account. The bloodline is a history of relationships, not a switch that changes everyone together when a hero sends a signal.

Feast Week and the Grey Return

The Hard Fork Games bring people, supplies and competing accounts into the city's orbit. W81 places rebel incursions and assistance to endangered emissaries around Feast Week. The local Grey Return of 3008 belongs to that wider pressure: a mixed returning party reached outside lodgings with interrupted travel records, damaged kit and conflicting instructions about who was expected where. Its name came from the plain grey coverings used over baggage, not a secret Army classification.

The Kids were asked to do what official arrival ceremonies had not prepared anyone for: arrange beds, find absent luggage, establish which family had already been told what, and keep a confused return from becoming a public confession. Tavi carried corrections between hosts. Mina reopened a room after its owner agreed to a shorter stay than organisers wanted. Cal had to pay for transport twice because the first booking assumed everyone could travel onward immediately.

Mira Latch's already recorded refusal of a simple city-versus-rebel account mattered to how some listeners approached the return. The Grey Return does not rewrite her biography or identify her as one of its passengers. It shows the use of her narrower lesson: returning from the Games is not evidence that a person agrees with whoever receives them. Some passengers wanted distance from both sides and resented being introduced as proof of anyone's cause.

Anomalies and Mnemonic Whispers

Dreams, unexpected language, inconsistent recall and the Eye Without Hash recur in the Bloodline accounts. W81's Mnemonic Whisper tradition describes untainted memories transmitted to the escaped Kids, with the GKniftyHEADS and Nomad Bears involved in protection and delivery. It also describes the Kids as a testbed. That word matters. A proposed defence can make vulnerable people valuable to institutions that do not have to live with the effects.

Among the Borrowed Rooms, listeners distinguish what someone experienced from an explanation of its cause. A repeated dream is recorded as a repeated dream. It does not become a verified message from NULL because its imagery resembles a known account. The same caution protects a person whose exhaustion has been misread as disloyalty. This is not certainty that the danger is imaginary; it is a way to avoid making every uncertainty serve the loudest theory.

The Kernel recovery tradition supplies useful language for separating a trace, a fragment, an interpretation and a reconstruction. The Kids borrow that language without claiming to know what the Kernel originally was. A listener may help someone preserve a memory while admitting that the available record cannot restore a lost interval. False reassurance can be as coercive as a threat when it persuades someone to accept a procedure they would otherwise question.

Being a community during mobilisation

When the Army calls for help, households decide what they can actually spare. A cook may be able to produce food but need someone to carry fuel. A parent may offer a room only if the Army covers the rent lost by cancelling work. A disabled repairer may be more useful remaining at the bench than being praised for appearing at a muster. Mobilisation makes these dependencies visible and does not always reward the people it reveals.

The HODL Warriors arise in the convergence account when faction conflict becomes a threat to shared memory and survival. The Kids can carry that call without owning everyone who answers. W81's stronger story of an unavoidable overwrite survives as a disputed activation tradition. It is not the everyday rule of the Borrowed Rooms, and a household's refusal of a specific task does not prove that it wants the world erased.

A returning patrol often finds that civilian work has become harder while it was away. Bills have accumulated, a promised repair is late, and hosts are angry at being thanked instead of paid. These are not distractions from resistance. They decide whether the same community will be capable of sheltering the next arrival.

Loss, departures and the unresolved future

Not every departure from the Kids is betrayal. Some people move for work, marry into another community, become tired of Alfie's followers or simply want to stop having their childhood discussed. The hardest cases involve a return toward the city. Friends may understand the material reason and still feel abandoned. A movement founded on leaving has to learn that leaving can happen to it too.

Memorials preserve more than combat losses. A person who died during a bad winter or after a repair injury can disappear from public memory if every wall is reserved for a spectacular stand. The Borrowed Rooms keep household accounts beside Army names where families permit it. They draw on the Sacred Chain's distinction between public record and closed shelf, allowing an absence to be acknowledged without exposing every private detail of a life.

In 3008 the Kids remain outside, divided, useful and unfinished. They have not freed the entire Bloodline, solved the Antichain or reached the Final Fork. The most reliable evidence of their existence is less grand: a room made habitable, a delivery corrected, a person who can disagree with Alfie and still be given supper. Their future depends on whether those ordinary arrangements can survive the scale of the war forming around them.
