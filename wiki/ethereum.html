Ethereum (ETH)

✅ Verified 📅 Last updated: October 2026 📂 Cryptocurrencies

Ethereum is a programmable blockchain whose native asset, ETH, pays transaction fees and supports proof-of-stake security. Smart contracts allow participants to maintain shared application state, including tokens, exchanges and lending arrangements. Their execution follows network rules, but an application can still contain bugs, depend on administrators or lose access to external services.

History and origins

The whitepaper (2013)

Vitalik Buterin proposed Ethereum in 2013 as a more general platform for blockchain applications. A wider founding group developed the project; Gavin Wood’s Yellow Paper described its technical design. The history does not reduce the implementation or the Solidity language to one person’s work.

The 2014 crowdsale

A public sale of ether in 2014 helped finance development. It preceded the operating mainnet, so participants were funding a proposed network rather than buying a demonstrated service. Retrospective peak-price returns omit timing, custody, liquidity and the possibility that an early project could fail.

Frontier (2015)

Ethereum’s mainnet launched on 30 July 2015 with Frontier, an early release oriented toward developers. Common token interfaces subsequently made it easier for applications and wallets to recognise assets issued by different contracts. ERC-20 became particularly influential for fungible tokens.

The DAO and the 2016 fork

The DAO was an investment experiment whose contract was exploited in June 2016. Ethereum participants disagreed over how to respond. The adopted hard fork introduced a specific state change that moved affected funds into a recovery arrangement; it did not roll back and erase the intervening chain history.

The chain continuing without that intervention became known as Ethereum Classic. The disagreement concerned social governance, software rules and recovery after loss. Describing one side as morally correct or economically inevitable would conceal the dispute rather than explain it.

Token fundraising and CryptoKitties (2017)

Token sales expanded sharply during 2017, financing both serious projects and poorly supported or fraudulent offerings. CryptoKitties then drew attention to unique digital collectibles and placed pressure on network capacity. These episodes demonstrated demand for shared applications alongside congestion and speculative risk.

DeFi Summer (2020)

Compound’s COMP distribution, Uniswap v2 and yield-aggregation services helped popularise lending, trading and liquidity incentives in 2020. Applications could interact through shared contracts and assets. That composability also linked their risks: an incentive programme could attract deposits without proving durable demand or solvency.

London and EIP-1559 (2021)

The London upgrade in August 2021 introduced a dynamically adjusted base fee that is burned, with a priority fee available to the block producer. Burning offsets some issuance. ETH’s net supply change depends on both issuance and activity; deflation is a possible outcome, not a permanent property guaranteed by this upgrade.

The 2021 market expansion

NFT trading, DeFi activity and broader market speculation accompanied a sharp rise in ETH’s quoted price during 2021. A historical peak is not a current price record or evidence that fee burning alone caused the move. Comparisons require a dated market source and a consistent measurement method.

The Merge (2022)

On 15 September 2022, Ethereum moved its consensus from proof of work to proof of stake through the Merge. It substantially reduced the energy needed for consensus and changed issuance. It did not by itself make every transaction cheaper or increase execution capacity enough to eliminate congestion.

Shanghai and Capella (2023)

The April 2023 Shanghai/Capella upgrades enabled withdrawals from validator stakes. Withdrawal eligibility, queues and the distinction between rewards and exiting a validator remain part of the mechanism. A staking service can impose additional terms beyond those of the base protocol.

Dencun and rollup data (2024)

Dencun, activated in March 2024, introduced blob data through EIP-4844. This provides a separate data-fee market used by rollups, reducing a major cost for many Layer 2 transactions. The saving is not a permanent percentage or universal fee: demand, rollup design and its own charges still matter.

US spot ether products (2024)

US spot ether exchange-traded products began trading in July 2024 after the relevant approval process. They provide brokerage exposure under fund and custody terms. Product approval does not settle every legal question about ETH, staking services or applications built on Ethereum.

How it works

The Ethereum Virtual Machine

The EVM specifies how contract instructions change Ethereum state. Nodes checking execution reproduce those results under the same rules. Gas bounds the computation that a transaction can consume. Deterministic execution means consistent interpretation of valid instructions; it does not mean correct business logic, uninterrupted access or immunity from censorship.

Gas and transaction fees

Gas measures work and resource use. Transactions specify fee limits and pay in ETH. The base fee responds to block demand; priority fees compensate block producers for inclusion. A failed contract call can still consume gas. A wallet estimate is not a promise that a transaction will succeed or be included at a particular time.

Proof of stake

Validators propose and attest to blocks using committed ETH. The established minimum to activate a validator is 32 ETH; pooled arrangements let participants contribute smaller amounts under additional rules. Rewards vary with participation, performance and fees. Downtime can incur penalties, while specific contradictory or otherwise slashable actions can destroy part of a stake.

Liquid staking adds a transferable claim on a position, not a guaranteed cash equivalent. Operator concentration, contract control, withdrawal queues and receipt-token pricing all deserve separate consideration. A historical dashboard can help inspect dated stake distribution without establishing a permanent annual return.

Layer 2 rollups

Rollups execute transactions outside the base-layer execution environment and publish transaction data to Ethereum alongside state commitments or validity proofs. Optimistic systems use mechanisms for disputing invalid results; validity systems supply proofs. Neither label alone guarantees instant finality or immediate withdrawal to the base layer.

Arbitrum, Optimism and Base are examples associated with optimistic rollups; zkSync and Starknet use validity-proof approaches. Sequencer operations, upgrade controls, bridges, proof systems and data availability differ. An application’s quick confirmation and settlement on Ethereum are separate stages.

Applications and shared standards

Ethereum offers common execution and token interfaces that let independent applications interact. DeFi protocols, stablecoins, NFTs and governance systems use this shared environment. A token standard establishes how an asset behaves at an interface; it does not confer copyright, guarantee reserves or decide legal ownership.

This flexibility makes the chain useful as infrastructure while concentrating dependencies. Several applications may rely on the same collateral, oracle or bridge. Activity across many interfaces can therefore carry a shared source of failure.

Risks and trade-offs

  • Validator and staking-provider concentration can weaken independence. A service’s market share changes and should be measured at a stated date.
  • Contract bugs, administrator keys and external data can affect an application even when the base chain operates normally.
  • Rollup bridges, sequencers and withdrawal procedures introduce risks beyond a simple ETH transfer.
  • Fees, token prices and staking returns vary; none is fixed by an adoption narrative.
  • Legal treatment differs by activity and jurisdiction. Approval of an exchange-traded product does not exempt every related service.
