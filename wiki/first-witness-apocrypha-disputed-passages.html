Apocrypha and Disputed Passages

First WitnessDisputed literature · Section 41Last updated: October 2026

A thousand-year scripture without apocrypha would be unbelievable. The First Witness accumulated letters, sermons, poems and alleged lost books almost immediately.

The Book of Mirrors

The most famous apocryphon describes a city where every citizen receives a mirror showing only what others believe about them. The city becomes obsessed with maintaining reflections and eventually forgets how to act without being watched.

The text is almost certainly centuries later than 2030, but its critique of reputation systems made it enormously influential before the World Chain collapse.

The Letters from the Flooded Archive

A collection of short letters supposedly written by archivists rescuing records during an unnamed flood. Some letters are practical instructions. Others are meditations on choosing which boxes to save when everything cannot be saved.

Their central question—“What deserves the last dry shelf?”—became famous among Keepers.

The Sermon of the Empty Wallet

This satirical economic text follows a wealthy trader who loses access to every account but still possesses food, friends and useful skills. The trader initially calls himself ruined. His neighbour asks which of those things the missing number had actually created.

Finance Guild teachers either love or despise the sermon depending on the school.

The Last Keeper Fragment

A damaged text contains only a dialogue between an old keeper and an unnamed successor. The surviving final line reads: “If you preserve only my answer, you have not preserved my work.”

Nobody knows whether the missing preceding sentence would make the line mean something very different.

The Crown Verse

A disputed line appears in some Book VIII manuscripts: “The fire shall know the rightful crown.” It is absent from the Bitcoin-witnessed canonical text and is considered a later interpolation.

Pro-Alfie cults historically quoted it anyway. Crownless schools preserve the verse as a teaching example of how one extra sentence can reverse an entire theology.

The Hundredth Name

This lost-list tradition claims the original Covenant contained one hundred names for human dignity, one of which was deliberately left blank. No authenticated copy supports the claim.

Despite this, naming ceremonies in several regions preserve an empty line called the Hundredth Name.

Why apocrypha stays visible

The First Witness tradition does not need disputed texts destroyed. It needs them labelled. A later poem can still be wise. A forged ancient date can still be false.

The distinction between value and provenance is one of the religion's central intellectual habits.
