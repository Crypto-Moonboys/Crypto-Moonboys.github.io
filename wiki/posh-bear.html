Life on the Waterfront

Posh Bear came to the Waterfront in a good suit and earned the nickname before he had finished his first shift. His green monocle magnifies the old tool marks that can tell a repairer whether two pieces belong together. He has spent more time assessing battered cabinet hinges than buying anything expensive.

The thing the dealer would throw away

Posh learnt to judge hinges at a bench where the prices were chalked up while the repairs were still drying. He began writing what he could see in a joint on one slip and what a dealer had offered on another. People would often ask to read the second slip first. A good coat gets him through doors where somebody with dirty knees would be sent round the back. He thinks about this whenever he quotes his price to a shop that has to stay open through the making of a repair.

He favours tea strong enough to register through a cold and keeps spare thread in the pocket below his monocle. Gentleman Bear brings him finds without telling him what the owner claims they are worth. Graff Bear trusts him to speak plainly about a painted surface, though Posh can spend fifteen minutes defining “salvageable” when he is afraid to say who might lose money.

The smallest sign

A dealer once offered a generous price for a group of workshop signs on the understanding that the smallest one would be thrown away. It carried the only legible last name from an old cooperative. Posh Bear walked it back to the family that had asked about the sale and had to admit he had nearly written the thing off himself.

Off duty

He likes estimating the age of a stitch through a layer of later mending. The seams of his own plaid suit are a running diary of repairs. He accepts a cup of tea for a small appraisal and insists on paying for a good stitch.

The border still in the wood

Under the direction arrow at the Ninth Berth he found a cut in the shutter that showed where the original lettering stopped. He gave the crew an edge to work to but refused to name a salvage price while the owner was deciding whether to keep the shutter. He had nearly priced somebody’s working history out of a sale once before. Seeing the former signwriter ask for her own tracing made him wonder what she must have expected his first answer to be.

He wants the next repair bill to include the cost of putting the old surface back in service. A new shutter may still come in cheaper. He keeps a sketch of the cut beside the stitches in his notebook and will show it to anyone who asks what the monocle is for.

Posh sells coke off the bench. A client who owed him for it offered him first refusal on the old shutter. Posh understood what the man wanted him to overlook in the price and walked away from a sum he could have used. He still sees the man on the Waterfront and has yet to tell the signwriter the whole story.
