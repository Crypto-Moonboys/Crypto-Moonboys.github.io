⚔️ The AllCity Bulls

⚔️ Lore 📅 Last updated: October 2026 📂 Lore

The AllCity Bulls build their Year 3008 culture around achievement that other people can see. They compete, stage contests, promote champions and trade speculative claims tied to performance. Their arenas make shared rules visible in a fractured world, but their appetite for ranking can turn every human difference into a contest somebody must lose. The name AllCity is an ambition to become recognisable everywhere, not proof that they govern every district. Their deepest fear is that a record can disappear while the effort behind it remains, leaving nobody able to prove what was accomplished.

Visibility among the Forty

The Bulls are one of the Forty faction cultures. The First Witness commentaries describe their reading of the Wall through visibility and the Hold through relentless presence. They understand repeated public signal: a name, colour or performance becomes familiar through many appearances. Their weakness is confusing widespread recognition with a right to dominate the places where they are recognised.

They are not a single sports league, a complete census of athletes or the official government of Block Topia. Arena crews, competitors, market operators and supporters gather under the name for different reasons. Some want an exact measure of personal improvement; others want a public hierarchy in which they can remain safely above somebody else.

Competition, speculation and public standing

W81’s dedicated account calls the Bulls the Hyper-Competitive Marketeers. It identifies high-stakes arenas, the Hard Fork Games, broadcast validation and Hyper-Shares, fictional speculative instruments tied to projected performance. It also records vulnerability to Rugpull Miners, conflict with the Blockchain Furies and fear that NULL will dissolve the records on which status depends.

The older urban-enforcer portrait preserves force, gladiatorial imagery and paid alliances. It does not establish absolute control of the city’s centre or universal superiority in combat. The broader archive provides a more useful picture: a culture that can produce disciplined athletes, skilled organisers and merciless speculators from the same insistence that effort must result in a publicly visible difference.

When the scoreboard broke

The Bulls’ origin tradition begins with the World Chain Triple Fork in 2880. People whose identities depended on recognised achievement face contradictory records of wealth, rank and reputation. The faction account imagines competitive minds surviving through the Genesis Kernel while retaining an obsessive need to establish where they stand. That is a mythic collective origin, not a verified biography of every Bull.

The subsequent world gives the fear a material basis. By 2930 fewer than a dozen secure regions remain, and the Queens stronghold becomes Block Topia’s foundation. In the outside territories, recognition must travel between communities with different records. Bulls answer with arenas and repeated performances: if an old victory cannot be verified, stage another one where everyone is looking.

The arena as a public institution

A working arena requires more than competitors. Builders maintain its surfaces, judges interpret rules, callers make proceedings understandable and stewards keep people moving. Someone must decide whether bad weather, damaged equipment or interrupted power makes two results incomparable. These decisions determine which achievements become portable beyond the event itself.

Bull organisers take pride in making a contest legible. A clear start and finish can create common ground between people who otherwise disagree about almost everything. Yet rules also decide what will remain invisible. Endurance measured through one body or machine may exclude another. A spectacular final lap can overshadow months of preparation and the work that allowed the venue to open at all.

Long Lap Yard

Long Lap Yard is a local outside training ground shared by several Bull crews. Its course changes when buildings become unsafe or neighbouring workshops need access. The organisers therefore preserve descriptions of each version instead of pretending that every record belongs to an identical route. The yard’s name travels more reliably than its physical boundary.

Jessa Pace, a trainer and former competitor, manages practice time. She can recognise fatigue in someone who is determined to call it weakness. She also resents losing a promising participant to another crew. Her authority is local and imperfect: people trust her knowledge while questioning whether her ambitions for the yard always match their ambitions for themselves.

The Measured Mile

The Measured Mile is Long Lap’s recurring public contest. “Mile” is the inherited name, not a claim that the changing route has one fixed length. Each event states its conditions so a result can be understood later. Crews compare like with like where possible and openly argue when a sponsor tries to merge incompatible records into a more impressive headline.

Milo Banner, the yard’s scorekeeper, keeps annotations beside the celebrated times. A stopped clock, a revised course or an assisted finish does not automatically cancel an achievement, but it changes the claim that can honestly be made about it. Milo’s handwriting appears on fewer banners than the champions’ names even though his careful work makes those names credible.

Crews, sponsors and household work

Bull crews often form around a venue, a discipline or a recognised competitor. Support can include equipment, food, lodging and access to practice. Sponsors expect recognition in return, but the nature of that obligation varies. Some fund a season; others try to make a young competitor’s entire future repay an early favour.

Households absorb much of the unstable work. Relatives rearrange meals around training, neighbours share rooms during events and a retired competitor may teach children while waiting for promised appearance fees. A crew that sees only the final ranking can mistake this support for an unlimited natural resource. Jessa’s training plans make room for outside work because enthusiasm cannot replace rent, care or sleep.

The Hyper-Share economy

In W81, Hyper-Shares convert expectation into a tradable fictional claim. They tie the audience’s appetite to the predicted fortunes of competitors and factions. A successful performance can therefore raise status and enrich people who never trained for it. A popular favourite may become valuable before winning anything at all.

The market changes the experience of competing. A participant who wants to withdraw can face pressure from people whose expected gains depend on the next appearance. A poor result becomes a public financial grievance. This economy exists within the story; it is not an offer, a live market description or a promise attached to the faction’s current community identity.

The Unranked Heat

Jessa reserves an Unranked Heat for practice without a published placing. Participants can test unfamiliar equipment or return after injury without turning every attempt into a permanent result. Competitive members argue that a performance without a ranking is merely rehearsal. Others discover that rehearsal is exactly what their daily lives have been missing.

The arrangement becomes controversial when a returning champion draws a large audience. Sponsors want the heat promoted; Milo refuses to add a retrospective scoreboard. The event earns less than it might have earned, and the champion later returns to ranked competition without an automatic triumph. The custom offers room to learn without requiring anyone to renounce the pleasure of winning.

The Fourth Lap protest

The Fourth Lap protest begins when a runner stops to assist another participant and is recorded only as a late finisher. Supporters demand that the time be changed to recognise the intervention. Milo refuses to falsify the clock but also refuses the sponsor’s request to omit why the delay occurred. Jessa initially wants a compensating rank, then accepts that one table cannot express every kind of achievement.

The published result remains. A separate account records the assistance and the affected runner’s testimony. The sponsor withdraws a bonus because the preferred victory story has become complicated. The assisting runner loses money but gains recognition of a different kind. The disagreement produces a new local award rather than a miraculous system in which care and competitive position always align.

The Carry Medal

The Carry Medal recognises work that made another person’s participation possible. At Long Lap, recipients have included a fitter, a route steward and a participant who surrendered practice time. The medal carries no automatic competitive rank. Its purpose is to make supporting labour visible without pretending it can be measured by the same instrument as speed.

It soon acquires its own status games. Well-connected helpers receive more nominations, and a sponsor offers to fund a larger ceremony in exchange for naming rights. Milo’s objection is not that recognition has become impure; public culture was never free of influence. He wants the award to retain a usable account of what the recipient actually did.

The Hard Fork Games and Feast Week

The Hard Fork Games are the Bulls’ largest arena of recognition in the source tradition. Block Node Defender status matters, but the faction also cares about broadcast statistics and the expectations attached to them. Feast Week becomes another performance space, where public duels, appearance and assessment can all be turned into comparisons.

The specialised account claims competitors pursue favourable biometric readings under P-fly’s concealed scanners. The claim describes the Games narrative, not a verified universal scoring system. The wider page preserves the forty-faction summons while leaving disputed trial mechanics attributed. A Bull’s desire to win does not explain away the institution’s coercion or guarantee that a conspicuous result yields the promised future.

When confidence becomes a liability

The Rugpull Miners exploit the Bulls’ belief that competitive intelligence will protect them from deception. W81 describes rigged-race promises and nonexistent prize pools. The important consequence is social: admitting the loss may feel like admitting that someone else understood the contest better. That shame can keep a crew defending a failed venture long after ordinary caution would end it.

The Blockchain Furies challenge manipulative gains; the Finance Guild seeks solvency and predictable obligations. Neither relationship makes all Bull winnings fraudulent. It does mean the faction cannot claim that a profitable outcome proves a fair contest. The Nice & Easy Bois sometimes mediate the disputes that remain after a public score has failed to settle anything.

People beyond the scoreboard

The Bulls’ source narrator dismisses Block Topia’s inside children as complacent beneficiaries of support. That is faction prejudice, not an adequate account of the Bitcoin X Kids. Stable meals and care do not prove that a person lacks ambition. Likewise, the outside society contains rules, maintenance and institutions even when a Bull complains that it offers no single scoreboard.

The outside Bitcoin Kids and Alfie’s Army are distinct. W81 places them in transactional relationships with Bull buyers seeking recent information. The Bulls’ interest in Thera-9 and other defectors similarly treats personal risk as a potentially valuable asset. Neither story grants the faction ownership of a witness or turns the witness’s life into a competition result.

The Afterimage Board

The Afterimage Board at Long Lap records former participants after they cease competing. Entries can include teaching, recovery, new employment or simply a statement that a person prefers not to be followed. Jessa begins it after discovering how many retired competitors remain familiar as records while becoming strangers as neighbours.

Some supporters want only the glorious names retained. Others demand proof of every new achievement. The board survives as a modest local practice because Milo refuses to treat retirement as disqualification from a life. It also reminds Jessa that her own history is more than the final result she would like everyone to forget.

A contest without dependable records

NULL’s Antichain threatens the Bulls through contradictory recognition. A victory might be recorded differently in two places, or the evidence of a participant’s existence might become unstable. W81 turns this into an image of psychological collapse. The page retains their acute vulnerability without declaring that every Bull responds identically or that one signal guarantees illness.

A crew can preserve multiple accounts, continue training and care for its members while admitting that a final ranking is unavailable. That response is harder for people whose status depended on a single authoritative table. The Final Fork remains unresolved. The Bulls cannot ensure that their names will survive it, but they can decide what kind of community exists while they are waiting.

Gladiators, Iron Pacts and territorial myths

The legacy gladiator cycle tells of ancient arenas, Iron Pacts with elites, humming territory totems and the Crush Protocol. These images preserve a remembered relationship between spectacle, force and patronage. They do not establish invincible contracts, a universal combat algorithm or ownership of Block Topia’s core.

Claims of inevitable victory are part of the faction’s performance of confidence. Some older tales turn the Nomad Bears into permanent territorial enemies; the stronger contrast is cultural, between mobile life and public ranking. Neither culture is explained entirely by the other’s opinion. The Bulls remain capable of discipline and generosity alongside the insecurity that makes every unmeasured life difficult for them to understand.

Milo Banner corrects a victory that was not an arrival

A programme promoter asks Milo Banner to include Ascension selection in a Long Lap display of successful careers. The examples include a Bull's accepted preparation role, a conditional nomination and Rack Three's ground return. Milo initially likes the comparison because it makes the yard appear connected to a larger future. Jessa Pace asks which person has actually arrived at a receiving destination.

The available evidence supports training and nominations, with no such arrival shown. Milo revises the display to keep those achievements in separate lines. The promoted title loses some spectacle and gains a usable meaning. A candidate with a real preparation job remains proud of it and objects to outside critics calling the entire role imaginary. Jessa agrees: a dishonest caption does not erase the work somebody has actually obtained.

Mira's Return Valve record also reaches the yard. Jessa uses it to teach the difference between a fault correctly found and a timed allocation left unfinished. Some trainees regard the omitted maintenance worker as grounds to reject the whole score. Others say Mira should have finished first and complained later. The exercise gives them a disagreement they can work through instead of requiring every losing entrant to have been secretly the true champion.

Milo adds no inferred orbital return to the Afterimage Board. The board can state a new job, a nomination or a person's wish not to be followed. It cannot turn prestige into a destination receipt. Hyper-Share expectations remain a fictional source tradition; these corrections create no real market, token promise or prediction of a competitor's future income.

The Night Heat and a winner already in debt

The Night Heat is a new local contest at Long Lap Yard during the early Night Cover publicity, before the Cinder Shed attack. It uses one stated version of the changing Measured Mile course; it is not another annual Hard Fork Games or a universal timed mile. Competitors, workers and spectators in this account are adults over 21. Vexa Row, 28, wants the event money, the status and the pleasure of beating people who have underestimated her.

Jessa Pace is 44 and Milo Banner is 30 here. Jessa notices Vexa carrying strain from earlier work and training. The appearance fee will cover costs the yard cannot easily replace. She narrows one practice interval and still lets the planned start go ahead. She is not secretly sabotaging her runner. She wants the runner to win and wants the event to keep the yard open, which makes stopping it harder than knowing a limit exists.

Vexa takes Glasswake before the event because she wants to feel sharper and stay ahead of exhaustion. This is the same fictional illicit stimulant named in Eno's earlier account; its composition remains unknown and no use instruction is supplied. Its presence does not establish a single cause for Vexa's later injury. She starts, wins the recorded heat and carries an injured leg through the finish rather than withdrawing when it becomes painful.

The win is genuine under the event's recorded conditions. Milo keeps it on the board and records the injury afterward. Kesh sponsors the winning image for Night Cover publicity. The runner looks fearless beside a service being sold to frightened carriers. Vexa enjoys the photograph before she learns which receiving room has burned. She cannot honestly claim she had hated every part of the sponsorship from the first day.

The winner’s debt and the audience that bought too much

Vexa had accepted an advance against further appearances. With her leg injured, she cannot perform the next agreed contest. The sponsor wants the unearned portion returned; the appearance claimant who expected a rise wants the event repeated. Her actual win does not deliver every imagined future on which other adults placed their money. 'You lost a bet,' she tells an angry visitor. 'You didn't buy my fucking body.'

Etta had bought a small speculative appearance claim before the burn and without telling Sella. She hoped Vexa's next result would restore her own thin reserve. The hoped-for return does not arrive. Vexa is not responsible for Rovan's attack or every buyer's decision to gamble, but she did speak in a promotional interval as if continuing appearances were dependable. Jessa wants that line corrected while still advertising the completed win. The narrower account pleases nobody expecting it to bring the money back.

Vexa's adult partner Neri Bask, 33, helps with ordinary work at home and is tired of being asked whether she is managing the champion well. They remain attracted to one another; recovery does not obligate either to perform constant reassurance through sex. Vexa sometimes mistakes Neri's tiredness for rejection and makes a cruel remark about finding someone with ambition. Neri sleeps elsewhere for two nights and returns to talk after Vexa apologises without blaming the leg.

Their argument does not end the debt. Vexa wants a useful job and hates the phrase comeback inspiration. Jessa finds a paid instruction interval she can undertake seated, then a sponsor asks for the injured runner smiling beside the old finish image. Vexa permits an image of the actual instruction and refuses the recovery claim. The offer is reduced. She takes it anyway and asks that the reduced fee be written down.

Milo keeps the win and loses the easy story

After the killing account reaches the yard, Night Cover wants its mark kept beside Vexa's result because it paid for the event. Milo retains the completed sponsorship entry as history and removes the mark from the next planned promotion after the separate agreement is ended. The old board still records who paid. Erasing the patron from the first night would make the yard look more independent than it was.

Jessa admits she accepted a start she would have discouraged without the fee pressure. Vexa says she would probably have started elsewhere. Both may be right. Neither statement makes the injured leg go away or establishes that every athlete who uses a stimulant will share her outcome. The account follows these people's choice, strain and consequences, not a clinical theory or faction-wide bodily rule.

At an Unranked Heat later, Vexa watches rather than competes. She recognises a tactical mistake, shouts advice and gets told to let the participant make it. She laughs, then spends the rest of the interval wishing she were on the course. Giving instruction is real work; wanting to run is a desire she cannot pay off through gratitude. The yard lets the awkward evening be awkward.

The Afterimage Board now holds a win, the original sponsor, an injury note, a cancelled appearance and a changed publicity arrangement. There is no settled comeback result. Bull ambition remains thrilling to the people who feel it and costly to the bodies, partners and households expected to keep it bright. The next loud evening begins while the winner from this one still owes money.
