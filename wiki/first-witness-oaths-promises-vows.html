Oaths, Promises and Vows

First WitnessLived tradition · Section 30Last updated: October 2026

The First Witness distrusts grand oaths because grand language can hide vague obligations. Its strongest vows are therefore specific, witnessed and breakable under defined conditions.

The Keeper's Oath

I will not confuse custody with ownership. I will record what I receive, what I change and what I pass on. I will prepare another hand before mine is gone.

This oath is used by archivists, maintainers, treasurers and sometimes parents taking responsibility for a shared estate. It is deliberately about process rather than personal purity.

The Witness Oath

I will say what I saw, not what my side needs me to have seen. I will mark uncertainty. I will correct myself without pretending I was never wrong.

Courts, dispute circles and Chain Scribe archives use variations of this. Some factions mock it as naive. Others consider it the minimum condition for testimony.

The HODL Oath

I will hold what must survive us. I will not turn emergency unity into permanent command. I will pass the weight when another hand is ready.

Later HODL Warrior versions add faction-specific clauses. The anti-command line is retained in orthodox versions precisely because HODL unity is powerful enough to become dangerous.

The Vow Against the Crown

Some decentralist schools require leaders to state publicly that no title makes their interpretation of the Covenant final. The vow became associated with Child of Fire sceptics and anti-messianic traditions.

History records several leaders taking the vow while behaving like monarchs. The contradiction became a teaching point: an anti-crown oath can itself become ceremonial camouflage for a crown.

Promises that may be broken

The tradition rejects the idea that every promise must be kept regardless of how it was obtained. A vow made under threat, fraud or deliberate concealment may be void. A promise requiring abuse may be refused. A pledge to hide a serious crime does not become holy because somebody said “swear.”

This rule is controversial because people can also invent coercion after regret. The practical requirement is evidence where possible and public reasons where safe.

The smallest promise

One teaching says a person's character is revealed less by spectacular vows than by small commitments nobody applauds: returning the borrowed tool, paying on the agreed day, arriving when you said you would, admitting when you cannot.

“Do not swear the sky when you cannot manage Tuesday” became a popular street version.
