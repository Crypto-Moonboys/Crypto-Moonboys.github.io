Songs, Chants and Call-and-Response

First WitnessOral tradition · Section 43Last updated: October 2026

The Covenant was written to survive quotation. Music and call-and-response made it survive people who never read the whole text.

The Keeper Response

Caller: Who owns the thing entrusted?

Response: No hand owns the future.

Caller: What proves the keeper?

Response: What survives the keeping.

The Witness Response

Caller: What did you see?

Response: Only what I can answer for.

Caller: What do you not know?

Response: I will not fill the gap with fear.

The Wall Response

Caller: Who gets the last wall?

Response: No generation.

Caller: Who gets remembered?

Response: Whoever we refuse to erase.

The HODL Response

Caller: What do we hold?

Response: What must survive us.

Caller: For how long?

Response: Until another hand can carry it.

The Crownless Response

Caller: Who speaks the final word?

Response: Not the living for the unborn.

Caller: Who carries the fire?

Response: Whoever refuses the crown.

Street versions

Street crews shorten the responses until they become rhythm rather than teaching. “WHO HOLDS?” / “WE HOLD.” “WHO OWNS?” / “NO ONE.” “WALL?” / “KNOWS.”

Orthodox teachers complain the short versions lose nuance. Street teachers reply that nobody chants a footnote during a raid.

The danger of chanting

Book VIII schools warn that communal rhythm can make weak ideas feel certain. Several gatherings therefore include one deliberate broken response where the crowd is expected to remain silent.

The silence is meant to remind participants that unity should never eliminate the ability not to answer.

A chorus from the back of the room

A Backlight street refrain asks, “Who stole the good seat?” The room answers, “Whoever got here first.” A second voice adds, “Who saved the last plate?” and receives whichever answer the dancers feel like giving. The refrain is newly authored local performance, not a lost line of the Covenant. It is popular because it lets a late arrival interrupt a song without having to make an important speech.

Lio's polished version lands the questions precisely on the beat. A Ducky visitor drags the answer until the next question nearly collides with it. Low Tide regulars prefer a pause in which somebody can shout an unrelated grievance about the food. Their versions are recognisable and not interchangeable. A visiting teacher once tried to turn the song into a lesson about priority; the next performance included a verse asking who had invited the teacher.

A quieter kitchen tune lists a pot, a spoon, a spare bowl and the person expected home. Each singer changes the final name. The tune can wait for a lover, a sibling or nobody in particular. Later memorial performances sometimes omit the name and keep the rhythm. That use does not mean every kitchen performance began in mourning, or that a song becomes more authentic when someone can attach a tragedy to it.

Who can change the words

Street songs travel through misheard words, unsuitable rooms and singers more interested in flirtation than textual fidelity. A changed line can reveal a new grievance or merely improve a rhyme. The Witness responses remain identified separately when a gathering quotes them, allowing the informal chorus to change without quietly rewriting the source.

Some Crownless teachers use the Blue Stair story to explain the deliberate broken response. The point is not that Eda Senn's recording was false. It became compulsory. At Leth Orr's later classes, silence after a question can mean doubt, fatigue, refusal or a learner still finding words. A teacher who supplies the answer to restore a satisfying rhythm may prevent the very testimony the session was meant to hear.

This leaves room for enthusiasm. A crowd can sing confidently, love a familiar voice and make a deafening entrance without appointing a sovereign. The question is what happens to the person who stops singing. At Backlight the usual answer is that someone offers water, teases them or asks for their seat. A ritual stays human when refusal does not automatically become accusation.

Sources and the later commentary
