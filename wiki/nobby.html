An apprentice with his own key

Nobby, forty-five, grew up behind a repair shed where people kept their coats on indoors. His mother re-soled dock boots and taught him to trace the wear on each sole before she cut it away. His father came home with new schemes and unfinished work. From the pair of them Nobby acquired a distrust of promised deadlines and a weakness for starting difficult jobs just to find out whether they could be done. A foreman called him Nobby after he used a wooden drawer knob in place of a broken handle. The replacement outlasted the foreman’s employment.

He went into municipal work for the paid training, the showers and the first regular wage anyone in his house had seen. He liked being taught by people better than him. He grew tired of being asked to keep a failing rule alive because the responsible office had already reported its success. He left after an inspection team kept a service room locked through a rain warning because the only licensed keyholder was on holiday. At the time he thought quitting settled his part in the decision. He learned a year later how much unpaid work the woman covering his shift had taken on when he walked.

A place in the Mosh Pit

The Hard Fork Rockers trace a strong strand of their origins to rebellion within Queen Sarah’s HARD FORK Program. The faction’s Mosh Pit Networks give dissenters room to try a different arrangement while the old one is still in force. Nobby first met a crew through a late-night repair exchange above a rehearsal room. He went in to borrow a tool and came out three hours later with somebody else’s amp under his arm and a job on Saturday. He liked that nobody asked whether he had finished a formal apprenticeship. There were plenty of other questions about the work.

His first public demonstration involved two exit lamps running side by side through a mock power cut. One belonged to the standard installation. The Rockers had built the other to be serviceable by the people who used the room. Their lamp failed first. Nobby made the crowd sit in the dark while he explained why. A cleaner offered him an extension cord. He has spent years trying to live up to the lesson: publish what went wrong and ask the person who actually needs the thing.

Who pays for the better version

During the double tide, Nobby and ZARAH8 got a reserve lamp and lifting gear to the upper landing. Their arguments about who should control reserve equipment were still unresolved. He wanted two custodians on separate shifts. She wanted somebody identifiable to answer for damage. Jimmy Rugpull asked which of them had money set aside for a call-out. That settled the immediate argument more effectively than either manifesto.

Nobby still had a dry workshop. He lent it as a sorting room and spent two days working in boots full of water because he kept giving visitors the chair nearest the radiator. When a Rocker crew proposed stripping out every municipal fitting, he made them list what people could no longer use that evening if they went ahead. They kept the sound fittings, replaced the faulty ones and argued about the keys in daylight. He drew criticism from both sides. A younger rocker called him the council with better music. The line made him laugh in the pub. It kept him awake at home.

The Thursday noise

Nobby keeps a shelf of damaged guitar pedals, shelves labeled by the day he will supposedly get round to them. He plays bass in a three-piece that has rehearsed on Thursdays for eleven years and booked two actual gigs. Teddy Boi Jones offered to record them if they would agree to be in the same room at a time Teddy could put in a calendar. The negotiations are ongoing. Nobby polishes tools when he has bad news and makes toast when he has worse news. A compliment from him arrives as permission to use his good screwdriver.

He treats Chada Boi as a first-rate listener and a public hazard around drawers. He respects Jimmy’s reading of contracts, even after discovering that Jimmy once charged the same group twice for unloading a stove. He goes swimming on quiet mornings because the water prevents requests for free repairs. His best friend from his municipal years still sends him birthday cards with no return address. Nobby has no idea whether this is a rebuke or tradecraft.

The second pair of hands

Nobby’s claim to the Ninth Berth is a repair bench and the right to argue about its use. He teaches a person to take over before he leaves a job. When the learner challenges his method he listens for a short while, then talks over them and has to catch himself. He wants a workshop where residents can fix an ordinary pump and younger cutters can tell him to go home without losing a wage. He is saving for the first year of rent and has told everybody he is happy to wait. In private he is tired of waiting.

“Could work. Who fixes it on Sunday?” is his most common verdict. The harder question for him is who gets to say the old thing is worth keeping. He wants every door to have an independent way out. His mother wants him to stop checking the latch every time he visits. Both can make a good argument.

A fork that earned its rent

The Rocker Mosh Pit demonstration Nobby talks about least was a pay dispute. One cell had opened an alternative repair room with a noble rule allowing anyone to volunteer. Its regular visitors began to assume they could leave a broken appliance and collect it later. A woman named Den took on all the jobs that nobody fancied. After six weeks she walked out with a coat, a broken lamp and no money for a bus.

Nobby’s first reaction was to draft another charter. Den suggested he open the cash box and tell her how much was there. They worked out a paid three-hour session with a set list of jobs. The waiting list lengthened. The people doing the work came back. Nobby spent a season taking abuse from a man who wanted his free kettle fixed on Tuesdays. On a bad day Nobby still thinks independence should feel more gratifying than getting a budget to balance.
