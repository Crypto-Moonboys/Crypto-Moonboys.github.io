⚔️ The CODE ALCHEMISTS

⚔️ Lore 📅 Last updated: October 2026 📂 Lore

The Code Alchemists are Year 3008 specialists in damaged memory and unstable records. Their craft can recover meaning or impose a convincing falsehood, making every commission a dispute about evidence, consent, payment and the limits of what should be changed.

Adult lore — 21+: These fictional accounts include strong language, adult relationships, intoxication, coercion, violence and lasting loss.

Repairers at the edge of alteration

The Code Alchemists are specialised technicians of the Year 3008 world whose work sits between recovery, interpretation and transformation. They handle damaged fragments, unstable records and systems that no longer agree with their own histories. Their reputation for sorcery arises from the gap between an apparently hopeless problem and a result a customer can suddenly understand.

The dedicated Technical Magicians of the Grid account makes memory alteration central to their identity. It portrays insular experts who sell rare services while guarding the knowledge that makes those services valuable. The First Witness commentary gives their central danger a moral form: the ability to rewrite something does not establish the right to rewrite it.

They are an independent faction within the forty-faction civilisation. Neither independence nor technical skill makes them free of political choices. A repair can preserve a household's history, restore a powerful institution's control or make a false account easier to believe. The Alchemists cannot escape those consequences merely by calling the result a successful intervention.

Ixa Vell, the Velvet Surgeon

Ixa Vell, 46, is an Alchemist whose private commissions travel by recommendation between wealthy adult households. Clients call her the Velvet Surgeon because she makes an invasive request sound like a discreet luxury. She wears pale gloves stained at one fingertip and declines to say whether the stain is a mistake or a signature. Copies of the mark appear at gatherings where nobody would admit to having employed her. Her reputation is a combination of real craft, cultivated mystery and customers who need to believe the work was worth its price.

Her promises concern bounded fictional memory work: shaping supported impressions, altering particular records and influencing what a client subsequently accepts. She cannot establish universal control over a mind, revive the lost or settle the Genesis Kernel's ontology. Those limits do not make the work harmless. An altered recollection can put a person at odds with a partner whose account is intact. A changed record can make the untouched witness look dishonest. The strongest service she sells is a convenient explanation that arrives wearing the authority of specialist care.

Orris Veil's Interval Room still distinguishes a requested repair from a demanded alteration. Ixa mocks that interval as a theatre for clients who cannot afford certainty. Their rivalry is substantive. Both possess difficult skills; one leaves awkward differences visible, while the other earns much more by reducing the difference a paying patron finds inconvenient. The faction's unique power is the ability to change the material through which people recognise a past. The question becomes urgent when somebody likes the changed version better.

Origins in a world that could break

Their dedicated origin account begins with the shock of the World Chain's Triple Fork in 2880. What had been treated as fixed became violently contradictory, and the Alchemists concluded that what could be broken could also be changed. The account describes consciousnesses inherited from esoteric engineers and speculative physicists. That remains a faction origin tradition, not a settled genealogy or proof of a universal transfer of human minds.

The Genesis Kernel's nature and exact chronology remain unresolved. Alchemist writings variously treat it as an original structure to repair, a source to transform or part of a larger cosmological mechanism. Their confidence does not resolve the contradictions between those accounts. A description attributed to radical Alchemists is evidence of a school of thought, not automatic access to the world's final architecture.

The Great Concord produced the Covenant in 2030; the later Sacred/Aether-Chain memory tradition has an open transmission date. The collapsed World Chain and Block Topia's True Bitcoin Fork are distinct systems. By 2930 the Queens stronghold was among fewer than a dozen secure regions, later becoming the city's basis. Technical metaphors must not silently merge these histories into a single chain.

The language of alchemy

Alchemist workshops use the language of purification, binding, distillation and transmutation to describe changes that outsiders find difficult to follow. A ritual can organise attention, slow a rushed decision or preserve the sequence of a delicate piece of work. It can also make an ordinary uncertainty look like a secret that only an initiate deserves to question.

The dedicated account names Quantum Patchers and Chrono-Stabilizers. Within the fiction, these are traditions of stabilising damaged logic and comparing conflicting temporal records. Their names do not establish limitless repair, control over time or proof that a technician can choose which history truly happened. The difference between aligning a display and establishing an event remains crucial.

Older stories speak of Ether Ward, Data Wither, the Fork of Shadows, the Crucible of Code, the Philosopher's Shard and the Codex of Ether. Those enchantment and relic traditions survive as accounts of feared power. Their inventories, locations and effects are not all confirmed. A story about an unbreakable shield cannot make every later customer safe.

Work in the Interval Room

The Interval Room is a small Alchemist workshop serving clients whose records have become difficult to use. Its name refers to the pause between receiving a request and deciding what work is actually being requested. Customers often arrive asking for restoration and discover they disagree about what the restored result should be.

Orris Veil is a technician there. Orris is skilled at making damaged sequences readable and less skilled at explaining why that success can leave an important question unresolved. A client sees a recognisable voice or image and understandably hopes the missing person has returned. Orris must then describe the limits before the workshop's achievement becomes another source of grief.

Tali Splice keeps the room usable, prepares ordinary materials and checks how proposed work will affect the remaining schedule. Tali is also the person most likely to notice that a client has agreed to a term without understanding it. The role carries no power to solve every dispute. It does create a pause in which someone other than the most confident expert can speak.

The Four Versions case

The Four Versions case concerned a damaged farewell message brought to the Interval Room by two members of a household. Several possible completions fit the surviving fragment. One sounded forgiving, another accusatory, and two left the central sentence unfinished in different ways. The surviving material did not justify selecting the emotionally most satisfying version as the lost original.

Orris initially presented the smoothest completion first. One client recognised the voice and wept; the other heard a phrase the missing speaker would never have used. The disagreement revealed that the presentation itself had given a possible version more authority than it deserved. Tali stopped the review long enough for Orris to explain how much had been supplied by interpretation.

The household chose to keep the surviving fragment and the alternatives separately. They paid for the work already agreed but declined further completion. Orris recorded the misleading first presentation as a workshop error. No one recovered a final answer. The result preserved something valuable precisely because the workshop accepted that an incomplete farewell could be more faithful than a convincing one.

The Kept Fault collection

Kept Fault is a small teaching collection at the Interval Room. It contains unsuccessful interpretations, repairs that did not hold and decisions that were reconsidered before work began. Each entry explains enough of the circumstances to make the limit intelligible without exposing a client's private material. It is not a public display of other people's distress.

Apprentices are asked what a successful-looking result might conceal. A clear image can omit context; a consistent timeline can have erased a disagreement; a polished voice can feel authentic because a listener badly wants it to be. The exercise does not teach that every restoration is fraudulent. It teaches that appearance and evidence answer different questions.

Orris once wanted to remove an early failure after finding a better technique. Tali argued that the old entry still showed why the workshop had believed the weaker result. They kept both. Improvement becomes harder to assess when every trace of the earlier confidence disappears. The collection also reminds skilled workers that being able to explain a mistake is part of competence.

Memory belongs to more than a commission

The archive's soft-forking tradition concerns local variants of memory or records. It has restorative uses and frightening coercive ones. The Alchemists' ability to imagine an alteration does not make a commissioner entitled to order it, especially when the subject is somebody else. A patron's ownership of equipment is not the same as a person's consent to changes affecting identity.

At the Interval Room, Tali asks who will live with the result after the payer leaves. That question can complicate an apparently simple job. A family may disagree; a subject may be unable to communicate clearly; a powerful employer may describe a worker's refusal as evidence of damage. The workshop cannot settle personhood by accepting whichever account comes with the payment.

Current Kernel history distinguishes trace, fragment, interpretation and reconstruction. Those terms give Orris a way to describe work without claiming resurrection. The First Witness reading on synthetic minds leaves important questions of continuity and moral standing open. The Alchemists operate inside that uncertainty. A convincing imitation is not by itself permission to erase, replace or possess someone.

The price of specialised work

Alchemist work consumes time, power, storage, replacement components and attention that cannot be supplied indefinitely. A difficult commission may support the workshop for weeks and prevent several smaller requests from being considered. The language of rare knowledge can hide this ordinary scarcity behind a more flattering image of unlimited mastery.

The dedicated account describes high-paying High Hat clients seeking purified asset records and exclusive protection. The Finance Guild seeks Chrono-Stabilizers that will preserve obligations through conflicting eras. Such patrons can fund useful research, but their idea of restoration may favour their own authority. A perfectly legible debt is not proof that the debtor consented or that the terms were just.

Orris once refused an offered premium for describing an uncertain result as definitive. The refusal delayed replacement equipment and reduced the workshop's available work. Tali did not romanticise the loss; colleagues had to absorb it. A principle can be necessary and still impose a cost on people who were not consulted when the noble sentence was spoken.

Disputes with the Chain Scribes

The Chain Scribes fear memory alteration because it can dissolve the distinction between evidence and invention. The Alchemists answer that refusing all intervention can leave damaged records unusable. Their strongest disagreement concerns which risks matter most: the loss of what cannot be read, or the confidence produced by a repair that has changed its subject.

The Scribe account condemns Quantum Patchers as tools of sacrilege. The Alchemist account describes scrutiny from the GKniftyHEADS and Blockchain Furies as intrusive and threatening. Neither school gets to settle the matter by naming the other unclean. Evidence of a careful repair deserves inspection; so does evidence that a technician has exceeded the agreed work.

A local working relationship can preserve an original fragment beside a labelled reconstruction and a statement of the changes. The existing Open Margin tradition makes room for challenge without treating every objection as sabotage. It does not guarantee consensus. Sometimes a Scribe declines to certify a work the Alchemist still considers useful, and the client must understand both positions.

The Saints, Ducky Boys and practical repair

The OG Pixel Saints hire Alchemists to repair treasured cultural fragments in their dedicated account. The Saints' language of purification can turn a skilled worker into a priest and a repair failure into an insult to the object itself. The Interval Room tries to keep the admiration from consuming the explanation: a restored image may be meaningful even where it remains partly reconstructed.

The Ducky Boys approach many problems through everyday function. The Alchemists sometimes dismiss that pragmatism as inelegant, while practical repairers resent a commission that becomes an endless demonstration of theoretical brilliance. Both require the materials supplied by the Salvagers. A subtle interpretation still needs a functioning place in which to be examined.

These relationships make the faction more than a collection of isolated geniuses. An apprentice learns from a carrier describing how a damaged object travelled, or from a performer showing which part of an image the audience recognises. Technical authority weakens when it refuses information because the person carrying it lacks the correct title.

The EVM Punks and the limits of neutrality

The EVM Punks' dedicated account describes a professional rivalry with the Alchemists. Punks favour decisive, efficient outcomes; Alchemists accuse them of crude destruction. Punks answer that elaborate transformations can conceal a failure to finish. Both are tempted to confuse the feature of their work they admire most with the thing every client actually needs.

The archive also places Alchemists in contested services for elites and in grand rebel war narratives. Those differences should remain visible. A faction that sells specialised work to several sides is not automatically above the war. A result can release a trapped memory or strengthen the authority that kept it trapped, depending on the commission.

The Interval Room's local limits are therefore a choice, not proof that every Alchemist follows them. Other workshops accept invasive work or guard their methods so tightly that no customer can meaningfully challenge an outcome. Their existence matters to the faction's reputation. A careful practitioner does not abolish the harm done under the same name.

The rebel anomalies and a dangerous curiosity

The dedicated account says the Alchemists see the escaped Bitcoin Kids' anomalies as valuable subjects for research. The Forkborn account similarly portrays Jodie ZOOM 2000's unusual abilities as something they want to dissect. These are accounts of predatory curiosity, not evidence that researchers possess a right to anyone's mind or a complete explanation of what the anomalies mean.

Alfie's Bitcoin Kids remain the escaped outside lineage. The Bitcoin X Kids are the distinct engineered generation inside Block Topia. A general label such as research subject must not collapse their histories. Nor does a person accepting one form of help consent to every future use of what a technician learns.

Tali's hardest refusals occur when a benefactor offers assistance conditional on access to a person's private experience. The offer can look generous because the unmet need is real. The faction's ethical question is not whether knowledge has value; it is whether that value lets the knowledgeable write terms that a vulnerable person cannot reasonably refuse.

The Great Consensus and the attraction of a final theory

Radical Alchemist transmissions offer sweeping accounts of an alien backend, SoulTags and a mathematically structured reality. The Great Consensus manifesto predicts a future synchronisation associated with 3030. These are named cosmological and prophetic traditions. Their elaborate equations do not establish real physics or fix the date and outcome of the unresolved Final Fork.

The accounts matter because a total theory can make disparate injuries appear parts of one repairable fault. To someone exhausted by repeated local failures, that promise is powerful. It can also encourage a technician to treat a person who disagrees as an error to eliminate. The First Witness Unwritten Block resists the claim that one generation can complete the meaning of the world.

Orris reads the manifesto and finds some of its images useful. Tali keeps returning to the Four Versions case: if the workshop could not responsibly complete one farewell, it should hesitate before promising a perfectly completed civilisation. That objection does not end speculative thought. It places the burden of explanation back on the people making the largest promise.

NULL and the temptation to weaponise absence

The dedicated Alchemist account treats NULL as a law of inverse creation and describes an ambition to turn its effects into permanent memory deletion. Other traditions describe an infection, an intelligence or a cult. The faction's preferred explanation is not the settled ontology of the antichain, and fascination does not demonstrate control.

The desire for an ultimate erasure tool reveals the dark edge of their craft. A repairer who can imagine removing pain may begin to imagine removing dissent, inconvenient evidence or the person carrying it. None of those outcomes follows inevitably from study, but the archive gives the fear a clear foundation. The Alchemists must answer for the purposes of their work as well as its technical results.

The Final Fork remains open. At the Interval Room, the most useful unfinished project is a collection of limits another worker can understand. It offers no immunity or final victory. It preserves the possibility that the next technician can stop sooner, ask a better question or leave a valuable fragment incomplete.

Fictional technology and present-day context

The Code Alchemists are a fictional faction within the wider Crypto Moonboys creator universe. Their quantum tools, memory rites, protective enchantments and cosmological accounts belong to that fiction. They do not describe a present service capable of rewriting minds, creating guaranteed financial returns or conferring rights over another person's work. The older present-day WAX transmutation rhetoric remains part of the archive's mixed promotional and mythic material.

The purchased farewell and the sister who remembers

A patron called Eron Silt, 54, commissions Ixa after his adult partner leaves their shared household. He wants the preserved farewell to show mutual kindness and a considered separation. The existing account contains an accusation that he repeatedly used his influence to damage her work. Ixa modifies a limited commissioned presentation and supports it with an altered memory impression accepted by Eron. She does not rewrite every independent copy or magically control the departing partner. His new confidence nevertheless changes how he speaks to everyone who asks.

The former partner, Vey Lark, 45, retains her account and refuses his improved farewell. Her sister Asha, 41, has an earlier copy that also contradicts him. Eron offers Asha a paid introduction, then calls her ungrateful when she keeps showing it. Other guests prefer his calmer version because it lets them continue liking him. Ixa's work supplies a social advantage before any archivist can resolve the disagreement. Vey starts losing invitations that described her refusal as hostility.

An Alchemist assistant leaks the fact of the commission, without possessing every private detail. Ixa calls the leak a betrayal of confidence. Some patrons believe her and book her precisely because she defended the paying client's privacy. Orris refuses a later request to certify the modified presentation as a complete restoration. He can identify its bounded alterations; he cannot make Eron's friends stop preferring it. Vey moves her working supplies out through a neighbour and keeps a relationship with Asha at the cost of repeated arguments about how publicly to fight.

Ixa retains fees and gains a notoriety that attracts new clients. Eron retains influence, a comforting impression and a diminished ability to understand why Vey left. Vey retains her account and loses access she previously took for granted. The Alchemists' horror is intimate: the inconvenience of a person can become a technical problem for somebody rich enough to commission a solution. This local abuse does not prove any faction's absolute theory of NULL. It proves that a useful craft has a profitable cruel use.

The room of borrowed grief

Ixa Vell demonstrates commissioned impressions in a small presentation room where supported fragments appear as reflections across dark panels. Adult clients can hear a voice associated with one recollection while seeing an image carried by another. The effect depends on prepared material and particular fictional equipment; it is not a universal view into anyone's mind. Its glamour is immediate: grief can look curated, and somebody's awful memory can seem like a room an expert knows how to redecorate.

Vey Lark sees the modified farewell there and recognises enough unchanged detail to feel her stomach turn. Eron hears the version he has paid to accept and calls it a kinder truth. Ixa adjusts the presentation, asks whether they would prefer less light and receives no answer that makes the room comfortable. The horrifying thing is not a complete magical replacement. It is a recognisable piece of private life displayed beautifully enough for one person to praise the alteration that diminishes another.

The Original Face salon

An Alchemist salon in the earlier 3008 season offered patrons a private presentation of an image they had stopped using. The commission required material the client supplied; it did not extract a hidden original self. Ixa invited Eron to view a supported rendering of an older portrait beside his current public image. He disliked the older one's anxious expression and asked whether it could be improved. She asked whether he wanted the person remembered or the portrait made more useful.

Another adult guest, Neris Fold, 52, paid to preserve a former partner's ugly laugh exactly as a surviving recording carried it. Ixa considered the result aesthetically crude. Neris wanted the crudeness. Their disagreement made the room interesting enough that later guests asked to hear the disputed laugh. Neris refused permission. The salon retained its reputation for the recording it could no longer exhibit, and Ixa found the refusal irritating because scarcity had improved the legend without improving her access to it.

Clients began calling the gatherings Original Face evenings even when the material was a voice, a room or a flawed surviving sequence. The title promised a purity the practice could not prove. Younger practitioners copied the glamorous name; patient restorers called it a marketing triumph over the word 'original'. The older laugh remains kept elsewhere. Its story has become a thing people can buy an evening discussing.

The taste for alteration

Alchemist circles cultivate a disturbing intimacy with choice. They ask which version of a farewell a client can bear, which blemish belongs in a preserved image and why a beautiful result should be trusted more than an ugly one. Their parties use mismatched reflections and recollections volunteered by adult guests. Some pairs revisit an earlier courtship together and enjoy disagreeing about it. A shared presentation is a performance of their relationship, not proof that they now possess the same memory.

Ixa enjoys clients who describe vanity as refinement. A maker called Jorra Splice, 33, instead wears one conspicuous failed alteration in her public work because it reminds viewers that expertise leaves traces. The two admire each other's skill and dislike each other's favourite customers. Jorra says the Velvet Surgeon sells innocence to people who can afford better lighting. Ixa replies that her critic sells the appearance of honesty to people who can afford to enjoy a defect.

Their rivalry produces extraordinary work and thoroughly nasty openings. Neither argument restores Vey's invitations. A craft capable of making damaged fragments legible also attracts patrons who want their damage displayed as profundity. The faction's essential difference from the Scribes is its willingness to change a presentation in order to create a new experience. The frightening question in an Alchemist room is who will be invited to enjoy that experience and who will recognise their own life inside it without being asked.

The doctrine beyond the equation

The Great Consensus laws and initiations explain why the future theory recruits people who are not engineers. A promised end to bereavement and doubt can be worth more to a customer than an honest account of a repair’s limits. Orris and Tali’s objections remain craft arguments; the Cold Choir’s fuel dispute reveals what the promise can buy for an institution before any synchronization has occurred.

The neutrality that protects a monopoly

Technical Magicians of the Grid claims absolute neutrality in order to preserve the Alchemists’ monopoly on specialised work. The source’s services include purifying High Hat asset portfolios and supplying exclusive digital bodyguards to city elites. Its Quantum Patchers stabilise corrupted logic through probabilistic mathematics; Chrono-Stabilizers temporarily align conflicting timestamps. A stable display or protected record can be valuable without proving that the resulting claim is innocent.

The monopoly’s secrecy also gives the GKniftyHEADS and Furies a specific demand: inspect the work and the client history behind it. The source calls that scrutiny threatening. A client can have a legitimate privacy interest while the workshop uses privacy to hide an abusive commission. Ixa’s paid farewell and the contradictory account Vey and Asha keep already make that distinction personal. Some patrons employ the Velvet Surgeon precisely because she defends the paying person’s version.

The Interval Room’s refusals remain a different choice within the same faction. Orris and Tali keep uncertainty visible and lose profitable certainty in consequence; they do not prove that the source monopoly has ceased to exist. Nor does a limited fictional repair grant the Alchemists universal mental control. What survives is the darker commercial possibility: an expert can avoid fighting for a ruler while selling the ruler a quieter means of making another person easier to dismiss.
