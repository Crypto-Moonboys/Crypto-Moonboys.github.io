Birth, Naming and Coming of Age

First WitnessRites of passage · Section 54Last updated: October 2026

The First Witness does not claim a child at birth. It treats childhood as protected development and adulthood as the gradual assumption of responsibility.

The First Name

Naming ceremonies vary widely because older religions and family customs remain intact. The First Witness addition is usually a simple record: who chose the name, what it meant at the time, and whether the child may later amend how they are known.

The point is not to make identity infinitely fluid. It is to admit that a name given before a person can speak is a gift, not a permanent command.

The First Responsibility

Children are gradually given custody of something real: a tool, plant, archive folder, pet-care task, shared budget item or communal duty. The object matters less than the fact that responsibility has consequences beyond the child.

A staged failure is not considered shameful. Adults who never allow a child to fail safely often produce adults who first encounter failure when the stakes are much higher.

The Question Year

Many communities mark adolescence with a period in which young people are explicitly encouraged to challenge family beliefs, faction customs and First Witness interpretation. The practice grew from the Quiet Question taught to children.

Adults frequently discover they liked the idea better in theory than in practice.

The Carrying

A common coming-of-age exercise asks the young person to name three things they intend to carry from their inheritance, one thing they intend to change and one thing they are not yet sure about.

The uncertainty is mandatory in some schools. A person who claims complete certainty about their future inheritance is considered not yet ready to govern it.

Adult status

There is no single universal age in the First Witness tradition. Legal adulthood follows local law. Ritual adulthood follows community practice. Moral adulthood is treated as an ongoing capacity for responsibility rather than a birthday transformation.

The anti-initiation rule

Rites that require serious injury, sexual coercion, irreversible mutilation or criminal acts as proof of belonging are rejected by most mainstream First Witness schools. Tradition does not erase consent.

Several factions preserve dangerous initiations anyway and defend them as ancient. The Concordance treats the antiquity claims separately from the ethical question.
