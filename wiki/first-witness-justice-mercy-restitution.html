Justice, Mercy and Restitution

First WitnessEthics and civic practice · Section 31Last updated: October 2026

The Covenant rejects both easy vengeance and cheap forgiveness. Justice must protect people, record wrongdoing and repair what can actually be repaired.

Why punishment is not enough

A person can be punished while the victim remains uncompensated, the damage remains unrepaired and the system that enabled the harm remains untouched. First Witness courts therefore distinguish punishment from restitution.

If somebody steals tools, a sentence does not magically replace the tools. If a company lies, a fine paid to an authority may do nothing for the people who relied on the lie.

The four questions

A common justice practice asks: What happened? Who was harmed? What can be repaired? What must change so the harm is harder to repeat?

A fifth question—“What does the offender need?”—is controversial but important. The tradition argues that preventing repetition sometimes requires treatment, education, work or reintegration rather than humiliation.

Mercy without denial

Mercy does not require calling the harm small. The strongest form of mercy in First Witness teaching occurs after the record is clear, not instead of making the record clear.

This is why forced public forgiveness rituals are condemned in most schools. A victim can decline revenge without being required to restore trust.

Restitution and impossible repair

Some harms cannot be restored. A dead person cannot be returned. Lost years cannot be repaid exactly. A public lie can continue travelling after correction.

In those cases restitution becomes asymmetrical: care for dependants, public correction, preservation of the true record, surrender of unjust gain, service or restrictions designed to prevent recurrence.

The problem of public shame

Street cultures can punish through reputation faster than any court. The Covenant treats reputation as useful evidence but dangerous sentencing. An accusation can travel farther than the later correction.

One later rule says: if you amplified the accusation, you inherit some duty to amplify the correction. This became especially important in network-era dispute ethics.

When protection comes first

Restorative language is not used to force contact with dangerous people. Immediate protection can require separation, restraint or exclusion. The right of an offender to change does not create an obligation for a victim to become the place where that change is tested.

Mercy without boundaries can become another way of handing the cost of reform back to the harmed person.
