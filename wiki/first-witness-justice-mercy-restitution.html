Justice, Mercy and Restitution

First WitnessEthics and civic practice · Section 31Last updated: October 2026

The Covenant rejects both easy vengeance and cheap forgiveness. Justice must protect people, record wrongdoing and repair what can actually be repaired.

Why punishment is not enough

A person can be punished while the victim remains uncompensated, the damage remains unrepaired and the system that enabled the harm remains untouched. First Witness courts therefore distinguish punishment from restitution.

If somebody steals tools, a sentence does not magically replace the tools. If a company lies, a fine paid to an authority may do nothing for the people who relied on the lie.

The four questions

A common justice practice asks: What happened? Who was harmed? What can be repaired? What must change so the harm is harder to repeat?

A fifth question—“What does the offender need?”—is controversial but important. The tradition argues that preventing repetition sometimes requires treatment, education, work or reintegration rather than humiliation.

Mercy without denial

Mercy does not require calling the harm small. The strongest form of mercy in First Witness teaching occurs after the record is clear, not instead of making the record clear.

This is why forced public forgiveness rituals are condemned in most schools. A victim can decline revenge without being required to restore trust.

Restitution and impossible repair

Some harms cannot be restored. A dead person cannot be returned. Lost years cannot be repaid exactly. A public lie can continue travelling after correction.

In those cases restitution becomes asymmetrical: care for dependants, public correction, preservation of the true record, surrender of unjust gain, service or restrictions designed to prevent recurrence.

The problem of public shame

Street cultures can punish through reputation faster than any court. The Covenant treats reputation as useful evidence but dangerous sentencing. An accusation can travel farther than the later correction.

One later rule says: if you amplified the accusation, you inherit some duty to amplify the correction. This became especially important in network-era dispute ethics.

When protection comes first

Restorative language is not used to force contact with dangerous people. Immediate protection can require separation, restraint or exclusion. The right of an offender to change does not create an obligation for a victim to become the place where that change is tested.

Mercy without boundaries can become another way of handing the cost of reform back to the harmed person.

Ira Noll’s award and the part nobody could replace

The Year 3008 broken hoist case gives restitution a material limit. Carrier Venn Krail ordered an overloaded lift after rigger Ira Noll warned against it; Slate Market had also allowed equipment fatigue to persist. Ira survived with a back injury and could no longer climb for work in the ensuing account. A Lantern panel apportioned responsibility between the carrier and the market. It did not describe an injured worker as a machine restored once replacement money had arrived.

The award distinguished lost earnings, immediate domestic help, damaged personal equipment and a ground-level work adaptation. Ira chose the adapted bench rather than a public ceremony presenting a symbolic new set of climbing tools. Household help was paid work accepted by named helpers, not an obligation placed on relatives because a court had announced that care mattered. The remedy covered a stated period and could be reviewed against continuing need; it did not promise unlimited resources the market did not possess.

Ira declined a face-to-face reconciliation with Venn. The refusal did not remove the award or obstruct the separate negotiation needed to obtain payment. A representative could carry the payment terms without reporting private pain as bargaining evidence. The public finding described the unsafe order and maintenance failure; detailed care notes stayed with those administering the support. Record keeping supplied enough information for recognition without making an injured person's body the district's permanent public exhibit.

Three secured instalments arrived after a failed enforcement attempt and a revised inter-host hearing. They paid actual bills and left future obligations outstanding. The bench supplied another kind of work but not the old freedom to move or the lost confidence around suspended loads. Restitution therefore made life more possible while remaining unable to recreate the before. Mercy, if Ira later chose it, would concern that unresolved relationship rather than a court's need for a pleasing conclusion.

The remedy after its own supporters caused harm

A Fury-backed party tried to collect Venn's cart without agreement from the receiving host. Its failure disrupted a ferry crossing and was followed by a guard's knife attack on a messenger. The compensation finding did not automatically justify every act performed in its name. A subsequent panel withdrew the unsupported collection instruction, accepted claims for the disrupted crossing and continued the original injury case.

This distinction prevents a righteous claimant from becoming a licence for allied recklessness. Ira was not required to pay the collectors' new liabilities. The guard's violence was heard separately, without erasing the boundary error that preceded it. A messenger harmed while carrying an overbroad instruction remained a harmed person. Correcting authority did not make the wound deserved.

The recognition agreement later withheld new commercial guarantees while preserving essential access and other households' cargo. It made enforcement narrower and more effective in this particular circuit. Elsewhere a wealthy carrier might find enough alternative trade to evade it. A restitution tradition must keep that weakness in view: a fair finding is an achievement, an implemented remedy another, and protecting dependants during implementation a third.
