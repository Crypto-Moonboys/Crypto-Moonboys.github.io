Altcoins

✅ Verified📅 Last updated: October 2026📂 Cryptocurrencies

Altcoin is a broad label for cryptocurrencies other than Bitcoin. It includes native coins of independent networks and, in looser usage, tokens issued on other chains. These assets differ in purpose, control, supply and legal treatment; sharing the label does not give them a common investment profile.

History and origins

Early alternatives (2011–2013)

Namecoin launched in 2011 using a separate chain based on Bitcoin’s code, with naming records as a central use case. Litecoin also launched in 2011, changing features including its hashing algorithm and target block interval. A software fork that starts a new network is different from a split sharing an existing transaction history. The XRP Ledger followed a different consensus design and a focus on transfers; it should not be described as simply another Bitcoin mining clone.

Ethereum and token fundraising

Ethereum’s 2015 launch made general-purpose contracts a widely used basis for issuing tokens. The 2017 fundraising boom expanded that use, alongside speculative and fraudulent offerings. The subsequent market decline showed that raising funds or issuing a token did not prove that a promised product existed. This history does not support one universal drawdown percentage for all alternative assets.

DeFi and competing networks

Lending, exchange and incentive programmes drew attention during 2020, while several Layer 1 networks competed for applications and users in the following cycle. Governance tokens and network fee assets serve different functions even when discussed in the same market narrative. A functioning protocol does not automatically direct its revenue to token holders.

A widening set of categories

Later discussion increasingly included rollups, computation, data services and tokenised claims on off-chain assets. Category labels can overlap and change. A project marketed around artificial intelligence or real-world assets still needs a specific account of what its token controls, pays for or represents.

Categories and their differences

  • Network coins: ETH and SOL are examples of assets used for fees and network participation. Different networks have different security assumptions.
  • Scaling and governance tokens: A token associated with a rollup need not be its gas asset or provide a claim on fees. A sidechain is not automatically secured like a rollup.
  • DeFi tokens: Rights can concern voting, incentives or particular economic claims. Those rights must be read from the actual protocol and terms.
  • Data, compute and interoperability: Tokens may pay operators or organise services, but a sector label does not prove demand or decentralisation.
  • Tokenised external assets: A claim on securities, property or other assets depends on issuers, custody, redemption and applicable law. Recording it on-chain does not remove those dependencies.

How to read market measurements

Market capitalisation is normally quoted price multiplied by a defined circulating supply; it is not cash available for all holders to withdraw. Fully diluted valuation uses a broader supply measure. Market aggregators can classify assets and circulating supply differently.

“Bitcoin dominance” is Bitcoin’s share of a selected total market capitalisation. There is no universal percentage below which an altcoin season must begin. Stablecoins, new token issuance and changes in an index’s coverage can alter the ratio without establishing predictable capital rotation.

What the category helps explain

Alternative networks and tokens show how different groups approach settlement, computation, governance and incentives. Comparing them can reveal genuine design choices. It is more useful to identify a token’s function than to assume every asset in a fashionable sector benefits equally from growth.

Diversifying among crypto tokens may still leave strong exposure to the same market, collateral or service providers. Understanding tokenomics, custody and application dependencies is therefore distinct from counting the number of assets held.

Risks and limitations

  • Concentrated allocations and scheduled unlocks can change available supply; they do not guarantee a price move on a known date.
  • Thin trading and misleading volume can make quoted prices difficult to realise.
  • Contracts, bridges, validators and custodians have different failure modes.
  • A name, whitepaper or exchange listing does not establish a working product or compliance.
  • Narratives can fade, development can stop and tokens can lose most or all of their value. No universal failure rate is asserted here.
