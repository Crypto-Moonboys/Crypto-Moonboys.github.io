The head in the channel

Nessie is the long-necked animal seen folding into the open channels of Lumina’s Waterfront. Low in the water, her brow and one eye can pass for a scrap breaking the surface. When she rises to breathe, the neck comes clear before the back; when she dives the back is often visible after the face has gone. Witnesses counting each dark curve have put three creatures in a channel where a single wake was recorded. Other watches have logged silhouettes in separate reaches at once. The district has no reliable count.

The boat crews use she. Nobody has examined the animal out of water. The old name came ashore in a sailor’s joke about a monster from a distant loch. It stuck because the deckhands needed a word they could shout to one another over a motor. Some families have since put their own ancestral stories under the same name. An account that knows the word can still be wrong about the animal.

Before the Ninth Berth

Before there was a berth agreement, a breaker’s widow named Iona Sedge kept a wet ledger in a cottage above the stone stairs. The business paid for returned floats by the lump. She paid the person who carried them up, including children who had fished them out after school, and used the rear pages to record the finds nobody would buy. A buoy gone inward against a falling tide. A cage with the outer wire rolled back and nothing left inside. Half a hinge found warm in a boat that hadn’t run all day.

Iona once saw a head cross the path of her own workboat. She put the oar flat in her lap and waited while the neck rolled past. Her husband, still alive then, told the dealer that an engine had done it. An engine claim could be priced. A woman frightened by a thing in the water was expected to row it off. Iona kept his version beside hers and continued to take the same boat out. Years later, when the engine did fail for real, she made her apprentice pole back against the shore instead of taking the deep shortcut.

The deck name Black Tow came from the refuse crews. Rotted fish drew everything small enough to feed on it. Some men claimed the longer shadows came for those fish; others swore the loads were being followed on purpose. A debt collector paid for the Black Tow story on a broadsheet and used the scare to buy three launches cheap. His crews still took the waste out after closing, with the lights off.

Teeth at the surface

A routine pass may show only the brow, the neck and a length of sliding wake. The same waters have produced a low, furious rise, teeth visible under a contracted eye and white water pushed out on both sides. People on the dock call the latter Angry Nessie. They use it as a description of what they saw. The watch has never had enough of a marked body out of the water to tell if it is always the same animal.

Nessie has been seen gliding past a hand-rowed punt and tearing into a boat that strayed across a line she was following. The butcher’s tow offered offal, a struggling hull and a trapped man in the same patch of water. It would be easy to turn that into a pretty theory about her hunting habits. The survivor remembered the sound of the wood and could give no account of where she was looking when she opened her mouth.

Dock gangs have sold teeth under the name. Nobby examined one that had been carved from a handle, right down to the notch left by its brass rivet. Iona’s ledger includes a long crescent gouge in a returned float. It was cut out of the float and sold on before anyone brought it to a person who knew what to look for.

At the Ninth Berth

The black tow incident happened after the first paid trial at the upper landing. The original double-tide flood had already been heard in public. On this later night the reserve rope was used for an off-book waste job. Nessie broke the returning launch beside the landing. One crewman lived with a ruined hand, and his mate never came back. The animal dived through the open channel with the boat’s lamp still shining into the water.

Sailor Bear was close enough to see the rope draw tight before the boat broke. He spent the rest of that night getting a straight account out of the people who had taken the money. He could show a broker where the rope had been stowed. He could not put a name to the person who sent the job down. When he next quoted a crossing time at the berth he put the unpriced wait on a separate line and dared anyone to promise it for him.

After the tow

Three mornings later Nessie surfaced between two occupied channels. The upper watch saw the back disappearing while the lower watch heard a short breath close to its steps. A trader called the all-clear because her delivery was losing ice. She got the delivery through. The porter she’d sent to fetch it took a longer route home and charged her for the walk.

The missing crewman’s brother put an empty float in the water where the line had gone under. He came back before dawn to collect it, because he knew what men were already saying they could sell by the piece. Danny kept the missing-man claim open while the insurer waited for a body. Iona’s old ledger ended up on Chada’s listening table wrapped in greaseproof paper. People could read the two versions of the engine story and decide which part they could afford to tell their employers.

Regular and angry sightings continue across open water. Sailor’s route book gives separate spaces for the head, the wake and the time last seen. It leaves room for two accounts of the same passage. Nobody can tell the watch where the animal sleeps, how many follow the deep channels, or why a quiet crossing can become bloody in one breath.
