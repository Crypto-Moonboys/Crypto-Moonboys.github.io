Land, Water, Animals and Stewardship

First WitnessStewardship tradition · Section 51Last updated: October 2026

Book III's custody language eventually expanded beyond archives and institutions. If nobody permanently owns the future, land and water become the clearest test.

Water first

Water traditions argue that some resources are too fundamental to be governed only as ordinary property. Access, maintenance and scarcity still require rules, but total monopoly over survival resources is treated with suspicion.

Ponds and later water-specialist cultures become natural interpreters of this tradition.

The upstream test

A common teaching asks whether an action still seems fair when imagined from downstream. Waste, pollution and extraction often export cost to people who did not choose the benefit.

The question became both ecological and political: who receives the profit, and who receives the runoff?

Animals

The Covenant never establishes one universal rule for eating animals. Later schools differ sharply. Most agree that unnecessary cruelty requires more justification than appetite or entertainment alone.

Some factions develop strong animal-protection traditions; others centre survival hunting and resource reality.

Inheritance without consent

Future generations cannot consent to poisoned soil, exhausted aquifers or irreversible waste. Keeper schools treat this as a special form of unchosen debt.

The argument is not that no resource may be used. It is that use creates obligations beyond the current market cycle.

Restoration

Repairing ecosystems becomes a literal form of restitution. Some damage cannot be restored completely, so honest restoration records what was lost instead of marketing partial repair as pristine recovery.

The beautiful reserve

One later proverb says: “A reserve nobody ever touches may be wisdom or vanity; ask what danger it was kept for.”

Conservation and hoarding can resemble each other from a distance. Stewardship requires explaining the purpose of restraint.
