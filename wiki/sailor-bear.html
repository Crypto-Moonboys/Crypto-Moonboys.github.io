Life on the Waterfront

Sailor Bear worked on broad harbor decks before taking a route on foot. He still wears his captain’s hat and red goggles when the wind drives spray over the rail. He knows which stairs stay open at a high tide and which dock approach backs up when two foremen expect the same truck.

The wage riding on a time

Sailor Bear began making tide notes on the inside of a storage lid so both shifts could read them. One foreman painted over the notes to keep the deck looking presentable for inspection. Six months later the foreman came looking for the person who could tell him why ropes were wearing on a particular corner. Sailor kept the diagram in his pocket after that and let the people working the ropes copy it before anybody put it on a wall.

He drinks tea with the Patrol Bike rider and makes small wooden hulls that float badly on purpose. A hull that takes water early is useful for teaching because somebody can do something about it while there is time. Nine Toes Joe has asked him to check the names on his landing notes. They still disagree about whether a step the city has fenced off ought to retain its old name.

The carpenter’s crossing

He once gave a crossing estimate using a set of timings kept in good weather. A carpenter missed a paid shift because she trusted him. He now keeps the range of possible arrival times in his pocket and marks the one he would give a person with a wage riding on it. The longer answer frustrates people in a hurry.

Off duty

In his spare time he carves little hulls from the wood that washes up in corners of the dock. He tests each one in a dish of rainwater and gives the best floaters away before he starts to feel possessive about them.

The longest crossing

At the upper landing trial, Sailor told Jack the longest of three crossing times because a relief shift was arriving from the bakery and its pay depended on getting there. He admits he could have put the full range on the board from the start. Jack’s argument about a clean number got under his skin. Afterwards the injured porter asked which figure Sailor would give to a person who had to stop twice to rest. Sailor had to measure the walk again.

He plans to bring that result to the next review with a version anybody can read over the rain on their goggles. He suspects the depot will print whichever figure fits the space on the board. He is considering painting a longer board himself. On a slow patrol he asks travellers what they have been promised at the end of their walk and whether they have enough time to argue about it when they get there.

He sells weed on nights when the boats do not call him in. One deckhand offered to settle a debt out of the next crossing wage. Sailor turned it down and then argued with himself all the way home about how much he could afford to forgive. The timing board now has room for the person who has to walk to work twice in one night.
