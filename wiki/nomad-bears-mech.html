Life on the Waterfront

The Nomad Bears Mech is the square-bodied walker with the orange balloon. Its pilot takes it along the edges of the new trails looking for places a carrier might need to set a heavy load down. The balloon helps other people find the pilot when the route runs behind trees or stacked freight.

The carrier behind the handle

The square-bodied mech’s pilot started as the rear carrier on a two-person crate crew. From that end of a long load they could see the shoulders of the person leading and very little of where they themselves could stand. When they were offered a pilot’s seat, they kept the same scratched hook from the front of their old harness. It hangs beside the orange balloon’s tether.

Nine Toes Joe sends them drafts of Rain Map notes to try out. Sailor Bear prefers the pilot’s reports when they say what somebody needed to set down before they could turn. The pilot loves a useful route name and will happily argue about the difference between the place a tree used to stand and the place its roots still push through the boards. The balloon is how their carriers find them when the hedge is high.

Room to turn a crate

The Nomad Bears’ Rain Map tradition puts particular weight on what a route is like for the next traveller. The pilot once recorded a narrow trail as passable after the mech walked through it. Sailor Bear asked how a pair of people carrying a long crate would turn around. The pilot went back with them and added the old turnout to the route notes.

Off duty

The pilot collects rejected route names on the back of delivery slips. They like a name that tells you what you can see from the path. The only argument they have lost three times is whether a turn can be named after a tree that is no longer there.

The crate that could go no farther

At the garden exit the mech waited while two people tried Joe’s dry-day turn carrying a crate. It could have lifted the whole thing and made the trial look easy. The pilot kept it where it was so both carriers could say what the route demanded of them. The person walking backwards asked the pilot to write down that the ground looked firm right up to the moment their heel came away.

The pilot has offered to mark the new turnout on one condition. The note must include who can use it and which other route still works when it fills up. A permanent board would let them help with the next crossing from farther up the island. It may take another bad-weather trial to persuade them. They ask visitors to describe the heaviest thing they carried and whether the person at the front could see them.
