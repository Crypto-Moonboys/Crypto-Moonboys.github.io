Paper Hands

🌙 Community Lore 📅 Last updated: October 2026 📂 Lore
📋 Contents

Paper Hands is the accused figure in HODL Wars’ mythology of resolve: the person said to have released a grip too soon. The older tellings contrast it sharply with Diamond Hands and promise a later transformation through renewed holding. The more difficult stories ask what was actually promised, who depended on it and whether the name describes a failure or merely punishes someone for leaving.

The curse in the older telling

The older Paper Hands story begins with a frightened release. A warrior hears a rumour, sees the crowd retreating or watches a familiar measure fall, and lets go before discovering whether the danger can be endured. The image makes the decision visible: hands that should grip turn thin, fold and tear. In the harshest versions, the condition marks an inferior kind of person.

W81’s subject register calls Paper Hands the opposing allegiance to Diamond Hands and describes its members as weak-willed traders who sell early. The Whale Lords chronicle treats them as the first casualties of engineered Dips and FOMO Plagues. These are the judgments of those tellings. They establish the role of the accused figure in the mythology, rather than a complete account of everyone to whom the name is applied.

The legacy page also calls Paper Hands a curse and makes its bearer the initial carrier of every Plague. Neither claim establishes a literal transformation or a reliable mechanism. A person who becomes frightened is not thereby the cause of everyone else’s fear. An allegation that someone sold, withdrew or changed their mind needs its own account of what happened.

The accusation in HODL Wars

By 3008, HODL language concerns the continuity of people, records, routes and culture as well as inherited market imagery. Paper Hands can therefore accuse someone of much more than leaving an asset. It can be used against a worker ending a shift, a witness revising an account, a household asking for borrowed space back or a faction refusing to extend emergency command.

Sometimes the accusation points toward a real failure. Someone may leave a task on which others rely without warning or deny a promise after receiving its benefit. Sometimes the same words conceal an unreasonable demand that the person remain indefinitely. The label does not distinguish the cases. Only the original undertaking, changed conditions and consequences can do that.

This is why the Paper Hands and Diamond Hands opposition cannot serve as a complete moral census. The sources do not establish two exhaustive populations dividing the forty factions, and neither category supplies a universal recruitment roll. A person can act dependably in one situation and badly in another. A group can praise endurance while refusing to pay the people whose endurance it requires.

The HODL Warriors’ emergency defence and the HODL X Warriors’ separate champion tradition remain distinct. Departure from a local common response does not automatically settle a champion’s existing service obligations, and possession of a champion title does not authorise permanent command over everyone answering a common call.

The Folded Flag

The Folded Flag is a new local Paper Hands account set in the 3008 world. Rell Fen agrees to remain at a receiving post until a replacement arrives. The work is ordinary: direct late arrivals to the correct shelter and keep track of which rooms still have space. A small cloth flag above the post indicates that someone is present.

After hearing that a nearby route may close, Rell leaves early to secure a passage for a personal errand. The fear is understandable, but Rell neither waits for relief nor tells the shelters that the post is unattended. The flag stays raised. Two arrivals wait beneath it, believing that the receiver is briefly occupied elsewhere; one eventually reaches a shelter after its kitchen has closed.

Another worker, Juna Rill, finds the unattended post and folds the flag. The next day the phrase Paper Hands appears beside Rell’s name. Rell argues that everyone would have left under the same uncertainty. Juna does not claim to know what everyone would have done. She asks Rell to acknowledge the narrow failure: the flag represented a service that was no longer there, and people relied on it.

The account does not redeem Rell by revealing that the route warning was secretly correct. Whether the route closed later cannot change the absence of notice. Rell had a reason for wanting to leave and still owed others a handover or an honest warning. The episode preserves responsibility without turning one failure into proof of a permanently defective character.

Return Without a Banner

Rell first offers to work twice as long on the next shift. The proposal sounds suitably dramatic to people repeating the Paper Hands accusation. Juna objects that exhaustion does not repair a misleading notice. The receiving work needs a clear way to show who is present and what happens when a receiver must leave unexpectedly.

Together they change the local arrangement. A second person knows when relief is due, and the flag is lowered whenever the post is unattended. A note directs arrivals to a particular staffed room rather than promising that someone will return soon. These practices require real labour from the people named in them; a new notice alone cannot make an empty room staffed.

Rell also helps cover the additional work created by the late arrival and apologises to the people who waited. One accepts; another declines further contact. The account leaves those responses different. Rell can undertake repair without acquiring a right to forgiveness, and the affected people do not have to perform reconciliation to make the community feel complete.

The episode is called Return Without a Banner because Rell resumes some ordinary work without receiving a public Diamond Hands transformation. No new asset is acquired, no spectacular test proves permanent courage and no ceremony erases the original record. The return is a changed pattern of action, open to judgment over time.

The release that needed no redemption

Another local telling follows Juna after the receiving-post dispute. A different organiser asks her to remain beyond an agreed shift because people now associate her with the corrected practice. Juna has already arranged care elsewhere and cannot extend the work. The organiser calls her refusal Paper Hands, using the very event in which she defended a handover to claim that she must always provide one personally.

Juna produces the agreed hours and the name of the person responsible for arranging relief. She has completed what she offered. The next shift remains a genuine problem, but its existence does not retrospectively enlarge her promise. Another worker covers part of the gap, and the receiving service closes earlier for the remainder.

The two Rell and Juna accounts belong together because their outward action is similar: someone leaves while work remains. The obligations differ. Rell departed during an accepted responsibility without notice; Juna declined a new demand after fulfilling hers. Treating both as one kind of weakness would conceal the very facts needed to judge them.

Juna does not undergo a redemption arc. The correction belongs to the organiser’s description of her refusal. Her continued place in the community should not depend on returning later to prove that she cared all along.

The Dry Sheet

The Dry Sheet develops the image of paper itself. Record-maker Nella Voss receives a batch of rain-damaged notices after a move between storage rooms. She has no implied family relationship to Kael or Dima Voss. The notices contain routine commitments that have not all been copied elsewhere. Some ink is readable, some pages have stuck together, and several readers want a clean consolidated record before any uncertainty has been resolved.

Nella lays out the surviving sheets with their gaps visible. The result looks less authoritative than a polished replacement. A visitor jokes that these are literally Paper Hands records: fragile material trusted by fragile people. Nella points to the marks made by different keepers and asks which would be lost if she pretended the new copy had always been complete.

The recovery produces useful fragments and unresolved promises. A carrier’s delivery is supported by two surviving notes; a disputed payment is supported by neither side’s complete record. Nella records both limitations rather than giving the better-looking claimant automatic priority. The paper’s weakness creates work, but the willingness to show its damage makes that work assessable.

The Dry Sheet does not declare paper superior to every durable medium. It gives the despised material a place in a culture too ready to confuse hardness with truth. A record can be physically fragile and honestly kept. A hard surface can preserve an error with equal confidence. The value lies partly in how people handle correction, not merely in what resists tearing.

The inherited redemption arc

The legacy sequence begins with a sale, then moves through regret, education, renewed entry and a final successful hold. That form explains why Paper Hands stories often end at the next test rather than following the person’s whole life. The ending must transform the figure into its opposite, or the audience may feel that the lesson has failed.

The newer local accounts retain the possibility of change without making that structure compulsory. Rell needs to address an actual failure and does so through accountable work. Juna has no failure of the same kind to redeem. Nella’s record has damage that cannot be reversed by a more confident declaration. Their stories are not interchangeable just because a mocking label can be attached to all three.

The First Witness Hold asks people to distinguish patience from denial and a falling price from a failing principle. It allows promises obtained through fraud to be released. Those distinctions prevent an accusation from imprisoning its target in the need to prove endless endurance. They also prevent release from becoming a universal excuse to disregard other people’s reliance.

An honest account can therefore say both that a person should have warned others before leaving and that the person was entitled to leave under different terms. The moral work happens in establishing those terms, rather than deciding in advance that every open hand has betrayed the cause.

Outside the allegory

Paper Hands is market slang as well as fiction. Its use in a story does not establish the motives of real people who sell, stop participating or redirect resources toward other needs. The earlier page’s selected tales of missed gains describe hindsight, not a dependable measure of the decisions available at the time.

This entry does not recommend holding or selling, promise a future reversal or treat financial loss as evidence of inferior character. Nor does it establish a literal curse, a clinical condition or a live game penalty. The fictional opposition remains useful for examining courage, obligation and group pressure without becoming an instruction imposed on a reader’s own assets.

The unresolved global war also remains larger than these small cases. Correcting a receiving post does not end HODL WARS, and Rell’s return supplies no prophecy of the Final Fork. The achievement is limited: fewer people are misled about who is present, and a name can remain in the record without becoming a permanent sentence.
