TREVOR FUNGThe London DJ who carried Ibiza’s open-minded sound into Britain’s acid-house revolution

Trevor Fung is a British DJ associated with Balearic music and the development of the UK house scene. The Crypto Moonboys archive identifies him as the GraffPUNKS Godfather and a radio ambassador. His place in the project rests on real music culture; later depictions of rhythm carrying memory through the Year 3008 Grid are fictional interpretations of that legacy.

The following outline retains the principal history described there: a grounding in soul, funk and jazz, early experience of Ibiza, and a role connecting that eclectic approach with London club culture. Soul, funk and the selector’s craft

The earlier biography places Fung’s musical grounding in soul, funk and jazz before house became a British mainstream sound. It describes a DJ learning to work with the mood of a room and with records arriving from different scenes. That broad listening background explains why the later Balearic idea cannot be reduced to one tempo or a fixed list of genres.

Selection is relational: the meaning of a record changes with what surrounds it, when it arrives and who is listening. In the profile’s reading of Fung’s approach, sequence and atmosphere matter as much as category. The cultural contribution is therefore not simply possessing particular records but making connections that an audience can experience together.

Ibiza before the familiar 1987 story

The inherited music-history account places Fung in Ibiza before the often-repeated 1987 origin story of British acid house. It connects his experience with the island’s mixed dancefloors and with Alfredo Fiorito’s eclectic approach at Amnesia. The important distinction is between the musical culture already present on the island and the later moment when a particular group of British DJs helped carry aspects of it home.

The existing profile describes the freedom to move among pop, rock, funk, electronic and other records as central to that encounter. Balearic is useful here as an approach to programming rather than a claim that one person invented every sound associated with Ibiza. Fung’s role belongs within a network of selectors, venues, travellers and audiences.

The Funhouse and The Project

In its London account, the earlier biography connects Fung and Paul Oakenfold with The Funhouse, then describes The Project Club as a place where Balearic ideas met emerging house and other imported music. It also names Carl Cox in the Project context. These are retained as the profile’s historical associations, not expanded into claims that Fung founded or controlled every venue mentioned.

The significance of the club setting is practical. An open-minded selection needs a room willing to hear it, and the room’s response can change how the next set is built. The Project narrative describes experimentation and resistance as well as recognition, preserving the work of creating an audience rather than presenting a finished movement that appeared overnight.

The 1987 network and its aftermath

The earlier account places Fung and Ian St Paul with The Project in San Antonio in 1987, and describes visits by Paul Oakenfold, Danny Rampling, Nicky Holloway and Johnny Walker. It links that encounter with the later London flowering of nights such as Shoom, Spectrum, The Future and The Trip. This is a history of connections among people and places, not a claim of sole invention.

The same profile traces Fung’s later work through the expanding UK rave and club scene, naming events including Sunrise, Energy, Biology and World Dance. A career spanning such settings is broader than a single celebrated holiday or club night. The current reference does not reproduce an unverified complete residency list, charity appointment list or recording catalogue; those deserve their own direct evidence.

Fiction: rhythm and collective memory

The existing Year 3008 interpretation imagines Fung’s musical legacy as a signal that carries remembered dancefloors through the Grid. Against Block Topia’s desire to classify experience into approved patterns, an unexpected transition between records becomes a symbol of people finding a connection that the system did not prescribe. This is cultural imagery, not a literal biography or a confirmed ability belonging to an NFT.

First Witness teaching on music, dance and performance makes a useful companion: no genre is inherently sacred, attribution matters, and a live mistake can remind an audience that people are actually present. Applied to this lore, music can sustain memory without becoming an unquestionable source of truth. The comparison does not assign Fung authorship of the Covenant or claim that he shares its fictional beliefs.

The image belongs to the unfinished main saga of Year 3008. It does not announce the Final Fork, unify the Sacred/Aether memory layer with the World Chain or True Bitcoin Fork, or erase the distinctions among the world’s forty factions. The symbolic force of a shared rhythm does not require every listener to become the same person or hold the same allegiance.

The current creator context

Crypto Moonboys is the Graffiti Kings creator umbrella, and music is one form of creative work within it. A DJ or listener does not need to purchase a token or adopt a fictional faction to have a place in that wider cultural project.

The real performer, his work, a programme listing and a fictional tribute retain their separate rights and meanings.
