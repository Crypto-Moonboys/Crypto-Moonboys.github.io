TREVOR FUNGThe London DJ who carried Ibiza’s open-minded sound into Britain’s acid-house revolution

Trevor Fung is a British Balearic and house DJ, producer and club-culture pioneer whose career connects late-1970s soul and jazz-funk, early Ibiza, the birth of Balearic Beat and the explosion of acid house in Britain. He is widely recognised as one of the people who reached Ibiza’s eclectic dance culture before the better-known 1987 origin story and helped introduce that experience to the London DJs who would build some of the era’s defining clubs.

Within the GraffPUNKS and Crypto Moonboys network, Trevor is presented as a Godfather and ambassador: a real cultural bridge between the original rave generation and a newer digital world of online radio, NFTs, character culture and community storytelling. His significance begins with music, not blockchain. The later Web3 role grows from a decades-long career spent moving records, people and ideas across scenes.

From Soul Boy to Professional DJ

Fung began DJing professionally at sixteen. His early musical grounding came through UK and American soul, funk and jazz, long before house music had entered the British mainstream. He has recalled starting as a warm-up DJ around the soul circuit, including work connected to Steve Walsh’s events, where large travelling crowds and all-night music culture already showed how a DJ could organise a temporary community.

This background matters because the Balearic approach was never simply a house-music formula. It depended on selectors who were comfortable moving between styles, tempos and moods. Fung’s early knowledge of soul, funk, jazz and imported records gave him the vocabulary to recognise the significance of Ibiza’s eclectic dancefloors when he encountered them.

Ibiza Before the Myth

Trevor Fung had been visiting Ibiza from the late 1970s and was DJing on the island in the early 1980s. Accounts place him at Amnesia before the famous 1987 trip that later became the simplified British acid-house origin story. Ibiza at that time was not yet the corporate super-club destination it would become. Its dancefloors mixed tourists, workers, locals, artists and international travellers, while DJs moved freely between pop, rock, indie, electronic music, Latin sounds, funk and emerging dance records.

Fung understood that the power of the island was not one genre but permission to ignore genre boundaries. He absorbed the influence of DJs such as Alfredo Fiorito and the broader atmosphere of venues including Amnesia, Es Paradis and Ku. The result was a style built around emotional flow rather than rigid classification.

Balearic was not a fixed sound. It was the freedom to play the right record for the moment, wherever that record came from.

The Funhouse and The Project Club

Back in London, Fung worked with his long-time friend Paul Oakenfold on The Funhouse, combining the eclectic influence of Ibiza with hip hop, go-go and the urban club sounds already moving through Britain. He later developed The Project Club, with Carl Cox also associated as a resident. The Project became an important test site for Balearic ideas, early Chicago house, Detroit music and records that did not fit established London club categories.

The London scene did not immediately understand the mixture. That resistance is part of Fung’s importance. He was attempting to build the bridge before the culture had agreed that a bridge was needed. The Project’s influence grew because the room demonstrated what happened when a DJ treated the dancefloor as an open system rather than a genre enclosure.

The 1987 Chain Reaction

In 1987 Fung and Ian St Paul operated The Project concept in San Antonio, Ibiza. Friends including Paul Oakenfold, Danny Rampling, Nicky Holloway and Johnny Walker visited the island and experienced the combination of Alfredo’s Amnesia sets, Ibiza’s mixed crowds and Fung’s local knowledge. When they returned to Britain, they helped launch or develop nights including Shoom, Spectrum, The Future and The Trip.

This period is often compressed into a single holiday story. More detailed histories place Fung as an earlier traveller, DJ and connector who helped guide the people later credited with carrying Balearic culture into London. His role was not that he single-handedly invented acid house. It was that he formed part of the human network that allowed music, places, friendships and new dancefloor behaviour to converge.

Acid House, Orbital Raves and Major Residencies

As acid house expanded, Fung played across the UK and at major M25-era events including Sunrise, Energy, Biology and World Dance. He also held or appeared at influential nights and residencies such as Rage, Spectrum, Enter the Dragon and Love at the Wag. These events moved dance culture from small clubs into warehouses, fields and large-scale one-off gatherings.

His career also extended far beyond the original acid-house moment. Fung continued performing internationally, appearing at clubs, festivals and reunion events while maintaining the broad musical approach that had defined his early work. His history includes appearances connected with major cultural gatherings and later Balearic, house and heritage-club events.

Selector, Producer and Cultural Connector

Fung’s identity is often reduced to the phrase “acid-house pioneer”, but his working method is wider. He is a selector concerned with sequence, atmosphere and the relationship between records. His sets can draw from soul, funk, jazz, house, Balearic music, electronic records, indie, dub and other styles when the room supports them.

He has also worked as a producer and recording artist. Releases under his own name include “Return to the Hills of Kathmandu”, while his broader career has included production work and projects under different names. The recorded output is one part of a career primarily defined by live selection and scene-building.

Fung has also supported charity activity and music education, with biographical material identifying ambassadorial connections to organisations including Last Night a DJ Saved My Life, Macmillan and Groove School. That work extends the original club-culture principle that the DJ can act as organiser and community catalyst rather than only entertainer.

Why Trevor Fung Matters

Trevor Fung matters because cultural revolutions are rarely created by one headline name or one night. They emerge through networks of people who travel, listen, introduce friends, test ideas in difficult rooms and continue before the wider culture understands what is happening.

His story corrects the simplified version of acid-house history. He was already visiting and playing in Ibiza before the famous 1987 trip, already trying to translate Balearic freedom into London clubs, and already connecting the people who would help turn that experience into a national movement.

Within Crypto Moonboys, he represents the music-side equivalent of the original graffiti wall: a real person whose work proves that culture is built by creating the space, selecting the signal and keeping the community moving.
