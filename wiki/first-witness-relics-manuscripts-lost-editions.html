Relics, Manuscripts and Lost Editions

First WitnessMaterial culture · Section 38Last updated: October 2026

A text born in a digital age still produced relics. Humans attach meaning to objects that touched history, even when the religion warns them not to confuse the object with the truth.

The Concord Drafts

Fragments of pre-ratification drafts are among the most important relics because they show what the authors removed. Their value is historical, not mystical. A deleted sentence can reveal where agreement failed.

Forged Concord Drafts became a major black market because one convincing “lost clause” could support a political movement.

The First Printings

Early physical editions authenticated against the Bitcoin witness became pilgrimage objects despite repeated warnings that paper carries no special holiness. Some communities insist handling one creates a duty to copy or teach from it.

Others regard that custom as superstition wrapped around archival value.

The Burned Edition

One famous relic tradition centres a partially burned copy whose surviving pages begin with the Keeper and end halfway through the Hold. Nobody agrees which historical purge damaged it.

Its missing sections made it symbolically powerful: a scripture about preservation surviving precisely as an incomplete object.

The Mirror Shards

Small storage fragments containing corrupted First Witness commentaries are collectively called Mirror Shards. Most are mundane data remnants. A few include textual variants not preserved elsewhere.

Collectors, Chain Scribes and fraudsters have fought over them for centuries because corruption can hide both genuine lost readings and very convenient inventions.

The Empty Key

Several museums claim to hold an original signing key associated with the Bitcoin Witness event. At least some claims are mutually exclusive. The key itself has no spendable authority and may never have had any.

Crownless teachers enjoy the irony: people line up to see a key whose main lesson is that no key should become the only key.

Relics versus evidence

The Source Register distinguishes relic status from evidentiary value. An object can be emotionally powerful and historically weak. Another can look boring and be decisive because its provenance is strong.

The First Witness repeatedly asks readers to resist the human tendency to trust the dramatic object over the better record.
