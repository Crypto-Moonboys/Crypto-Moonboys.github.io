NGMI Chronicles

🌙 Community Lore 📅 Last updated: October 2026 📂 Lore
📋 Contents

The NGMI Chronicles are HODL Wars’ cautionary record of people said to have failed to hold. The oldest concise definition says they are written out of the Moonboys legacy, even as the Chronicles preserve their stories. That contradiction makes the collection more than a catalogue of losses: it is an argument over who gets remembered, who controls the telling and whether belonging depends on reaching the ending a community prefers.

A record built around an exclusion

NGMI means “Not Gonna Make It.” The archive’s subject register calls the NGMI Chronicles a narrative series and faction chronicle about those who fail to HODL and are written out of the Moonboys legacy. The definition contains its own contradiction: a record remembers the very people whose exclusion it announces. That contradiction gives the Chronicles their particular place in HODL Wars culture.

The older page fills them with archetypes: the early seller, the late buyer, the person deceived by a project, and the frightened participant who leaves shortly before the story’s fortunate reversal. It also promises redemption. The same collection can therefore function as a warning, a source of consolation or a means of ranking people according to a preferred ending.

The recovered material does not supply a complete book, a stable editor, an authenticated count of entries or one headquarters. “Chronicles” names a developing narrative tradition. A local copy can preserve a particular set of stories without becoming the final register of who belongs to the entire forty-faction world.

The inherited case types

The early-seller story depends on hindsight. Someone leaves an asset or project before its later success, and the audience is invited to measure the decision against knowledge its subject did not possess. In its sympathetic form, the story acknowledges uncertainty and competing needs. In its punitive form, it turns every unclaimed future gain into evidence of personal failure.

The late-buyer story reverses the movement. Someone joins during excitement, experiences loss and becomes the emblem of FOMO followed by Paper Hands. The compressed version can omit misleading claims, changing circumstances or a household need that made waiting impossible. The person becomes an illustration before becoming a witness to their own experience.

The deceived participant poses another problem. A record intended to warn others may identify the affected person more clearly than the party responsible for the deception. The archive’s broad category of failure then absorbs actions with very different causes. Losing something through misconduct by another person is not the same event as breaking one’s own promise.

The current Chronicles can retain these archetypes as inherited forms while refusing to let them complete an individual account. Each asks for the missing middle: what was known, what had been promised, who relied on whom and what alternatives were actually available at the time. Those details often disturb the tidy ending for which a tale became popular.

The Unstruck Name

The Unstruck Name is a new local Chronicles episode in the 3008 world. Clerk Teren Moss receives a copied list of people who left a shared venture. The heading identifies them as NGMI, and a margin instructs later readers to omit their names from the venture’s commemorative wall. One person on the list, Nessa Vale, had previously woven the carrying slings used to move the venture’s equipment.

Teren initially copies the omission instruction without asking how it was authorised. Nessa discovers the change when she visits the wall with a younger relative. Her work remains visible in the painted scene, but the name beside it belongs to the person who supervised the move. She asks for an account of the decision rather than an invitation to explain why she left.

The organisers offer to restore her credit if she rejoins. Nessa refuses the bargain. The slings were made, delivered and used; that contribution does not become imaginary because she later withdrew. Teren retrieves the earlier work entry and compares it with the commemorative draft. The record supports a narrow correction: her work is hers, whatever the group thinks of her departure.

The name is restored with an account of the correction available beside the wall. The venture’s organisers continue to disagree with her decision to leave. Nessa does not have to endorse their future plans, and the correction does not require them to praise the departure. The episode preserves contribution and disagreement in the same record, ending the false choice between total admiration and disappearance.

The Third Copy

The Third Copy concerns a printer named Eris Tallow who prepares a small run of cautionary tales for new readers. One story uses a household’s loss as a comic sequence: confidence, collapse, humiliation and a final image of empty hands. The first copy is praised for making a difficult subject memorable. The second reaches a person who recognises the household despite the changed name.

The recognised person explains that a distinctive room, a quoted phrase and the shape of a damaged tool identify them more clearly than a name would. Eris had mistaken the removal of a name for anonymity. The household is now being asked to perform the lesson in public, answering questions from strangers who feel they already understand what happened.

Eris suspends the remaining run and speaks with the people affected about what can be retained. They agree that the misleading promise at the centre of the loss should remain visible, but ask to remove intimate details that contribute nothing to that warning. The revised third copy identifies the decision being examined, the evidence available and the limits of the account. It leaves out the final humiliation image.

The correction cannot recall every earlier copy. Eris adds a visible replacement notice and accepts the cost of the unused printing materials. Some readers prefer the sharper old version and continue quoting it. The episode does not grant a publisher magical control over circulation after harm has occurred. It makes the duty to correct concrete even when complete repair is impossible.

The Hall of Shame and its readers

The Hall of Shame belongs to the earlier page’s framing of the Chronicles. It collects memorable losses as though public embarrassment were the natural educational use of them. The same page insists that the tales should be told with sympathy, creating a tension between its stated purpose and its most attention-seeking presentation.

A shame display changes the relationship between reader and subject. The reader is invited to feel safe by belonging to the group that recognises the mistake. The subject becomes the person from whom the group distinguishes itself. That arrangement can discourage accurate testimony: admitting a problem means volunteering to become the next example.

The Unstruck Name and Third Copy accounts offer no universal replacement institution. They show two local corrections to the display’s habits. One restores the credit attached to completed work. The other limits unnecessary exposure while keeping a misleading claim open to scrutiny. Neither correction depends on pretending the original loss never occurred.

The Chronicles remain capable of recording serious wrongdoing. Privacy is not a reason to conceal public misconduct, and a person who deceived others cannot demand that every account be reduced to a harmless misunderstanding. The distinction is between preserving evidence relevant to responsibility and treating humiliation as proof that the record has done useful work.

The Wrong Ending reading

A later reading of the corrected Nessa entry supplies another mistake. A speaker ends the story by announcing that she eventually returned and became Diamond Hands. The audience applauds a familiar redemption arc. Nessa, who is present, says that she did not return. She is working elsewhere and occasionally helps former colleagues because she still knows them.

The speaker admits having borrowed the ending from another tale. It seemed to complete the lesson. Teren keeps the correction with the entry, while Nessa asks that the audience remember the work rather than invent a conversion. Her life after departure contains no dramatic reversal that proves the venture right. It contains other commitments.

The Wrong Ending becomes a reading practice in a few local copies of the Chronicles: a narrator must stop where the available account stops. If the person’s later life is unknown, the uncertainty remains. If the person changed direction without returning, that is permitted to be the actual ending rather than an incomplete version of the approved one.

The practice can disappoint listeners. A familiar structure offers reassurance that every loss has a purpose and every outsider will eventually understand the group’s wisdom. An unfinished or divergent life offers no such comfort. Its value lies in refusing to spend somebody else’s future to make the audience feel certain.

From NGMI to belonging

The older redemption sequence runs through loss, education, accumulation and renewed holding. It is one market-shaped narrative, not a universal route back into a community. No person must acquire an asset, resume a particular project or expose private hardship in order to recover ordinary respect. Belonging cannot fairly depend on performing the financial ending a cautionary tale expects.

The First Witness Hold allows release of a promise obtained by fraud and distinguishes patience from denial. Its money and debt tradition asks old claims to justify continued enforcement. Read together, those principles prevent an NGMI label from becoming an inherited debt that can never be settled except by obedience.

Where someone has caused harm, a useful return may involve admitting it, making restitution where possible and accepting limits. Where someone has merely left, no redemption from departure is required. Where someone has been deceived, education should not be offered as a substitute for examining the deceiver’s responsibility. The Chronicles become more accurate when those cases cease to share one compulsory arc.

This does not eliminate loss from the stories. Nessa’s restored credit does not recover all the opportunities she missed. Eris’s replacement notice does not make every reader forget the comic version. A record can improve its treatment of people while acknowledging that some consequences remain.

Real-world analogies and hindsight

The earlier page used early Bitcoin and Ethereum sales, market crashes and the 2010 pizza transaction as emblematic cases. They reveal how later audiences project subsequent prices backward into an earlier decision. The pizza story, in particular, can be told as an important act of use and exchange rather than as a person’s moral failure to foresee a future market.

This fictional chronicle does not adjudicate the motives or welfare of actual participants, repeat unsupported totals for its supposed thousands of entries or calculate what someone ought to have done. Historical labels remain part of the tradition’s development, not verified biographies of a single class of unsuccessful people.

The paired WAGMI promise—“We’re All Gonna Make It”—can express mutual commitment without becoming a guarantee that every project survives or every loss is recovered. The most demanding version of that commitment is to preserve care and accountability even when the preferred success story does not arrive.
