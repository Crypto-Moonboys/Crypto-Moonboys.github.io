Bitcoin (BTC)

⭐ Featured 📅 Last updated: October 2026 📂 Cryptocurrencies

Bitcoin (BTC) is a digital payment network and its native asset. Participants verify transfers under shared rules without a central issuer. Its proof-of-work system, limited issuance and public transaction history have made it an important reference point for later cryptocurrencies. Those properties do not guarantee a rising price, anonymous use or freedom from loss.

History and origins

The whitepaper (2008)

On 31 October 2008, the pseudonymous Satoshi Nakamoto published Bitcoin: A Peer-to-Peer Electronic Cash System. It combined earlier cryptographic and distributed-systems ideas into a way to order payments and resist double spending without relying on a bank. The innovation was the combination of signatures, proof of work, peer-to-peer verification and incentives; cryptographic chains of records already had precursors.

The genesis block (2009)

Bitcoin’s genesis block was mined on 3 January 2009. It contains the newspaper headline “The Times 03/Jan/2009 Chancellor on brink of second bailout for banks.” The inscription places the launch in its financial-crisis context; broader claims about Satoshi’s intentions remain interpretations. On 12 January, Satoshi sent 10 BTC to Hal Finney in the first recorded person-to-person Bitcoin transfer.

Bitcoin Pizza Day (2010)

On 22 May 2010, Laszlo Hanyecz arranged to pay 10,000 BTC for two pizzas. The event is remembered as an early documented exchange of bitcoin for physical goods. Later price comparisons describe a hypothetical valuation of those coins, not money the seller could necessarily have realised at one quoted price.

Mt. Gox and exchange custody

Mt. Gox grew into a major early Bitcoin exchange before suspending activity and entering bankruptcy in 2014. Its reported loss initially included approximately 850,000 BTC; some coins were later located. This was an exchange custody failure, not a change to Bitcoin’s issuance rules. Its history shows why a balance credited by a company differs from funds controlled directly on the network.

Early price cycles and Cyprus (2013)

Bitcoin passed the thousand-dollar level during the 2013 market cycle and fell sharply afterwards. The Cyprus banking crisis appeared in contemporary discussion of alternatives to bank deposits, but a complex price move cannot be assigned to one event alone. Selected historical prices do not establish a dependable trading cycle.

The 2017 market and futures

The 2017 expansion brought wider retail interest, exchange growth and fundraising through other crypto assets. Bitcoin approached twenty thousand dollars before a prolonged decline. Cboe and CME both launched Bitcoin futures in December 2017. Their launches expanded regulated derivatives access; neither proves a single explanation for the subsequent market reversal.

Corporate treasuries (2020–2021)

MicroStrategy began buying bitcoin for its treasury in 2020, and companies including Square and Tesla also announced purchases during this period. These decisions made corporate exposure more visible. Holdings, company names and rankings change, so this history does not present an undated treasury balance as current fact. Bitcoin reached new price highs in 2021 before another major decline.

The FTX collapse (2022)

FTX’s collapse in November 2022 damaged confidence in crypto intermediaries and coincided with severe market stress. Misuse of customer funds exposed failures of custody and corporate governance. It did not prove that cryptography alone makes an investment or self-custody arrangement safe; those arrangements carry different risks.

Spot ETFs and public policy

US spot Bitcoin exchange-traded products received SEC approval in January 2024. They allow exposure through brokerage accounts while adding a fund, custodian, fees and legal terms between the investor and the underlying bitcoin. The fourth subsidy halving followed in April 2024. A US Strategic Bitcoin Reserve was announced in March 2025; that historical announcement is not evidence of an unlimited buying programme or a price floor.

How it works

Nodes and transaction validation

Full nodes check transactions and blocks against their own consensus rules. Miners propose ordering; they do not gain authority to make an invalid signature or excessive subsidy valid. When valid histories compete, Bitcoin’s chain-selection rule follows accumulated proof of work, rather than counting one vote for every computer. Light clients rely on different verification assumptions, and not every participant stores the complete transaction history.

Proof-of-work mining

Miners repeatedly hash candidate block headers, looking for a result below the network’s difficulty target. Bitcoin adjusts difficulty every 2,016 blocks to keep the long-run average interval near ten minutes. Individual blocks can arrive much sooner or later. A successful valid block can pay its miner the scheduled subsidy plus included transaction fees. The subsidy became 3.125 BTC at the April 2024 halving.

Proof of work makes rewriting accepted history costly under the network’s security assumptions. It does not make disruption or a majority-hash-power attack mathematically impossible. Mining concentration, software faults, connectivity and economic conditions still matter.

The halving schedule

The block subsidy halves every 210,000 blocks. Under the existing rules, issuance approaches a limit commonly described as 21 million BTC. Changes to those rules would require participants to adopt different software and could create a competing network; no single administrator can simply increase the subsidy on behalf of everyone. A known reduction in issuance does not guarantee higher demand or a future bull market.

Wallets and private keys

A wallet manages the information needed to authorise transactions. Assets are recorded on the network, not stored inside a phone or hardware device. Spending conditions can require one signature, several signatures or other supported conditions. Losing every usable key and backup can make funds inaccessible. A stolen key may let an attacker spend funds, but technical control does not transfer legal ownership. Custodial services instead manage keys on a customer’s behalf.

Lightning Network

Lightning uses payment channels to move some payment activity away from the base chain. Opening and settling channels involve on-chain transactions; routed payments depend on channel liquidity, reachable participants and the chosen wallet or service. Successful payments can be fast and inexpensive, but fees, routing failures, liquidity limits and custody arrangements vary. Base-chain throughput likewise depends on transaction size and block usage rather than one permanent transactions-per-second figure.

Unspent transaction outputs

Bitcoin tracks unspent transaction outputs, or UTXOs. A transaction spends existing outputs and creates new ones, often including change. A wallet’s spendable balance is derived from outputs it can satisfy the conditions to spend. This model supports independent validation, but it does not provide automatic privacy: public inputs and outputs can reveal links between activities.

What Bitcoin makes possible

Bitcoin offers a shared settlement record with publicly inspectable issuance and spending rules. People use it for transfers, savings, collateral and speculation. Its lack of a central issuer can reduce some intermediary dependencies, while price volatility and transaction costs may limit particular uses. Scarcity alone does not establish purchasing-power stability.

Censorship resistance is a network property with practical limits. Custodians can freeze accounts; service providers can restrict access; authorities may seize keys or assets through legal and physical processes. Possession of bitcoin does not place a person outside applicable law or guarantee that a transaction will be included promptly.

Risks and unresolved questions

  • Market risk: Bitcoin has experienced deep declines. A later cycle need not remain above an earlier peak.
  • Custody and recovery: Lost keys, compromised devices, mistaken transfers and failing custodians create different paths to loss.
  • Consensus and software: Concentrated mining, implementation bugs and network disruption can affect availability and settlement confidence.
  • Energy use: Mining consumes electricity and equipment. Environmental impact depends on energy sources, location and methodology; a single undated estimate is inadequate.
  • Future cryptography: A sufficiently capable quantum computer could threaten existing signature systems. Neither a precise arrival date nor an effortless migration is established.
  • Governance and law: Protocol development, tax treatment and restrictions on services can affect use. Satoshi’s identity and exact holdings remain uncertain.
