Life on the Waterfront

The Police Bike is the Waterfront’s robot rider and bike pair. They make good time when radio reception breaks up between buildings. The rider carries written messages between crossings and brings back a person’s answer in their exact words. The bike has had three front mudguards. The rider still has the first one, bent nearly in half and marked with the names of everyone who helped retrieve it.

The distance between two answers

The rider was trained in straight streets with numbered doors. Its first Waterfront route went through a passage locals called the Back of the Oven, which opened beside a bakery at two different heights. It spent half a morning delivering to the correct door on the wrong floor. A shop porter took it over the route again at walking pace and made it name each person it would have to ask when the radio cut out.

The rider likes braking without upsetting a cup and collects bad directions on the back of delivery slips. Sailor Bear can make it laugh by giving three answers to a question that sounded like it wanted one. It dislikes the Police Car’s habit of calling every trip a chance to find a warm seat. At the bakery the rider pays for its tea in loose change, even when the queue behind it has senior officers in it.

The handover

A dispatch officer once praised the pair for arriving before the route closure. The rider accepted the praise, then realised the warning had reached a shop door while the shopkeeper was away. Now it records the handover as well as the arrival. It will wait with a notice long enough for the relief shift to show up.

Off duty

The rider sketches wheel tracks after rain to remember the places carts naturally turn. On a quiet evening it picks one curve and tries to take it smoothly enough to keep a cup of tea upright in the saddlebag.

At the bakery door

During the trial it waited for the incoming bakery crew to repeat the directions before it left. The repeated message exposed a difference between their understanding of “after closing” and the schedule Teddy was about to read aloud. The person who spotted it asked whether they were being paid for time the depot had just assumed they owed. The rider passed the answer back word for word, including the awkward last line.

Its current ambition is a route card with a place for the handover, the reply and the recipient’s permission to use their name. That should keep a good delivery from being mistaken for everybody’s agreement. It is likely to cost some of its fastest round times. Ask it which crossing to take and it will ask what you’re carrying, when you need to arrive and who expects you on the other side.
