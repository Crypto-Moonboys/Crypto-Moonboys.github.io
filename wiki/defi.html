DeFi (Decentralised Finance)

✅ Verified📅 Last updated: October 2026📂 Concepts

Decentralised finance (DeFi) describes financial applications that use blockchain contracts for activities such as exchange, lending and collateral management. It operates across several networks. Some components are open to direct use, while others depend on administrators, issuers, interfaces or service providers. DeFi replaces certain intermediaries with different technical and governance dependencies; it does not remove risk or regulation.

History and origins

Early applications (2017–2019)

MakerDAO’s collateral-backed DAI system, Compound’s lending markets and Uniswap’s exchange helped establish widely used DeFi patterns during 2017–2019. They were important developments, not the invention of every underlying idea. Automated market makers and earlier on-chain exchange designs predated Uniswap, which popularised a particular implementation.

DeFi Summer (2020)

Compound’s COMP distribution drew attention to liquidity mining in 2020. Uniswap v2, Curve, Yearn and SushiSwap developed different approaches to trading and incentives. SushiSwap’s migration campaign encouraged providers to move liquidity; calling that entire event a theft obscures the difference between a competitive incentive and an exploit.

Rapid deposits showed that incentives could attract capital. They did not prove that the same activity would continue when rewards fell. Reported total value locked also changed with asset prices, so it cannot be read simply as new money entering a system.

Expansion and failures (2021–2022)

The 2021 expansion was followed by the 2022 Terra/UST collapse and failures among centralised lenders and trading firms. Their structures differed: a custodial company’s insolvency is not the same mechanism as a collateral or stablecoin failure. Contracts continuing to execute did not mean all users remained solvent or protected from losses.

Later designs and incentives

Later development included revenue-based rewards, liquid staking, restaking, points programmes and new exchange designs. Each changes who bears risk and where payments originate. A new label such as “real yield” does not prove sustainability, and a points balance does not guarantee a future token distribution.

Key mechanisms

Automated market makers

An AMM uses programmed rules and liquidity reserves to quote trades. The constant-product formula x × y = k is one model, not a universal rule. Other designs use different curves, weights or concentrated ranges. Liquidity providers may receive fees while facing inventory losses, contract risk and changing utilisation. Some decentralised exchanges instead use order books.

Lending and collateral

Many lending protocols require borrowers to supply collateral and maintain a specified ratio. Interest and liquidation rules vary. Falling collateral values, oracle updates or debt growth can trigger liquidation under those rules. Congestion, liquidity and implementation can affect execution; liquidation is not universally instantaneous or complete.

Flash loans

A flash loan must be repaid within the same transaction, together with any required fee, or the transaction’s state changes revert. The relevant boundary is the transaction, not an entire block. Failed attempts can still incur network fees. These loans support specialised financial operations but do not create risk-free profit or remove the risks of the applications involved.

Liquidity mining and reward sources

Rewards can come from borrower interest, trading fees, protocol issuance or a treasury allocation. Additional token rewards can attract users without making the underlying activity profitable. APR and APY describe rates under stated assumptions; neither is automatically the inflation rate of the whole token supply. Compounding, fees, price changes and dilution affect realised outcomes.

Oracles and stablecoins

Oracles supply data that a contract cannot observe directly, such as an external market price. A price feed adds assumptions about sources, timing and availability. Stablecoins introduce a different set of dependencies: collateral, reserves, redemption, governance and, in some cases, an issuer able to freeze tokens. A dollar target does not guarantee a dollar redemption.

Bridges and multiple chains

A bridge transfers messages or representations of assets between networks. Security can depend on contracts, external signers, light-client verification or other mechanisms. Using a familiar destination chain does not eliminate those bridge assumptions. A wrapped asset can fail to track its underlying asset if custody or redemption breaks.

Interpreting DeFi measurements

Total value locked usually values assets associated with a protocol at current market prices. Definitions differ and positions can be counted across interacting applications. TVL is not a complete balance sheet, an audit of liabilities or proof that every user can withdraw.

Public transaction records improve inspectability, but do not expose every off-chain agreement, beneficial owner or correlated position. Hidden or hard-to-reconstruct leverage remains possible. Historical volume and deposits should carry dates and methodology rather than appear as permanent measures of safety.

Access and composability

Shared contracts can make settlement rules inspectable and allow applications to use one another’s assets and functions. This can reduce some access barriers and enable financial services without a conventional account at every step.

Practical access still depends on networks, fees, interfaces, supported assets and applicable restrictions. Upgrade administrators, pause functions, token issuers and service providers may freeze or restrict parts of a system. “Decentralised” does not mean every component is beyond anyone’s control.

Risks and trade-offs

  • Faulty contracts, unsafe upgrades and compromised administrators can expose funds. An audit reduces uncertainty but is not a guarantee.
  • Collateral, oracle and liquidation design can produce losses without any software exploit.
  • Liquidity providers can underperform holding the assets separately; fee income may not cover that difference.
  • Bridges, stablecoin issuers and custodial components introduce additional dependencies.
  • Leverage, concentrated governance and correlated collateral can spread failure between applications.
  • Legal and tax obligations depend on jurisdiction and activity; an open contract does not settle them.
