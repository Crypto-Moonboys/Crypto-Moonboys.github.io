Marriage, Partnership, Adoption and Leaving

First WitnessHousehold ethics · Section 47Last updated: October 2026

The 2030 Covenant deliberately avoided creating one universal marriage law. Later communities built different family forms while arguing over the same duties: consent, care, honesty, responsibility and freedom from possession.

No single household model

Because the Concord did not abolish older religions or cultures, marriage customs remained diverse from the beginning. Some communities used religious rites, others civil contracts, clan ceremonies or simple witnessed promises.

The First Witness layer asks a narrower set of questions: did the people consent, are obligations clear, can abuse be challenged, and can leaving occur without social annihilation?

The household ledger

Some couples and shared households record practical obligations before ceremonial language: money, care, housing, children, property, debts, elder support and what happens if the relationship ends.

Romantics mock this as unpoetic. Keeper schools answer that love should not require financial ambiguity to feel real.

Adoption and chosen kin

Common Blood traditions recognise family duties created through adoption, long-term care and chosen kinship. A biological connection can create history without automatically creating trust.

Several Street Kingdom cultures use formal “name-giving” ceremonies to recognise a person who has performed family responsibility over time.

Leaving without erasure

The Fork becomes especially personal in separation. The ideal is not that every former partner remains friends. It is that the end of a relationship does not require rewriting the entire past or deleting the other person's humanity.

Where abuse exists, distance and protection override ceremonial ideals of mutual closure.

Children after the fork

A widely quoted family commentary says: “Do not make the child carry messages between two adults who chose not to speak.”

Children should not become evidence, spies, bargaining chips or judges of which parent was morally pure.

The renewal question

Some partnerships revisit vows periodically and ask whether the promises still describe the relationship they are actually living. The aim is not to make commitment temporary. It is to keep commitment conscious.

A promise never examined can survive as habit after its meaning has died.
