Humour, Satire and Sacred Mockery

First WitnessCultural tradition · Section 61Last updated: October 2026

A religion that cannot survive jokes has usually made somebody's status more sacred than its own principles.

The permitted joke

Most First Witness schools permit satire of leaders, rituals, symbols and faction habits. Mockery is considered one way of preventing ceremonial language from becoming immune to ordinary judgement.

The Crown Test

A common saying claims: “If the leader cannot survive the joke, the crown is already too tight.” Leaders are not required to enjoy ridicule. They are expected not to criminalise every wound to pride.

Mocking down

Satire aimed at powerful institutions is treated differently from entertainment built around humiliating people with little ability to answer back. Context matters more than simply claiming “it was a joke.”

The holy parody

Several sects perform deliberately absurd versions of their own rituals once a year. Keepers may ceremonially guard an empty box; Chain Scribes archive a blank page; Crownless groups crown a chair and then argue with it.

The purpose is to expose how easily form survives after meaning disappears.

Blasphemy arguments

Because the First Witness has no single deity claim, blasphemy laws historically centred more on insults to sacred memory, the dead or community identity. These laws became controversial because authorities repeatedly used them to suppress political criticism.

The joke that reveals the rule

Anthropologists in the setting often learn more from what a faction jokes about than from its official constitution. Humour reveals which rules are negotiable, which wounds remain open and which authorities are genuinely feared.
