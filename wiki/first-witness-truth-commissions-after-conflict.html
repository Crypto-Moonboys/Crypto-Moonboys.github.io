Truth Commissions After Conflict

First WitnessPost-conflict tradition · Section 66Last updated: October 2026

Ending violence does not end the war over memory. Truth commissions emerged because peace agreements repeatedly failed when each side preserved a completely different account of what had happened.

One commission, many archives

First Witness commissions do not begin by writing the final history. They collect competing records, witness testimony, physical evidence and institutional documents into one inspectable archive.

Admission versus prosecution

Some commissions offer reduced punishment for complete testimony. Critics call this cheap justice. Supporters argue that hidden mass wrongdoing may otherwise remain permanently unknown.

Victim testimony

Participation should not become compulsory public trauma. Victims may give private testimony, submit records through representatives or decline entirely.

Names and anonymity

Public naming can establish accountability but can also expose families to retaliation. Different cases therefore use different disclosure levels rather than treating transparency as absolute.

The minority report

When commissioners cannot agree, dissenting conclusions remain attached to the final record. A smooth unanimous report produced through pressure is considered less valuable than visible disagreement.

Peace without amnesia

The central principle is that reconciliation should reduce the political use of memory without requiring memory itself to disappear.
