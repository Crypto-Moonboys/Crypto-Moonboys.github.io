Healing, Illness, Disability and Care

First WitnessCare tradition · Section 56Last updated: October 2026

The Common Blood becomes hardest to fake around bodies that cannot be optimised into usefulness. Illness and disability therefore became major tests of whether a society meant what it said about dignity.

Care is not a debt ledger

Care creates obligations, but the cared-for person does not become property of the carer. Likewise, receiving care does not make a person morally inferior or permanently indebted for existing.

Cure and dignity

Some conditions can be treated. Some cannot. A culture that values only cure can make people feel like failed medical projects.

First Witness care traditions therefore separate the question “Can this be changed?” from “Does this life remain fully human?” The second answer does not depend on the first.

The exhausted carer

The Keeper applies to carers too. Sacrifice without relief can destroy the person providing care. Communities are expected to share difficult care where possible rather than romanticise one exhausted family member as heroic.

Consent and treatment

Medical authority is treated as expertise, not ownership. Patients should receive understandable information and meaningful choice where circumstances allow. Emergency treatment creates harder cases but does not erase the obligation to explain later.

Disability and design

Many later schools distinguish impairment from disabling environments. A staircase may turn limited mobility into exclusion; a communication system may turn hearing difference into isolation.

Repair therefore includes changing environments, not only bodies.

The miracle problem

Stories of extraordinary cures appear throughout the tradition. The Source Register classifies them as testimony, not automatic proof of doctrine. Healers are forbidden from blaming an ill person for lacking sufficient belief when treatment fails.
