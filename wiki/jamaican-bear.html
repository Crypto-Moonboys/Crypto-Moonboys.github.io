Life on the Waterfront

Jamaican Bear inherited a record case with a broken hinge and a paper list of the people who had lent the records inside. He rebuilt the case and took it down to the Waterfront when he heard workers were running a small listening session after the shops closed. Several of the names in the case were still working across the harbor.

A good borrowing voice

The case Jamaican Bear inherited had a clean hinge on one side and a repair on the other that clicked whenever a record lifted the lid too far. He learnt which of the old borrowers could hear that noise from another room. One gave him his first paid hour on a listening shift and asked him to put something good on while she went outside to sit down. He still programmes one record an evening for the person who is sick of hearing their own favourite.

He is careful with other people’s possessions and uncomfortable when that care makes him sound like a customs officer. He wants the Boombox to pick more adventurous songs and hates how quickly it can make him admit a record of his has gone on too long. Chada’s habit of writing down the place of a recording before calling it good has made him start carrying spare pencils.

The watermarked sleeve

He likes a set that makes room for somebody else’s favourite. Once he borrowed a rare pressing from a neighbour and left it backstage long enough for the sleeve to get wet. He spent a month finding a replacement and learned that his neighbour cared most about a note on the original sleeve. He now checks for marks, dates and dedications before he takes a record out of someone’s home.

Off duty

His hobby is mending old sleeves with hands that were never particularly steady. While the glue dries he asks the Walking Boombox for one song he would never pick. The two of them disagree happily about which song that should be.

The note in the sleeve

On the trial evening Jamaican Bear asked for the lender’s note back before allowing the borrowed record on air. He said it with a smile and saw the sound operator’s jaw set. Once, he would have let it go and chased the sleeve by himself at closing. The note had already reached him when the apprentice’s question came through the gap in the track. He held it up so the operator could see what had mattered before he put it away.

He wants the trial sets to become paid bookings with time to agree terms before people start playing. He would also like a walk where he doesn’t talk about a contract all the way to the end. The likely compromise is one quiet side after the last announcement if the next shift goes ahead. He asks visitors what they would lend to someone they might never see again and takes their answer seriously.

He still sells weed between paid sets. When a regular offered to clear what he owed by lending a rare pressing, Jamaican left the record in its sleeve. He knows how quickly a loan can start to feel like a sale when only one person can afford to walk away. He wants that regular back at the listening table even if the debt hangs between them.
