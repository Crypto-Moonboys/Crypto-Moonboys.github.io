Life on the Waterfront

The Police Car is a self-driving Waterfront patrol unit. Dockworkers nicknamed it the Waiting Room after it held its cabin warm for a stranded mechanic while they waited for the evening crossing to clear. Its compact frame and roof lamps are visible above a parked row of freight carts.

How the Waiting Room got its name

The car remembers its earliest service as a succession of places nobody wanted to stay. It could get a report into a dry glove box in less than a minute and once kept a mechanic warm for the length of an entire closed crossing. Afterwards the mechanic brought it a recovered heater dial that reads COLD at both ends. It keeps the dial because the mechanic had known exactly which direction made the seat comfortable.

The car is proud of managing its own rounds and touchy about being told that a parked vehicle is doing nothing. It trades screws with the Hard Fork Rockers’ repair bench and keeps an unlabeled envelope of fares people insist on paying. Gentleman Bear has offered to keep the envelope in his ledger. The car keeps putting off the transfer because he would ask who the money belongs to.

The wrong cart

The car carries dry report forms, spare route boards and a seat for whoever has the longest explanation. It once left a signed removal notice on a cart that belonged to another shift. The driver lost an evening’s work setting the matter straight. These days the car asks the desk to read back a location and a name before it marks a report closed.

Off duty

Between rounds it matches loose dashboard screws to their original fittings. It keeps the cup holder free for its next passenger, even when it has nowhere else to put the screws. Its preferred sign-off is “I’ll leave the light on until you’re through.”

The passenger who kept losing her place

When the Police Car opened its cabin during the upper landing trial, it assumed sitting down helped everyone. The woman who lost her place in the queue taught it to ask whether she wanted company or somebody to keep track of who was next. It began carrying a small seat card with the time a passenger first joined the line. The card can stay in someone’s pocket when they would prefer their waiting to go unannounced.

The car would like to park at the next trial even if it means giving up a lap around the main yard. Dispatch might count that as a missed round. Its strongest argument is that the seat freed a helper to work the landing. It remembers that the woman described it as the first warm room she had sat in since her shift began. Ask it about the dashboard and it will introduce you to the heater dial before it mentions the lamp on its roof.
