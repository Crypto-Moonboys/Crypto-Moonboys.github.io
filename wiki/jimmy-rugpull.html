His uncle’s bad ledger

Jimmy Rugpull is forty, dresses for rain even under a roof, and can tell how badly a person needs cash by the kind of receipt they accept. His uncle stocked chairs, rostrums and red carpet for launches. Jimmy worked for him from his mid-teens, unloading other people’s visions of wealth for a handle of small notes. The uncle made elaborate promises to clients and exacting demands of porters. At home he left a list of wages in the bread tin. Jimmy discovered that a second ledger credited him with all the food and rent his uncle reckoned he owed. By the reckoning in that book, Jimmy would have needed to work there for the rest of his life.

He left with a fold-up trolley and the name of the afternoon shift. He became good at finding reusable material inside deals that had already failed. Early on, he sold seats in a freight scheme he knew might never run. The person arranging the shipment had walked before Jimmy sold the last two seats. Jimmy took the money anyway. One buyer was a cook saving for a stove. She still sells bowls near the pier. Jimmy repaid her deposit in instalments and is quietly relieved she does not think that makes them friends.

A name with a price

The Rugpull Miners make opportunity from the distance between a convincing promise and the thing that can actually be delivered. Their histories include deception, ventures stripped of value and the people who get left holding the loss. Jimmy understands that trade intimately. The nickname began as a warning from the hands who watched him haul the marquee carpets out of a failed opening while the sponsor’s speech was still going. He kept it because denying somebody else’s successful joke seemed like losing twice.

Jimmy speaks for a Waterfront outfit of the Miners that deals in salvage, lifting and short contracts. It includes people who came to the faction for quick money and people whose wages used to get left behind. Everybody wants a cut. He writes terms in ordinary language, gives each worker a copy and takes offence when anybody calls the practice virtue. If somebody starts saying a deposit is unreturnable, Jimmy asks what has already been spent. A number should be able to survive the name of an actual person being said out loud.

Receipts from the night shift

During preparations for a rigging demonstration at the Ninth Berth, Jimmy accepted a sponsor’s reassurances about a relief haul and booked porters against money still on its way. Then the water rose, the demonstration collapsed and the workers were asked to move essential supplies anyway. Jimmy had enough in his own tin to pay only part of the shift. He paid the wages he could and wrote down every balance before anybody went home. Later he tried to claim the margin he had hoped to make. Danny Rankz put the figure on the table beside the signed wage slips and asked what work it represented. Jimmy withdrew it, in language that inspired the clerk to take a longer lunch.

Jack the Bull had sold the haul as proof his crew could deliver where its rivals talked. Jimmy gave Jack twenty minutes to persuade a backer to make good on the unpaid work. Then he sourced an advance from two stallholders on terms that sent their next collection to the front of his ordinary queue. Jack paid the remaining wage balance. Jimmy remembers the payment and the queue. Different people paid for the delay.

Little things in good order

Jimmy has a weakness for restoring old wind-up kitchen timers. He can bring the enamel mark back from rust but tends to leave the dent in the side. He makes breakfast in a heavy pan, keeps a bonsai in a paint tin and tells visitors it’s a forest in a tight financial position. His chess rival Chada Boi has never won a pawn back after accidentally dropping it in Jimmy’s tea. Jimmy replaced a broken pawn with a washer and insisted the position still counted. They now use it as a piece.

He scratches a line through every paid sum, always starting on the right. He hates being thanked while work is still under way. When he is worried about someone, he asks whether they want a sandwich with the angry edge of a man asking who damaged his tools. He can spot a dubious offer in someone else’s kitchen and talk himself into a similar one when his own cupboard is bare. He stays away from loud rooms for a week after a bad deal. Nobby usually knows where to find him anyway.

His next handover

Jimmy wants a yard with a dry clock and a covered place to pay people. He would also like enough capital to buy his own lifting gear so he can stop handing a share to somebody who does not know the weight of anything. The two ambitions compete whenever a dubious job promises to finance both. Danny keeps the early invoice from the Berth. Jimmy respects the decision and would still like the bastard to lose it.

At the Berth, an offer from Jimmy tends to open with what the day will cost in real terms. He reads warranties aloud with no gift for drama and can pick a quarrel over the words future expenses. His favoured answer to a glowing proposal is short enough to fit on a nail head. “Looks smashing. Who’s doing the shift?”

The upholstery job

Jimmy once arranged a paid recovery of theatre seats from a venue that had closed in a hurry. One buyer agreed to pay per intact chair. By the time the last row came loose, the buyer was reclassifying cracked varnish as damage. Jimmy knew this arrangement. He had used it on smaller yards himself. His porters were still working at the full advertised rate, and he could already hear the explanation he would give them about a bad market.

The upholsterer who lived upstairs offered to take the damaged chairs on instalments and asked to choose six to keep for a waiting room. Jimmy wanted eight held back so he could sell the best ones separately after they had been recovered. She walked out. He eventually sent a porter after her with six labels and the good kettle. When he tells the story he tends to leave out how long his friend waited at the roadside for the payment she had already promised on his behalf.
