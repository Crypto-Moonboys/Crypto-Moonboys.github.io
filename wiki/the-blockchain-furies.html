⚔️ The BLOCKCHAIN FURIES

⚔️ Lore 📅 Last updated: October 2026 📂 Lore

The Blockchain Furies insist that a broken world does not cancel the wrongs committed inside it. In Year 3008 they investigate fraud, expose abusive power and seek to reverse gains taken through betrayal. Their anger gives people abandoned by ordinary institutions a force willing to listen and act. It also makes the Furies dangerous when certainty outruns evidence or punishment becomes more important than the life of the person harmed. Their central struggle is whether accountability ends with a target’s defeat or begins a longer, less spectacular obligation to repair.

Adult lore — 21+: The Red Ledger Night sections contain strong language, adult sexual relationships, sex work, drugs, coercion and lethal violence.

Conviction among the Forty

The Furies belong to the Forty faction cultures and operate beyond Block Topia’s official enforcement. The First Witness commentaries connect them to Hold and Fork as militant defence of conviction. Their virtue is courage: they will challenge people whom quieter institutions fear. Their habitual failure is escalation, especially when compromise sounds to them like another request that victims remain patient.

They are not a single court with universally accepted jurisdiction. Crews differ over what makes a case actionable, which remedies are proportionate and whether anybody outside the faction may review their decisions. That disagreement matters because a Fury finding can affect traders, households and services whose users never consented to the faction’s authority.

Nessa and the name that burns back

After the mistaken violence in the Red Ledger Night account, Nessa's standing at the Ash Desk becomes bitterly recognisable. Adults paint a broken red stroke to invoke uncompromising exposure. Others use the stroke to remember what public certainty did to Dala. Nessa sees both versions and cannot remove one without proving the accusation that she wants to control the evidence. Her icon survives as an argument, which some supporters find more compelling than a clean victory.

The Furies can confront a patron everyone else is afraid to name. Their unique leverage is a public accusation backed by collective force. That force makes an overlooked victim audible and makes a mistaken target's ordinary life difficult to continue. A new complainant asks Nessa for the same speed the old case was praised for before its error. She slows the public release and is accused by a supporter of becoming respectable. The supporter leaves to organise a harsher local action elsewhere; the account does not approve his claim to speak for all Furies.

Nessa resents the complaint even while admitting its necessity. She still likes a room that falls quiet when she enters. Dala still has losses no symbol repays. The faction's defining conflict is between courage and appetite for being the avenger. A Fury can be right about a powerful enemy and wrong about what everyone standing nearby deserves. The broken red stroke makes those two truths visible together.

Investigation, exposure and reversal

The dedicated W81 account identifies victims of scams, harassment and lost fortunes as the Furies’ imagined collective origin. It gives them data-forensics, public Digital Trials and fictional Reversal Protocols intended to undo ill-gotten gains. Rugpull Miners are their principal enemies, but their attention also reaches manipulative markets, city secrecy and abuse around the Games.

The archive calls their analysis unrivalled and their evidence irrefutable. Those are claims of authority, not guarantees. A recovered record can be incomplete; a correct fact can support more than one explanation. The faction’s value lies in pursuing questions others avoid, while its danger lies in assuming that willingness to investigate makes its conclusions incapable of error.

Betrayal after the World Chain

The World Chain Triple Fork in 2880 shattered shared recognition of identity, wealth and public record. Some losses resulted from collapse; others were caused or enlarged by people exploiting that collapse. The Furies’ origin account refuses to let the general catastrophe erase the difference. Its imagined survivors carry the anger of seeing responsible people disappear behind systemic failure.

The claim that the Genesis Kernel fused betrayed minds into one avenging culture remains a mythic account, not every member’s literal biography. By 2930, fewer than a dozen secure regions remained and the Queens stronghold underlay Block Topia. The Furies of 3008 act across the later city and outside societies, inheriting disputes that neither reliable records nor a single accepted court can easily settle.

What arrives at a Fury desk

A complaint is rarely a complete case. People arrive with broken receipts, contradictory witnesses, missing belongings and a story rehearsed so often that they no longer know which details they observed directly. Others cannot bear to tell the story again. The Furies’ reputation draws both urgent claims and attempts to recruit their force into an ordinary rivalry.

Good intake work turns neither confidence nor distress into proof. It identifies what the complainant wants: explanation, return of goods, protection, public acknowledgement or punishment. Those aims can conflict. A person who needs a workplace to reopen may not want the triumphant shutdown that an angry supporter imagines on their behalf.

The Ash Desk

The Ash Desk is a local Fury receiving room in the outside territories. It takes its name from the faction’s older Ash Forge imagery, but it is a worked-in room with benches, storage and a place to sit through a difficult account. Its reach is limited by available witnesses, labour and the willingness of neighbouring communities to recognise its findings.

Nessa Trace, a listener and claimant advocate, keeps the first account separate from later interpretations. She joined after watching a real loss within the story dismissed because the victim lacked a presentable record. Her patience is selective: she can tolerate confused testimony more easily than an official who asks somebody to begin again for the fourth time.

Who carries a case

At the Ash Desk, a case passes between people responsible for listening, comparing records, contacting affected parties and proposing a remedy. These are working roles rather than a universal Fury constitution. A small crew may combine several duties, creating pressure when the same person becomes investigator, advocate and judge.

Omi Calder assesses what restitution would require. Omi’s practical questions irritate colleagues eager for a public finding: where will replacement goods come from, who still needs wages and what happens if an asset being claimed supports an unrelated household? Omi does not excuse wrongdoing. The questions make it harder to confuse destruction of the offender’s property with repair of the victim’s life.

Records and their limits

The Furies work with fragments from several traditions. The collapsed World Chain’s civic records, Block Topia’s True Bitcoin Fork and the Sacred/Aether memory tradition are not interchangeable systems. A trace that can be connected to one record may still lack the context required to establish responsibility in another.

The established distinction between trace, fragment, interpretation and reconstruction helps the Ash Desk describe uncertainty. It does not make the desk immune to bias. Nessa can recognise an omitted voice while missing the omission that favours her preferred conclusion. The most useful record is sometimes the one that survives long enough to challenge the people who originally preserved it.

Digital Trials and the audience

W81’s Digital Trials turn evidence into public exposure. Publicity can prevent a powerful actor from quietly burying a complaint. It can also make correction difficult once an audience has learned the first accusation as a memorable story. The size of the audience is no measure of the completeness of the case.

Some Fury crews regard public confrontation as essential. Others publish a narrower finding first and leave contested parts open. The Blockstars can amplify either approach, bringing resources and attention while rewarding drama. A witness may want to be believed without becoming a public emblem whose words are repeated long after the witness has asked for quiet.

Reversal Protocols

Reversal Protocols are the fictional punitive power most closely associated with the Furies. The archive describes the cancellation of fraudulent gains, speculative claims and hoarded wealth. Their narrative purpose is to make an apparently final theft answerable; they are not a universal mechanism for recovering any loss or instructions for interfering with real accounts.

Reversal creates its own questions. What if the contested goods have changed hands? What if an apparently corrupt account also pays innocent workers? W81’s Ducky Boys account explicitly places repairers around harms requiring reversal work for innocent victims. That complication prevents the Furies’ preferred story from ending at the moment a target loses power.

The Six Windows reversal

In the Six Windows reversal, a local Fury crew acts against a supplier accused of taking payments for unavailable components. The public account identifies real discrepancies. Its remedy also interrupts obligations serving six neighbouring workshops whose owners were neither investigated nor heard before the announcement.

Nessa supports the finding at first. Omi’s visits reveal that the interruption prevents ordinary wages and repairs while leaving some complainants no closer to receiving what they bought. The crew narrows its claim, arranges limited restoration with repairers and publicly acknowledges the affected workshops. Several losses remain unpaid. The supplier’s exact responsibility for all missing goods is still contested, so the case cannot be reduced to either a perfect victory or a wholly invented accusation.

The Second Hearing

The Ash Desk’s Second Hearing takes place after an intervention. It asks what changed for the complainants and for other people affected. At Six Windows, this produces testimony nobody included in the first broadcast: a worker who went without pay, a household whose promised repair was delayed and a complainant who welcomed the exposure but still needed a replacement part.

Some Furies treat the hearing as an attack on colleagues who accepted danger to act. Others see it as the point at which courage becomes accountable. The hearing has no authority over the whole faction. It remains an uncomfortable local institution because its most important findings often reduce the simplicity of the story that first united the crew.

The Restitution Bench

The Restitution Bench is where Omi works through practical returns, replacement labour and disputed valuations. An object’s market price does not capture the work someone must repeat when it is lost. Nor can a promise of future labour reliably replace money a household needs today. The bench records those differences instead of presenting a single total as an automatic settlement.

At Six Windows, available goods satisfy some claims but not others. The crew contributes labour to one repair and argues over whether its own error makes it responsible for more. People leave with different views of what justice achieved. The incomplete result gives the bench its purpose: a case remains consequential after public attention has moved elsewhere.

Who supports the investigators

Investigation needs food, rooms, transport, interpreters and time. The Finance Guild funds anti-fraud work in W81, particularly against the Rugpull Miners. That support can make difficult cases possible while favouring wrongs legible as financial disorder over harms that do not threaten a wealthy patron’s records.

The Ash Desk accepts limited contributions and keeps its open costs visible to participating households. This does not eliminate dependence. A donor can withdraw; an overworked listener can leave. Nessa dislikes charging complainants whose loss brought them to the desk, but unpaid work can exclude people who cannot afford to become investigators. There is no pure funding arrangement waiting just beyond the next argument.

Enemies and unstable partnerships

The Rugpull Miners embody the betrayal that drives the Furies. Their conflict is enduring, but a faction label cannot settle a particular person’s guilt. Bally brokers may supply useful information about a rival and later become subjects of inquiry themselves. Information Mercenaries sell valuable accounts while trading with opposing interests.

The AllCity Bulls attract scrutiny where competition becomes manipulation. Moonlord propaganda can waste investigators’ attention through spectacular distractions. The Furies’ challenge is to pursue identifiable conduct without allowing a satisfying enemy image to decide what every piece of evidence must mean.

The dedicated account takes the Miner pursuit beyond recovery of goods: it says the Furies hope to destroy the Miners’ fragmented consciousness permanently. This severe aspiration is part of the vigilante tradition, not an established sentence against every individual. It sharpens the difference between restoring a claimant’s life and making vengeance irreversible.

Games testimony and the people inside it

W81 places the Furies around Feast Week investigating wealth, resources and the treatment of women assigned the “Yellow Layer.” Their concern is the gap between the city’s public promises and the specialised account of classification and exploitation. Intervention belongs to that narrative; it is not proof that every identified person escaped or that the Furies understood the whole system.

The source dismisses the Bitcoin Kid Army as a domestic dispute outside the faction’s main concern. That narrow view does not erase the escaped and outside Bitcoin Kids or merge them with the inside Bitcoin X Kids. The existing Games witnesses retain their own histories. Jodie and Aleema are not retroactively made Fury recruits, and the third rejected recruit remains unnamed.

The same source qualifies that indifference by describing close observation of the outside Army’s networks, whose activity can expose Rugpull Miners and corrupt Block Node Defenders. The Furies may use what the rebels reveal while refusing to regard their wider cause as their own; observation is not an alliance.

Witness Supper and the Unclosed Book

A local Witness Supper lets people who carried a case meet without performing it again for an audience. Conversation can turn to work, family or an ordinary joke. Some survivors want the case remembered constantly; others value a room where their loss is not the first thing said about them. Nessa tries to make space for both.

The Unclosed Book records matters whose evidence or remedy remains incomplete. It is not a list of people permanently suspected. At the Ash Desk, names can be withheld from its public summary while the unresolved question remains visible. The book prevents a shortage of attention from being mistaken for a finding that nothing happened.

The White Shutter cart

A Fury team intervenes when a carrier's disputed stock is being moved into an inspection cage as a failed lot. Tern Vale's held-lot record supports the challenge: inspection has not yet established failure. The team prevents the classification from being completed without a hearing. A carrier retains a real claim that the city machinery was about to make harder to recover.

The same intervention blocks a cart containing part of the sealed clinic delivery. Its receiving shift closes, the stock returns for the next shift, and a household loses another night of expected provision. The team had not intended that result. It still follows from its decision to stop the shared cart instead of first separating the loads. A correction saved for one person has imposed a delay on another.

The Restitution Bench hears both accounts. It does not reverse the true correction merely because the team caused harm. It records the missed shift and a compensation obligation whose actual funding remains limited. Some supporters want to publish only the successful challenge. Case workers insist that the delayed household belongs in the same record. The faction's conviction becomes more credible by taking responsibility for its own choice, and that responsibility does not magically pay the household's loss.

The Final Audit

NULL threatens the Furies by attacking the records through which accountability can be attempted. Their Final Audit is the ambition to find an origin and a comprehensible cause even in the Antichain’s contradictions. It reflects determination, not a demonstrated ability to explain or end the threat.

Their arguments with HODL collective defence concern the balance between preserving what remains and actively investigating what destroys it. HODL X Warriors stay a distinct tradition. No Fury inquiry has completed the Final Fork, and a future without usable evidence would not retroactively make past harm unreal.

Grief Chains and Ash Forges

The old wiki gives the Furies cracked-coin and burned-ledger marks, a blood-coded oath, Grief Chains and Ash Forges. These stories turn pain into visible belonging. The most extreme versions require every recruit to relive a personal ruin and make vengeance compulsory until every wrong is righted. They remain attributed traditions, not universal initiation requirements or literal limits on a person’s ability to leave.

Their remembered symbols are powerful because loss can isolate people. A mark can tell a survivor that someone else will listen. It becomes coercive when the faction demands continued anger as proof of authenticity. Nessa keeps her own mark while allowing a visitor to cover theirs, an ordinary choice that some of her colleagues still find difficult to accept.

Jex at the Ash Desk: vengeance with the wrong body

Nessa Trace is 40 and Omi Calder is 47 in the new Red Ledger Night account. News of the porter-door killing arrives with an attempted faction story already attached: a cheated worker finally fought back. The account omits the hired porter who died. Nessa asks for his name before accepting another account of the unpaid debt. Jex Sable was 41, had a partner and worked the door. He was not an avatar cast into the scene to demonstrate how angry everybody else had become.

Dala Sable brings the wage note and asks who holds Jex's coat. She does not offer to become a permanent Fury witness, recount her relationship to a public room or endorse a punishment. Nessa initially gives her a long explanation of what the desk can pursue. Dala interrupts: 'He went to work. I asked for his fucking coat.' Nessa stops talking and contacts the worker who took his belongings. The practical answer arrives before the desk has an elegant case title.

Bex's outstanding Empty Dividend loss remains real and separate from responsibility for his blows. The Ash Desk preserves the older evidence rather than handing Rafe an exoneration through someone else's violence. It also refuses to call Jex's death a necessary price of confronting fraud. One angry colleague says this splits the movement. Omi asks which part of the movement needed a porter to die. There is no satisfactory answer from the people who liked the first headline.

The desk contributes accounts to the locally recognised hearing process. Bex remains held pending it; a final sentence is not established here. The holding consumes workers and rooms, and access to representation remains difficult. Tor Pell's earlier Dry Step term is not applied by analogy as if every violent case had the same answer. Nobody closes the matter through an automatic sentence, forced mediation or claim that a memory trace reveals intent beyond doubt.

The public name Nessa lets escape

Nessa wants to challenge Daro's use of the private record. In an early public case notice, she identifies the affected people more fully than Ione agreed to be identified. The notice does not reproduce the stolen interval, but its naming is enough to draw another visitor to Ione's next workplace. Nessa has made the case easier to follow and made its subject easier to bother. The mistake is her decision, not a NULL disruption conveniently absolving the investigator.

Ione comes to the Ash Desk furious. 'You all need me naked in a different fucking way,' she says. She means exposure, not an invitation to describe her body. Nessa removes the unnecessary identifiers from the desk's notice, contacts its known recipients with the correction and cannot retrieve every retelling. She initially says she was trying to stop the sale. Ione answers that she already understood the intention and still had to meet the visitor it brought.

Nessa pays a limited lost-work amount from her own available reserve and logs the desk's contribution separately. Ione accepts the money without accepting an apology as a full settlement. Further work losses cannot be safely attributed to one notice rather than the earlier packet, and the desk does not invent a precise total because a figure would make its remorse more impressive. Nessa's household postpones a planned fitting replacement; the inconvenience does not entitle her to gratitude.

The crew restricts the next notice to the desk's sale-related finding and unresolved handling questions. Some colleagues complain that the narrower statement lets a patron remain comfortable. Nessa agrees that he remains comfortable and no longer offers Ione's life as the price of making him less so. The correction is late, limited and necessary. It does not turn an investigator who exposed somebody into the scene's quiet hero.

Dala’s refusal and the money nobody calls enough

Omi helps collect Jex's earned wages through the hire account. Daro's separate contribution toward practical support arrives with a proposed statement about his concern for all workers. Dala accepts the support on terms that do not require signing his statement. Daro lets the payment stand because withdrawing it would look worse. The money helps with immediate costs. Dala calls it too little and keeps it. Needing help does not make her endorsement available for purchase.

The desk can support a claim, record expenses and seek recognition from neighbours. It cannot command every patron's reserve or immediately replace Jex's future earnings. Bex's small remaining property cannot meet the proposed support amount; taking it would also affect a household already living with an unpaid loss. That shortage does not excuse his act. It makes the promised repair smaller than the harm, which Omi says without putting a hopeful slogan underneath.

Dala declines the Witness Supper and a Rocker memorial verse. Later she chooses one ordinary visit to Low Tide with a friend. These decisions are hers at those times. They do not amount to a universal rejection of company, a final forgiveness or an obligation to teach other bereaved people how to recover. Nessa does not publish the visit as evidence that support is working.

At the Ash Desk, the case has separate open entries: Jex's death, Bex's responsibility and pending hearing, outstanding Empty Dividend obligations, the stolen private interval, Daro's coercion and the desk's own exposure of Ione. Several overlap; none is a solvent for the others. Nessa keeps a Fury mark on her sleeve and sometimes wants the simple revenge her old story promised. On those evenings she leaves the notice pen to another worker before going home angry. The Unclosed Book still has more work than anyone can fund.

The Scarlet Intermission

A Fury gathering in the later 3008 season was interrupted by a man called Korr Sable, 46, who had previously supplied names to a predatory patron and wanted to explain his reasons. The room contained adults who had lost work after those names circulated. Nessa allowed him to speak, partly because she wanted to hear his account and partly because she wanted witnesses to see him attempt it. He described pressure, debt and a household he feared losing. None of the circumstances made the names cease to be recognisable.

Someone struck him before he finished. Nessa stopped a second attack and received a blow herself from an adherent who called the interruption betrayal. Korr survived with a badly injured hand and left under a separate host's protection. The attacker was excluded from that gathering and welcomed by a more aggressive circle. People later called the gap in the speech the Scarlet Intermission. Some meant the point when an excuse deserved to end; others meant the point when the room stopped hearing anything it might need to understand.

Rage does not make everybody kin

Fury friendship can feel intoxicating because a person who has been told to calm down finally finds a room willing to be furious beside them. Adults wear the broken red stroke over repaired finery, interrupted love notes and damaged banners. They make obscene jokes about patrons who expected wounded people to express themselves politely. Their loyalty can make private grief bearable and make a friend's later doubt sound like abandonment.

Nessa has no desire to become the room's endlessly available weapon. Admirers want the woman who names a powerful wrong; lovers may want the woman whose evening is allowed to concern something else. She enjoys being desired for her force and hates discovering that somebody has been waiting for a quarrel simply to watch her perform it. A partner who asks for a quiet night can seem disloyal while she is still living inside an accusation she cannot put down.

The faction's most dangerous internal claim is that loving the injured requires accepting their preferred punishment. The Scarlet Intermission leaves that claim contested. Nessa's earlier mistaken accusation remains a warning she cannot wear away with a more magnificent red mark. Korr's hand stays injured after his account becomes unfashionable. The people who rejected the second attack still have to live alongside people who consider stopping it the only shameful act of the evening.
