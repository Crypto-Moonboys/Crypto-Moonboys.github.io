Life on the Waterfront

Gentleman Bear keeps a ledger of lost things. His bowler and dark suit have persuaded more than one visitor that he represents an official property office. He got into the work after a luggage trolley carrying a singer’s handwritten set list was sent to a salvage room. The set list was intact, but finding it took most of an evening.

A ledger nobody asked him to keep

The lost-property counter began as a dry stretch of bench near the ferry. Gentleman Bear kept a set list there until the singer could say what got written in pencil on the back. He gave it up with more ceremony than either of them had expected. People still offer him official-looking stamps and an occasional box of things they want off their premises. He has learnt to ask whether the box was taken out at closing or brought in by the owner.

It matters to him that people can describe a repair nobody else would notice. Posh Bear gets impatient when he reads from three pads to establish where a tiny object was left. He knows he can drive a customer away by asking for an exact memory when exhaustion would explain a rough one. His favourite possessions are receipts that say who expects to come back.

Who can claim a lantern

People sometimes ask him to hand an item over in reliance on a job title. He much prefers a description of the thread under the handle or the repair to the zip. The system once kept a borrowed lantern away from a person who urgently needed it. He now makes a record when he lends out a lost item before its owner is found and takes responsibility for getting it back.

Off duty

He presses old ferry and workshop receipts flat beneath his dinner plates. On a quiet shift he can spend an hour working out which receipts were issued from the same pad. Posh Bear calls it an excellent way to turn a small pile of paper into a large one.

One drawer out of two

During the trial he knew the stallholder’s drawer handle from its copper binding. She wanted both drawers and he could only account for one. He has thought a lot about a moment when he almost congratulated her on solving the mystery. Danny’s table let him write a return and leave the missing entry open. He scored through the line that had called the drawer unclaimed and made sure he had her permission to use the small detail that helped him recognise it.

He is thinking of keeping a second shelf for objects recovered from the flood, with a private mark for anyone who wants to look without announcing why. The shelf will take up room he used to offer without asking anyone’s permission. If a visitor describes a lost object by its price, he will ask how they fixed it the last time it broke.

He sells weed with the same disarming courtesy he uses at the lost-property table. A customer once tried to leave a borrowed lantern with him to cover a debt. He sent the lantern back to its owner and carried the debt himself. After the hearing he takes particular care to learn who chose to give something up and who will come looking for it tomorrow.
