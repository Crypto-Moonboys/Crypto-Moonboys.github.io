The Bitcoin Witness

First WitnessProvenance history · Section 02Last updated: October 2026

The phrase “minted on Bitcoin” survived for nearly a thousand years. The technical record is less theatrical: the Covenant was given a public cryptographic witness so later rulers could not quietly replace its founding text.

Why paper was not enough

The Concord delegates expected translation disputes, political pressure and forged editions. They had watched archives disappear behind private servers, corporations change ownership and public records mutate under legal or commercial pressure. A physical master could be stolen or burned. A central digital archive could be altered by whoever inherited the keys.

The answer was not to trust code as sacred. It was to make alteration visible. A cryptographic digest of the ratified Covenant, together with a provenance manifest describing its ordering, was committed to Bitcoin. Surviving technical accounts disagree on the exact inscription method and whether later full-text fragments were also placed directly on-chain. The First Witness locks the principle while leaving those implementation details disputed.

Witness, not god

Bitcoin was useful because no bishop, ministry or company could issue a replacement history by decree. That did not make the network a deity, prophet or church. The Covenant itself warns against confusing a tool that preserves evidence with the truth that evidence describes.

A chain can prove that a particular file existed. It cannot prove every claim inside that file is morally right. It can preserve sequence. It cannot decide justice. This distinction later became one of the main arguments against both chain worship and chain nihilism.

The First Witness package

Later archivists reconstructed three layers: the human-readable Covenant; a canonical ordering manifest defining the accepted Books and sections; and one or more cryptographic commitments linking those files to the Bitcoin record. Copies could circulate anywhere, but a claimed 2030 edition had to reconcile with the witness.

This architecture did not prevent counterfeit scriptures from gathering followers. It prevented them from quietly becoming the original. The distinction between “a later interpretation” and “the 2030 witnessed text” could survive even when institutions could not.

How minting entered the language

Early documents preferred words such as witnessing, anchoring, sealing and committing. Street language won. People said the Bible had been minted. The phrase was technically loose but culturally durable because it captured the idea of a text receiving a public, irreversible point of origin.

By 3008, “First Witness” can mean the original Covenant, the Bitcoin provenance event or the wider religious-historical tradition. Readers have to use context rather than assume every use means the same thing.

The irony that followed

The oldest decentralised witness preserved a text warning against dependence on one keeper. Centuries later, civilisation built the World Chain and moved money, identity, law and public memory into one integrated settlement reality. The Bitcoin Witness preserved the warning. It did not force anyone to obey it.
