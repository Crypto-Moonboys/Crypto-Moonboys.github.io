Migration, Refuge, Exile and Borders

First WitnessMigration ethics · Section 57Last updated: October 2026

The Common Blood protects the stranger, but the Keeper reminds communities that resources, safety and trust are not imaginary. Migration ethics therefore became one of the tradition's most disputed fields.

The stranger is human before becoming a category

Refugees, migrants, travellers and outsiders retain human dignity before any legal status is decided. This does not automatically settle who may remain where. It sets the moral floor beneath the argument.

Borders are not sins by themselves

Book II says draw a boundary before drawing blood. Boundaries can prevent conflict, organise responsibility and protect communities. They become dangerous when crossing them strips a person of moral status.

The host's burden

Host communities may face real costs: housing pressure, resource competition, security concerns and cultural disruption. First Witness ethics rejects both pretending those costs do not exist and using them to justify dehumanisation.

The guest's duty

Hospitality does not exempt newcomers from ordinary obligations. Learning local rules, avoiding exploitation and contributing where possible are treated as part of reciprocal belonging.

Exile

Forced exile is regarded as a severe punishment because it attacks identity, family, language and memory at once. Some factions historically used exile in place of execution, creating long-running arguments over whether exile is mercy or slow social death.

Return

A right to return may conflict with people who built lives during an absence. The tradition therefore resists simple “restore everything exactly” solutions where multiple generations now hold real claims.
