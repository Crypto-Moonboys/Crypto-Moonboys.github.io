The FOMO Plague

🌙 Community Lore 📅 Last updated: October 2026 📂 Lore
📋 Contents

The FOMO Plague is HODL Wars’ story of urgency spreading faster than understanding. Its archive forms include panic selling, a rush toward apparent opportunity and a swarm of digital locusts wearing familiar masks. In the 3008 setting, its pressure reaches beyond a market: people fear losing a place, a hearing or the approval of their own group, and a hurried message begins making decisions for them.

Urgency that begins to command

The FOMO Plague takes its name from fear of missing out. HODL Wars gives that familiar pressure a hostile life: messages hurry ahead of their evidence, a crowd’s excitement becomes a demand, and hesitation is treated as proof that someone does not belong. The older page described panic buying, abandoned research and copied influencer claims. Within the fiction, those behaviours are signs used by storytellers, not a clinical symptom list.

The archive contains a useful tension. Its short subject register says the Plague spreads panic selling and weakens Paper Hands. The Whale Lords chronicle describes sudden enticing gains, a rush to join, and collapse afterward. These are different moments in an account of contagious urgency. They should not be forced into a rule that fear always makes people buy or always makes them sell.

The defining pressure is narrower: act now, accept the crowd’s explanation and postpone the questions. A person may feel that pressure even when no money is involved. A place in a shelter, a promised audience, a public title or the chance to stand with a respected group can become the object everybody fears losing. That wider reach gives the Plague a role in the 3008 world beyond a reenacted price chart.

The locust-mask tradition

In The Chronicles of the Whale Lords, the Plagues appear as sentient digital locusts wearing neon GraffPUNKS masks. The swarm turns distributed pressure into an image a reader can see. It descends first on the supposedly weak and then tests the Diamond Hands. This is the harsh morality of that telling, which describes the ordeal as removing weakness and rewarding fidelity.

The mask does not establish that GraffPUNKS as a faction created or spread the Plague. A hostile image can borrow a familiar face. Nor does the image decide whether every outbreak is a literal swarm, a perception under stress or a later artist’s representation of collective behaviour. The sources do not provide one universal mechanism.

The same chronicle attributes powerful influence to the Whale Lords and describes a Triple Crown Dip. Its confidence that hidden actors coordinate the entire sequence belongs to the narrative. It does not prove a single controller behind every local rush, still less a real conspiracy behind every market movement. The Plague can cause harm through copied mistakes and ordinary incentives as well as through deliberate manipulation.

The Green Bell

The Green Bell is a new local Plague account in the 3008 setting. A covered exchange court has a bell used to announce that a shared notice is ready to be read. During a crowded afternoon, a hurried visitor tells notice-reader Sava Pell that a nearby hall will offer a limited number of spaces for an evening presentation. The visitor has heard it from someone who saw a partly covered poster.

Sava has not seen the poster. She nevertheless rings the bell because the story sounds like something people should know before it is too late. By the time she begins explaining its uncertainty, the first listeners have already moved toward the hall. Their departure becomes evidence for the next listeners that the opportunity must be real. The sound carries farther than the qualification.

The hall is actually preparing a rehearsal with no public admission. People arrive expecting a scarce offer and interpret the closed door as proof that places have already been taken. A queue forms anyway. Some abandon ordinary collections and appointments to keep their position. Nobody needs to order them to wait; the visible investment of everyone ahead makes leaving feel increasingly costly.

When Sava reaches the hall with the correction, she is asked why she used the bell at all. She answers publicly that she treated urgency as a reason to announce before checking. The answer does not instantly disperse the queue. Some people suspect a private admission arrangement, and others are reluctant to accept that they have lost an afternoon. The Green Bell records that awkward interval between correcting a statement and repairing the decisions it has already shaped.

The Unmade Broadcast

A sound editor named Olen Kest has recorded the gathering outside the hall. The recording catches the bell, an excited shout and a fragment of Sava’s correction. Olen begins assembling a dramatic sequence in which the crowd appears to receive the same message at once. It would make a powerful Plague story, complete with the bell as an apparent activation signal.

A cleaner who was present hears the rough version and points out that the correction arrived after a long period of waiting. Moving it earlier makes the crowd look as if it knowingly rejected clear information. Olen checks the recording against the cleaner’s account and finds that the sequence has compressed distinct stages into a single foolish moment. The audience’s preferred explanation has begun shaping the event a second time.

Olen abandons the dramatic version and prepares a slower account. It includes pauses during which nothing conclusive happens, a mistaken estimate of the queue’s size and the fact that different people heard different versions of the offer. The piece is less widely circulated. Some listeners complain that it lacks a clear villain; others recognise how easily they could have joined the same queue.

The original edit is kept with a note explaining why it was not released. The episode becomes known as the Unmade Broadcast. Its subject is not only the people who were hurried into action, but the later storyteller who was hurried toward certainty. A warning about the Plague can reproduce its pressure if it demands an immediate and satisfying explanation.

The Two Queues

The Two Queues follows Mara Brill, who manages collections at the exchange court. While the hall queue grows, several people leave the ordinary collection line to join it. Others remain because they need material promised for household repairs. Mara worries that her court will look deserted and sends a helper to tell everyone that their place may be lost if they do not return immediately.

The message creates a second urgency. People begin moving between the hall and the court, afraid of missing either possibility. A resident caring for an injured relative cannot keep making the journey and asks Mara to state what has actually changed. Nothing has changed about the agreed collection. The threat to the resident’s place arose from Mara’s concern about the court’s appearance.

Mara withdraws the threat and arranges a later collection for people affected by the confusion. The adjustment costs additional work and does not suit every resident. She records that cost as part of the court’s own mistaken notice, rather than charging it entirely to those who followed it. The injured relative remains outside the public account; care needs are acknowledged without making private details the price of being believed.

The Two Queues shows how people resisting one rush can create another. Mara is not selling an imaginary opportunity, and she has a useful service to maintain. Her mistake is to make the service’s credibility depend on visible attendance. The correction requires her to tolerate an emptier court while its real obligations continue.

The older Great FOMO Events

The legacy article associated the Plague with Dogecoin excitement, the NFT boom and the Terra/UST collapse. Those references explain the market culture from which the metaphor developed. They do not make every participant’s motives identical or reduce complex events to a single emotion. The archive is not used here as independent verification of precise financial losses, individual intentions or claims about real people.

W11’s Triple Crown Dip is a particularly elaborate hybrid. It places a coordinated-wallet story, a supposed seventy-two-hour mural response and named creators inside a triumphal account of survival. Those details remain attributed archive storytelling. They do not establish that a named living creator performed an otherwise unverified intervention, and the title is unrelated to the World Chain’s Triple Fork of 2880.

The Hard Fork Shield appears in the same chronicle as a supposedly protective staking mechanism. Its presence in prose is not evidence of a functioning service, compensation promise or immunity token. The fictional Plague entry cannot establish current product behaviour. Similarly, the archive’s anticipated phygital rewards are narrative claims rather than benefits this page offers a reader.

These limits do not erase the imagery. They keep the locusts, masks and whales available as stories while preventing their vividness from becoming a substitute for a record of what actually happened outside the fiction.

The myth of a perfect inoculation

The older page called DYOR the only proven inoculation and described Diamond Hands as largely immune. Research can improve a decision, but the lore provides no perfect protective state. Someone who knows a subject well can still be tired, rushed, deceived or anxious about belonging. An expert can also become especially reluctant to admit a mistake after being praised for immunity.

The Green Bell’s response therefore remains ordinary and limited. Sava distinguishes what she has seen from what a visitor reports. Mara states which collection terms have actually changed. Olen keeps the gaps that his first edit tried to remove. None acquires a permanent shield against future error. Their practices work only while people continue doing the work and making correction possible.

The First Witness Hold says to keep doubt beside belief and evidence that one’s own side behaved badly. Those instructions matter here because the Plague’s most effective pressure often comes from a trusted group. People do not need to be outsiders or enemies to repeat a misleading message. Loyalty can include refusing to pass it on in its most flattering form.

The language of plague also needs a boundary. A distressed visitor is not an infection to remove from a community. The metaphor concerns pressure, stories and decisions, not a class of contaminated people. Assistance and a chance to explain should not depend on proving that one has never been hurried into a mistake.

After the bell is quiet

The court eventually resumes its ordinary rhythm. The hall holds its rehearsal, delayed by the crowd at its door. Some residents collect their materials late; others lose work time that nobody can fully replace. Sava continues as a notice-reader under a shared arrangement rather than being erased from the court’s history as the embodiment of the error.

One later poster shows a neon locust perched on a bell. Visitors disagree about whether it mocks Sava or warns about the whole court’s conduct. She asks that the accompanying note name the copied rumour, the premature announcement and the competing collection threat. The picture remains, but it can no longer make a single person carry every part of the story.

The episode closes without identifying a hidden mastermind or proving that all future outbreaks have been prevented. It gives the FOMO Plague something more particular than a moral about foolish crowds: a chain of understandable choices, an inconvenient correction and losses that outlast the excitement.
