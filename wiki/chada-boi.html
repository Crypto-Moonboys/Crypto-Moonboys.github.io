The boy who put a drawer on trial

Chada Boi is a thirty-two-year-old Crypto Stoned Boy, a collector of sounds that get thrown away, and the Waterfront’s most infuriating witness. He grew up over a second-hand shop that tested door chimes on the same rented strip of counter. His first archive was a tea tin of recordings: a sad six-note bell, a worn hinge, his stepfather buttering toast before the rest of the house got up. When a customer insisted a till drawer was haunted, Chada convened a hearing and gave the drawer thirty seconds to answer. It opened halfway through and hit him on the hip. The audience decided that counted as contempt.

The tag stuck at an open-mic night when he forgot the name under which he had entered, turned his mug around and read the stamp on the bottom. He carried “Chada Boi” away with a second-place voucher and a mild dislike of the man who came first. The spelling survived because his friends put it on a tape before he could object. He is most at ease in rooms where the prize can be eaten or given back.

A faction that leaves room for doubt

The Crypto Stoned Boys make an art of contemplation, absurd questions and glitch satire. Chada takes seriously the idea that paying attention can be a public service. When somebody brings a strange object to an evening table, he asks for three things: what the owner saw, what they think it means and whether anybody else was there. Then he makes a pot of tea. He is happy to spend a night talking about a ghost. He gets cross when a ghost story is used to collect money from somebody who can barely keep a room.

One of his early cassette shows set out to satirise a powerful building supervisor. Chada borrowed a lodger’s account of a cupboard falling without noticing that the account identified her through the other people in the room. The supervisor dismissed her. Chada removed the recording and found her a temporary bed. She said she would have preferred to keep the job. He still tells that story at shows, with her permission and with his own name given before anyone else’s.

Two bells, one cold night

On the night the tide came in twice, Chada was recording the harbour rain from a covered loading lip. The berth bell sounded the same interval twice. He held both recordings while his friends argued about the story they wanted to tell. Danny Rankz asked what time his recorder was on. Chada admitted he had never matched it to the berth clock. It could establish the order of the sounds and the gap between them. A claim about the exact minute needed another witness.

His favourite explanation involves a bell embarrassed to have interrupted someone. In public he says the power cut made the equipment repeat and the rising water was real. The recording also caught somebody crying inside the service shed. He retained the original for the hearing and made a public copy that ends just before the cry. A festival wanted to play the whole recording. He walked out on the fee and complained about losing it to everyone for a fortnight.

Pocket museum

Chada plays slow chess with Jimmy Rugpull on a board missing two black pawns. One is a washer and the other is a bead. He will gladly spend fifteen minutes deciding which substitute has the more sinister intentions. He rolls paper boats from unpaid adverts and races them down gutters after storms. He repairs pocket recorders by borrowing Nobby’s tools, then writes improvised jingles to apologise when he brings them back in the wrong drawer. His private pride is a collection of twenty distinct imitations of doors closing.

He is habitually late to gatherings with a start time and early to funerals. At a bad meeting he asks a seemingly daft question that often sends everyone back to the point they skipped. At a good meeting he can ruin the pace by doing exactly the same thing. Eddie Stax can hear the difference between their silences now. It took several years, a broken cassette deck and a fight about which of them had permission to keep a dead friend’s voice.

The things he can afford to hear

He runs a small listening table at the Ninth Berth. Visitors who bring an object can leave their version with it, challenge a previous account or simply go home. His greatest ambition is a floating night library with a listening booth nobody can hire exclusively. His greatest fear is opening one and losing so much money that he starts putting his best stories behind a gate. Chada measures plans by whether he could explain them over a cold meal without making the cook feel stupid.

Chada can be a kind companion to someone whose account has been laughed out of a room. He can also keep saying “maybe” after a person needs him to commit. A tenant called Pell learned the difference when Chada refused to back a complaint in the morning and arrived at her door with the missing evidence that evening, after the deadline. She let him in and made him tell the whole story with the kettle off. He still checks the hour before promising he will think about something. “A theory’s like a paper boat. Lovely to watch. I wouldn’t put your shopping in it.”

The quiet auction

At a Stoned Boys evening someone offered a brass bracket as the hinge from a door that had never existed. Chada’s first instinct was to make him the feature act. A machinist in the audience recognised it as one of a batch she had made for a refuse truck. She said she would confirm the identification for a share of the door’s admission fee. The audience thought she had joined the joke and began placing bids. Chada let the room run long enough to sell two tickets to a second show before the machinist got tired of being laughed at.

He paid her out of his own fee and asked whether they could still use the hinge. She came back the next week to tell the story of the truck, which had driven the same route for twenty-one years. The room stayed to hear about damp gloves and christmas envelopes. The brass now props open the door to his listening table on humid nights.
