Music, Dance and Performance

First WitnessPerformance tradition · Section 77Last updated: October 2026

Some people met the First Witness through books. Many more heard it in rhythm, sampled it in songs or learned its phrases through performances that never looked like worship.

Memory likes rhythm

Repeated rhythm makes language easier to remember. Call-and-response traditions therefore spread rapidly through crews, work sites, marches, funerals and festivals.

No sacred genre

The tradition develops through whatever music communities already make: drums, choirs, rap, electronic forms, folk songs, industrial noise and later synthetic performance.

Sampling as commentary

Artists often sample old First Witness recordings the way scribes quote text. Context can honour, criticise or parody the source. Disputes over attribution mirror Book IV's remix ethics.

Dance and the body

Some schools treat dance as communal memory carried through bodies rather than text. Others distrust ritual movement as emotionally manipulative. Both criticisms have historical examples behind them.

Performance and propaganda

Block Topia perfected massive performances that fused devotion, order and state spectacle. Street cultures answered with improvised gatherings designed to be impossible to schedule completely.

The live mistake

A later artistic saying praises the live mistake because it proves the performance is not merely a perfect playback pretending to be present.
