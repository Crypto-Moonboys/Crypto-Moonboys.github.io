Solana (SOL)

✅ Verified📅 Last updated: October 2026📂 Cryptocurrencies

Solana is a programmable blockchain whose native asset, SOL, pays network fees and supports its proof-of-stake system. Its architecture emphasises rapid block production and parallel execution. Performance depends on workload, network conditions and confirmation requirements; a fast interface response is not the same as final settlement.

History and origins

Origins and mainnet beta

Anatoly Yakovenko’s 2017 proposal introduced Proof of History as a way to provide a verifiable sequence of events. Solana Labs developed the network with a broader engineering team, and mainnet beta launched in March 2020. The design combines that sequencing mechanism with stake-weighted consensus; Proof of History is not a substitute for agreement among validators.

The FTX and Alameda relationship

FTX and Alameda Research invested in and promoted parts of the Solana ecosystem during its early expansion. Projects including Serum, Raydium and NFT marketplaces helped make the network visible. These relationships did not make the network identical to either company, but they created financial and reputational exposure.

The 2022 shock

The collapse of FTX in November 2022 contributed to severe uncertainty over investments, asset sales and applications tied to its ecosystem. SOL’s price fell sharply. Network outages and congestion were separate engineering concerns; neither a price collapse nor a subsequent recovery alone measures technical reliability.

BONK and community activity

BONK’s December 2022 distribution became a prominent cultural event among Solana users. It is one example of how a token can attract attention and reinforce a shared identity. Claims that one airdrop single-handedly saved a network overstate causation and ignore continuing work by developers, operators and users.

Applications and network development

During 2023–2024, activity included trading through Jupiter and other services, NFT marketplaces, consumer applications and memecoin launches. BONK, dogwifhat and POPCAT are examples associated with Solana. BRETT is associated with Base, illustrating why similar meme themes do not identify a common network.

Engineering work addressed transaction transport, congestion and prioritisation. That work should be evaluated through dated incident and performance records. An improvement does not establish that future outages are impossible or that every application has the same reliability.

Investment products and changing status

Interest in exchange-traded SOL products belongs to a separate financial and regulatory history. Filings, approvals and actual trading are different events, and status differs by jurisdiction. An old report that approval is pending needs its date and jurisdiction; this reference makes no new claim about an active application or product.

How it works

Proof of History

Proof of History uses a sequential hash process to supply a verifiable ordering reference. Validators can check this sequence without relying only on a leader’s stated clock. It helps coordinate processing, but still requires consensus to decide which proposed history is accepted. A sequence of hashes cannot establish that off-chain claims are true.

Tower BFT and commitment levels

The established Tower BFT design uses stake-weighted voting and increasing lockouts when validators build on prior votes. Commitment levels distinguish processing from stronger confirmation and finalisation. Voting depth and lockouts concern successive decisions, not a simple count of independent validators that can be converted directly into a fixed settlement time.

Block time, confirmation latency and finality are different measurements. Applications should state the commitment level they require. No fixed finality time is asserted here, and exceptional failures remain outside normal consensus assumptions.

Block propagation and transaction forwarding

Turbine distributes block data in smaller pieces through the network. The transaction-forwarding design commonly called Gulf Stream sends transactions toward scheduled leaders. These approaches organise transport and reduce some bottlenecks; they do not eliminate congestion, guarantee delivery or create unlimited processing capacity.

Parallel execution

Solana transactions declare accounts they will access. The runtime can schedule operations touching independent state in parallel, while conflicting access must be coordinated. Parallel execution can improve throughput for suitable workloads; contention on popular accounts and resource requirements still constrain performance.

Reading performance claims

A useful benchmark states whether it counts user transactions, validator votes or both, describes the workload and separates attempted from successful transactions. Theoretical capacity is not demonstrated sustained performance. Fees also depend on priority and activity, so neither a universal sub-cent price nor an old headline throughput figure belongs in a timeless specification.

Applications and trade-offs

A shared environment with rapid processing can support payments, exchanges, collectibles and consumer services. Different applications value different trade-offs: frequent small actions may be sensitive to fees, while larger transfers require careful settlement and custody decisions.

Examples such as Jupiter, Raydium and Magic Eden illustrate application categories, not a guarantee of liquidity or permanent network support. A familiar token ticker or application name must still be matched to the exact network, asset and service terms.

Risks and trade-offs

  • Availability: Solana has experienced outages and periods of degraded service. Incident counts require a defined period and category.
  • Concentration: Stake distribution, operator independence, software diversity and hardware requirements affect resilience.
  • Applications: Wallets, contracts, bridges and custody providers introduce their own failure modes.
  • Distribution: Large holders and unlock schedules can affect markets without dictating a guaranteed price response.
  • Measurement: Comparing chains requires equivalent definitions for fees, throughput, finality and decentralisation.
