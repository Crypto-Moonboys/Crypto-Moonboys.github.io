Life on the Waterfront

The Police Robot took its current shift at the Waterfront after a repair crew trained it to recognise a queue from the back. It had previously reported clear passage whenever the person at the front stepped aside. Now it counts the people still waiting and asks who has been there longest. Its lamp is easy to recognise where the harbor streets narrow into the island paths.

The queue from the back

Its first beat ran between a weighbridge and a turnstile. The Robot could process a hundred clearances a shift as long as every person fit the width of a gate and could stand for the length of a report. A repair apprentice got it to change by asking it to carry her toolbox on the regulation route. Its return took longer than the paperwork allowed. It has kept a second tally on every shift since, recording the person who is still waiting when the front of the queue looks clear.

It will argue with the Police Bike about the difference between delivering a notice and changing what actually happens at a crossing. It trusts the car’s cabin clock when the depot clock rounds to the nearest quarter hour. Its collection of damaged numbers began with a seven it rescued from a closed lunch shop. If asked to identify a door it will tell you the paint colour, the last business and the one before it.

A place to wait

It keeps two records of every obstruction. One gives the time and location. The other records the account of whoever had to turn back. A shopkeeper once asked it to remove a folding stool from a landing. The stool belonged to the person who opened the landing each morning. The robot reported the blocked space and asked the shopkeeper to find that person somewhere else to sit.

Off duty

After the shift it collects damaged door numbers and sorts them by the depth of the paint. It knows every shop that has changed names twice. Its favourite conversation begins with a simple question. “Could you get through here carrying something heavy?”

The trial shift and the empty spaces on the tally

At the upper landing trial the Robot found the injured porter and entered the waiting passenger by her original place in the queue. What bothered it afterwards was the count of crossings that depended on a second person carrying. The count went up whether the helper volunteered or had been denied a chance to clock off. The porter later showed the Robot a one-handed way of steadying a handcart. It offered to hand him a commendation. He asked for the address of the office that could pay the next helper.

It now wants a line for assisted crossings on the depot’s form and expects resistance from anyone who likes a single number. A consistent tally might support the next paid shift. It might also be used to argue the landing needs fewer staff. Around the map the Robot asks visitors where they last had to turn back and listens for the reason before it asks how long the queue was.
