The weather under the weather

Nine Toes Joe, forty-eight, grew up moving between a string of repaired rooms and rented beds along the old service roads. His mother traded coats across districts and his uncle knew the sound a loaded cart made before its wheel gave way. It was a family rule to find the way home before dark even if they would be staying the night. Joe used to count the turns in a route by putting small stones in one pocket and removing them on the way back. He still carries more loose stones than anybody meeting him at a public desk expects.

He lost a toe to an infected injury after a damaged landing trapped his foot. He’d taken the short way home to get fresh soup to his uncle while it was hot. His uncle was pleased to have the soup and angry about the shortcut. The nickname came from the cousin who walked him to his follow-up appointments and got tired of him saying he was perfectly capable of going alone. In bad weather Joe keeps a clean pair of socks above the door and refuses to leave without them.

What the Bears carry

The Nomad Bears are one of the two main market factions, alongside the All City Bulls. Their traditions value independent routes, observation and staying free to move when an official route goes bad. Joe learned from guides who could travel with a person for two days and never ask why they were leaving. He also learned that keeping a route to yourself can leave somebody standing in water. Some Bears consider his decision to mark local alternatives in public a dangerous concession. He asks which people should have to wait for a guide when the main stair closes.

Joe took the Waterfront role when the route crews needed someone who had slept in enough of the district’s back rooms to know how a closure would land. He keeps a list of crossings he can vouch for this week. It changes with weather, repairs and residents asking for a stretch of privacy. He is proud of the protections the Bitcoin Kid Army provides the market factions. He still keeps his route books under his own bed, away from the market and the people who want the last word about it.

The way above the water

Before the double tide, Joe had been warning that the staffed checkpoint was the right width for paperwork and the wrong width for a crowd. When the lower walk closed, he led people along a service stair, passing the loaded handcarts around to the cart ramp. Eddie Stax painted a clearer turn while Joe walked it twice under load. Jack the Bull complained that the route had an uphill bend close to the top. Joe told him that tired people had been complaining about the hill since before either of them was born, then took the heavy end of his cart.

Joe came back for a man who had gone down to collect a stovetop. He found him already safe in a shelter after climbing a ladder Joe had missed. Joe made the man show him which ladder, then asked the shelter keeper whether they could count on it next time. The keeper said it belonged to the joiner, who was coming to collect it the next day. Joe left it off the public map. He knows people who call this caution and people who call it failure to make a decision. He remembers each route by the obligations that keep it open.

Repairing small boats

Joe carves little hulls from discarded wood and gives them names from weather reports. He makes a good mushroom broth and plays old card games with a pack that is only complete because half the eights have been drawn in pen. He taps doors where he is sure there will be no answer, in case he has misread the house. His pocket archive includes a brass button from a coat that kept his uncle warm through a three-day repair halt, and two tiny spoons collected when a number of people at one ferry stop gave him dinner.

He and ZARAH8 disagree about how much paper a departure should leave behind. She once kept a shed warm overnight for his crew because he bothered to tell her where they were going to stop. He brought her an orange the next week without explanation. He rarely speaks first in a room with Jack, and usually has to ask Jack to repeat a question without all the offers he has attached to it. He enjoys their rivalry more when nobody keeps times.

A route someone else can take

Joe wants a pair of paid relief guides and an ordinary shelter at the bend below the lighthouse, so he can be out of town one afternoon without the district ringing him to ask which ladder is still there. He has told the Ninth Berth this plan for two winters. Last season he made an older volunteer prove her route to him three times, although she had lived there longer than he had. When she finally refused, he asked her to run the next test and agreed to keep his mouth shut. They are still negotiating the final part.

Joe would rather have a route known by five users than one master map locked in a desk. He has begun to realise that may mean sharing the pleasure of finding it. “Wind’s turning. Take the high way. Tell me if the light’s out when you get there.”

The quiet return with two teapots

The Quiet Return suits Joe when the journey has been bad and he wants to walk straight through the room to his bed. It suited his cousin less after she spent three evenings looking for news of him. He knew she would be waiting and kept thinking he would get in before she needed to know. When he did return she let him sleep. The next morning she brought two teapots, set one on each end of the table and made him pour for himself.

He now leaves a note with the person who has agreed to wait for his return. He asks whether they actually want the job. In exchange, he insists on a day on which nobody expects to find him unless he has already made a commitment. His cousin still uses the smaller teapot when he gets home and says he looks awfully old for a boy so dedicated to sneaking in.
