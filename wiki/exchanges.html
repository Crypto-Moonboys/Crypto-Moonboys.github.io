Crypto Exchanges

✅ Verified📅 Last updated: October 2026📂 Tools & Platforms

Crypto exchanges provide ways to trade digital assets. They differ in who controls funds, how orders are matched, which networks are supported and what happens when a trade or withdrawal fails. A familiar brand, high reported volume or the label “decentralised” does not settle those questions.

History and origins

Early exchanges (2010–2016)

Mt. Gox began operating as a Bitcoin exchange in 2010 and became a major trading venue before its 2014 failure. Descriptions of its market share concern exchange trading, not all Bitcoin transactions. Its initial loss report was later revised when some coins were found; the event remains a major example of custody and record-keeping failure.

Bitstamp, Kraken, Coinbase and other firms developed different approaches to brokerage, exchange and custody during the early 2010s. Bitfinex’s 2016 theft provided another reminder that a company’s customer balances depend on operational security and recovery arrangements. Long operation is relevant history, but does not guarantee future safety.

Global platforms and on-chain exchange

Binance launched in 2017 and became a prominent global exchange. Coinbase’s 2021 Nasdaq direct listing made an exchange business accessible through public equity markets. A stock-market listing and regulatory registration have defined scopes; neither insures every customer asset or certifies every listed token.

On-chain exchanges developed alongside these firms. Uniswap’s 2018 launch popularised an automated market-maker design, building on earlier AMM and decentralised-exchange work. FTX launched in 2019 with a strong derivatives focus, demonstrating that rapid growth and prominent investors could coexist with serious governance failures.

FTX and the regulatory aftermath

FTX entered bankruptcy in November 2022 after a withdrawal crisis exposed misuse of customer funds and its relationship with Alameda Research. Sam Bankman-Fried was convicted of fraud-related offences and sentenced in March 2024. His sentence is a historical court outcome, not a measure of what every customer ultimately recovers.

Binance reached a major US enforcement resolution in November 2023 and its chief executive stepped down. Enforcement and market adaptation continued afterwards; the resolution did not end all unregulated activity or create one worldwide rulebook. Different venues and jurisdictions impose different obligations.

Types and operation

Centralised exchanges

A centralised exchange usually operates the matching engine and holds assets for customers. Internal trades may update company records without immediately moving assets on a public chain. Deposits, custody, withdrawals and any lending of assets are therefore separate parts of the arrangement.

Examples include Binance, Coinbase, Kraken, OKX, Bybit and KuCoin. This list identifies established names rather than ranking their safety, confirming present availability or recommending a provider. Services, legal entities and customer protections differ by location.

Decentralised exchanges

A DEX uses contracts or a network protocol to perform some exchange functions. Designs include liquidity pools, order books and aggregators that route orders among other venues. Users may retain their keys while granting contracts permission over particular assets or depositing collateral. Non-custodial key control does not mean the position has no custody-like, contract or administrator risk.

Interfaces can restrict access, and identity requirements differ among services. Fiat access may be supplied by a separate provider. Names such as Uniswap, Curve, Raydium and Jupiter identify different designs; the last is chiefly an aggregator, not simply another identical liquidity pool.

Derivatives

Futures, perpetual contracts and options provide exposure under contract-specific terms. Leverage magnifies losses as well as gains; margin and liquidation rules determine what happens when collateral becomes insufficient. Perpetuals commonly use funding payments to help align contract prices with an underlying reference. These payments are distinct from an ordinary spot-trading fee.

Fees and execution costs

A maker order adds resting liquidity; a taker order executes against available orders. Many order-book venues charge different fees for each, but a limit order can still be a taker if it crosses the book. Fees depend on the venue, account tier and product.

The complete cost can also include spread, slippage, network fees, withdrawal charges and derivative funding. A prominently advertised zero trading fee does not make a transfer or round trip costless. A quoted price may apply to only a small available amount.

Comparing venues

Reported volume, registered accounts and asset reserves answer different questions. Volume may include incentives or artificial activity; registrations need not be active people. Proof of reserves can show particular assets at a time without proving all liabilities, control arrangements or future solvency.

Useful comparisons specify the legal entity, supported jurisdiction, custody model, withdrawal terms, execution method and date of measurement. Old rankings and maximum daily volumes should not stand in for that information. The links retained below and in the reference list are starting points, not endorsements.

Why exchanges matter

Exchanges help discover prices and connect people seeking different assets. Some also connect bank payment systems with blockchain transfers. Others allow users to trade directly through shared contracts. Their value lies in the service actually provided, rather than a universal claim that every crypto user must use one.

A choice between custodial and self-managed arrangements changes the risks: company solvency and recovery processes on one side, key management and transaction authorisation on the other. There is no single custody arrangement suited to every person or organisation.

Risks and limitations

  • Custodians can lose assets, misuse deposits, pause withdrawals or enter insolvency. Customer recovery depends on the circumstances and legal terms.
  • DEX contracts, bridges, upgrade keys and collateral systems can fail despite users retaining wallet keys.
  • Thin order books and manipulated activity can make advertised liquidity misleading.
  • Leverage can trigger forced liquidation; a temporary price move can have permanent consequences for a position.
  • Identity records and account recovery processes create privacy and security obligations.
  • Jurisdictional restrictions can change access, supported products and withdrawal procedures.
