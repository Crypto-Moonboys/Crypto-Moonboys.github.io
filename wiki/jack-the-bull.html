A score somebody can check

Jack the Bull, thirty-nine, grew up on the edge of a freight run that served as a race track on the traders’ day off. His father painted numbers on borrowed carts and his older sister ran the timing bench. Jack was a fast starter with a bad corner. On one rainy run a friend climbed back up the hill to help him right a cart. The announcer credited Jack with finishing under his own power. Jack waited a week before correcting it. He can still remember the pleasure he took in the wrong version before he made himself give it up.

“Jack the Bull” started as a heckle from a rival who watched him shoulder a gate after a bad call. The gate opened so sharply that Jack dropped an armful of oranges. He carried the heckle on a race shirt the next week and had it painted across his first crew board when he took a shift organising meets. He likes a visibly fixed starting line and a clock everybody can see. He also likes to stand where the clock is most visible.

The prize and the work behind it

The All City Bulls are one of the two main market factions, alongside the Nomad Bears. Their arena culture turns competition, proof and public reputation into clout. Jack came into it because he wanted a result no back-room patron could change. He stayed when he learned how easily a public result could hide the people who made it possible. As a Waterfront representative he can commit his own crew’s labour and put his name behind a score. His crew has to choose whether to follow him the next time around.

The Bitcoin Kid Army protects members of both market factions. It answers to GKniftyHEADS under Alfie Blaze’s leadership. Jack understands a person can protect him and still tell him to mind his own business. He has no interest in making the Army responsible for an agreement his own crew signed. Knowing the boundaries is easier for him in a conversation than it is when he wants somebody in a hurry to clear a room.

Promises in public

Jack’s big Waterfront exhibition was meant to prove his people could turn a timed unload into a relief run with the same hands. He advertised the tonnage before the sponsor’s money landed. The double tide shut the lower walk and wrecked the show. His natural response was to keep the sponsor watching while he found a way to deliver. Nine Toes Joe put the two of them on opposite ends of a loaded handcart and walked him out of range of the cameras. They spent the next stretch of the night moving supplies uphill under a reserve lamp.

Jimmy Rugpull showed him the wage slips for the relief unload. Jack initially promised a bigger crowd and a better sponsor next week. Jimmy asked whether either paid this week’s wages. Jack pawned the award board from one of his best meets and made up the balance. He was tempted to mention it in the public hearing. His sister warned him she would have the award board back and over his head if he did. He gave Danny Rankz the figures and left the story of the trophy out.

What he does off the clock

Jack watches bad old racing tapes and makes a game of working out who finished just outside the camera frame. He is good at cooking rice for forty people, poor at cooking it for himself and keeps a collection of cheap sunglasses lost by visitors to the stands. He repairs canvas shoes with bright laces on the assumption that everybody would rather wear a louder pair. When a silence bothers him, he starts listing what he could do next, often over the person trying to explain why they wanted the silence.

He and Joe race only on ground they have walked together first. Joe has beaten him in the rain often enough that Jack now brings a thermos for the inevitable account of how it happened. Teddy Boi Jones makes Jack laugh in rooms where Jack would otherwise look for someone to outdo. Teddy says it is a waste putting him behind a podium, because his best stories come out carrying chairs into a room. Jack’s feelings on that subject depend on the quality of the podium.

Nine names at the top of the bill

Jack’s next meet at the Ninth Berth has a second board listing setup, lifting, timing, cleanup and every hand who wants to claim their shift. He has already caught himself placing his own name higher up than its job. He can start something that needed a push and can make a promise sound real before he has earned the confidence. People who work with him have learned to ask which part he has already paid for.

His long-term dream is a covered race hall with a late session for workers who miss ordinary meets. He wants the fastest time on the wall. He also wants the person who scored it to get home with both knees working and all of their wages. “Put it on the board. Put your name next to it. Then let’s see you do it.”

The heat he kept rerunning

In the Bulls’ arena circuit a timed heat can buy a crew a season of work. Jack once ran an evening qualifying session on ground that grew slick as the temperature fell. The early entrants had a much better run at the same bollards. An opposing coach called for a second heat. Jack saw that his crew stood to benefit from closing the board then and there. He agreed to rerun the last heat only. The coach went home furious, and Jack spent much of the winter at meets where nobody wanted him to work the clock.

His sister eventually got him a job timing a charity cart run with no sponsor and a rope that sagged across the start. It was the best contest of his year. He keeps the rope in the shed, and takes it down every time a visiting patron says people will only compete for a purse. He usually leaves out how upset he was that day when his own crew finished fourth.
