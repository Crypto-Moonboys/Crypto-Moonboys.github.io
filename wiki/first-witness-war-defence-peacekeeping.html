War, Defence and Peacekeeping

First WitnessConflict ethics · Section 48Last updated: October 2026

A civilisation shaped by collapse could never avoid the question of force. The First Witness does not offer a clean pacifist or militarist answer; it builds constraints around necessity, accountability and what must survive the fighting.

Defence is not conquest

Later commentators distinguish force used to stop immediate harm from force used to expand territory, status or ideological control. Every army claims defence eventually, so the claim requires evidence rather than slogans.

A faction crossing a boundary to prevent an attack may still become an occupier if the emergency ends and it refuses to leave.

The civilian line

Common Blood traditions insist that enemy civilians do not become legitimate targets merely because their government or faction is hostile. Infrastructure supporting immediate attack creates harder cases, especially in chain societies where civilian and military systems overlap.

The tradition treats uncertainty as a reason for caution, not permission for unlimited force.

Ceasefires

Book VI's claim that a truce is not surrender became central after the Triple Fork. Temporary ceasefires allow evacuation, burial, repair and negotiation without requiring either side to pretend the conflict is solved.

Breaking an agreed safe corridor is one of the most universally condemned acts across the Forty because it destroys the possibility of future restraint.

The HODL exception

When NULL threatens the memory substrate shared by all factions, ordinary conflict rules change. HODL unity permits temporary command structures and resource pooling that would otherwise be rejected.

The exception exists precisely because the threat is existential. Using HODL powers for ordinary faction rivalry is considered one of the gravest abuses of the doctrine.

Victory conditions

One military commentary asks commanders to state what outcome would allow them to stop fighting. If no such condition can be named, war has become identity rather than strategy.

A movement that needs an enemy forever eventually becomes dependent on the enemy's survival.

After the fight

The Keeper asks what happens after victory: prisoners, displaced families, damaged water systems, archives, unexploded devices, debts, grief and retaliatory cycles.

Winning the battle while making ordinary life impossible is treated as tactical success and civic failure.
