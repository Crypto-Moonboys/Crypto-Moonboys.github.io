Digital Inheritance and Memory After Death

First WitnessDigital remembrance · Section 71Last updated: October 2026

By later centuries, death no longer meant only possessions and photographs. People left wallets, identities, synthetic models, private archives and systems that continued acting after them.

The digital will

A digital will records account access, recovery methods, archives, deletion requests, licences and instructions for public material. It is kept separately from ordinary passwords where possible.

Keys are not wishes

Possessing a private key may grant technical control without granting moral ownership of everything accessible through it. Keeper traditions distinguish capability from entitlement.

Delete, preserve or release

People may request private material destroyed, family material preserved or creative archives released. Conflicts arise when historical value and personal privacy point in opposite directions.

AI replicas

Synthetic personalities trained on the dead require explicit labelling in most mainstream traditions. A simulation may preserve voice patterns without becoming the person.

Unfinished autonomous systems

Some people leave bots, contracts or agents still acting after death. The Unwritten Block becomes legally relevant: how much authority should the dead retain over future circumstances they cannot inspect?

The mercy delete

Families sometimes preserve everything because deletion feels like a second death. The tradition permits letting ordinary data disappear. Witness is not a command to archive every message forever.
