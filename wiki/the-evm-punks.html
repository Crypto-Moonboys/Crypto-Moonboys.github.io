⚔️ The EVM PUNKS

⚔️ Lore 📅 Last updated: October 2026 📂 Lore

The EVM Punks are Year 3008 builders and technical mercenaries whose independence is tested by the work they accept. Their crews can create alternatives to corrupt systems or serve the next paying power, with the difference visible in the people and obligations left after a contract ends.

Adult lore — 21+: These fictional accounts include strong language, adult relationships, intoxication, coercion, violence and lasting loss.

Builders, fighters and contractors

The EVM Punks are technically skilled crews whose Year 3008 culture joins independent building with the sale of force. Their name preserves the Ethereum Virtual Machine lineage of older computing traditions. In the dedicated Aggressive Digital Mercenaries account, they sell decisive results to paying clients. In the First Witness commentary, they read Fork as executable dissent: a corrupt arrangement can be challenged by building something people can actually use instead.

The tension between those readings is central to their society. The ability to create an alternative can free a neighbourhood from a dependency. The same ability, hired without regard to consequences, can strengthen the next patron who wants control. A Punk may speak sincerely about freedom and still find that the work paying for their tools serves someone else's authority.

They are an independent faction within the forty-faction civilisation, with several crews, workrooms and competing interpretations of their inheritance. No single contract speaks for all of them. Their shared pride is competence under pressure; their recurring failure is imagining that a completed technical task settles every obligation created by doing it.

Hard Smile: the mercenary people hire anyway

Dax Vanta, 42, is the public face of Hard Smile, one EVM Punk contracting crew. His coat carries a broad gold jaw plate that flashes when he laughs; cheap copies appear on workers who have never met him. He leaves one boot deliberately unpolished because a fully immaculate contractor looks, in his words, like a bastard who has never finished a job. Clients like the joke. So do the people he underpays. Recognisable style lets him arrive with a reputation before anyone asks which part of that reputation they are about to buy.

Hard Smile sells reliable completion to clients who want their chosen outcome more than they want a fair one. Its workers are technically capable, fast, boastful and often funny. A crew member who rescues a failed job can become a minor idol overnight; another who asks too often about the people excluded gets called a priest. Dax's favourite public line is that remorse belongs in the customer's budget. He makes the line sound like honesty. It also tells workers that acknowledging harm is weakness unless the acknowledgement can be billed.

Vera Hexen's Second Shift practices remain real. They represent a crew willing to accept less money to keep an exit usable. Dax treats that choice as a competitor's expensive personal preference. People exhausted by consultation sometimes prefer him because he names a price and does the work. The faction's decisive place in the world is this ability to build or enforce an alternative quickly. That ability can liberate a household, sell a ruler a stronger enclosure, or make a predatory arrangement dependable enough to survive public disgust.

Gas Wars and older code traditions

The dedicated archive account traces their practices to Gas Wars before the Datapocalypse: fierce competition over constrained capacity, speed and cost. It describes consciousnesses formed from developers and combat programmers who carried that rivalry into the post-collapse world. The current historical anchor is the World Chain's Triple Fork in 2880, followed by the Great Unravelling. The precise founding of the faction remains open.

Their older wiki tradition claims descent from the first smart contract and a rebellious mark in Ethereum's genesis history. Those are myths of technical ancestry, not a verified account of real Ethereum development. They explain why the Punks make origin marks important without identifying any present developer as an ancestor or member of the fictional faction.

The Covenant's 2030 Bitcoin witness, the later Sacred/Aether-Chain memory tradition, the collapsed World Chain and Block Topia's True Bitcoin Fork retain their distinct histories. By 2930 fewer than a dozen secure regions remained, with Queens becoming the city's foundation. The EVM name does not mean that every faction system runs on one surviving historical machine or that the whole world can be equated with a present blockchain.

Marks earned through work

The archive describes armour and consciousness forms marked with hashes from difficult engagements. Among the Punks, an earned mark can recall a completed task, a dangerous return or a moment when a crew held together under pressure. It is a claim with a story behind it, not a complete account of what happened.

A mark that proves a payment occurred does not prove the work was just. A celebrated engagement may have left another crew repairing damage nobody paid to recognise. Younger Punks learn those distinctions unevenly. Some treat every scar as authority; others ask who is absent from the story the veteran tells.

The older Ether Forge and unshackling-contract legends imagine tools capable of defeating any central power. They express the faction's desire for a final escape from dependence. They have not become guaranteed weapons. The more useful inheritance is a habit of asking whether the next user can understand and leave a system after its celebrated maker has gone.

Second Shift Hall

Second Shift Hall is a local Punk workroom and rest place used by several small crews. Its name honours the work after the public result: repairs, explanations, missing payments, equipment returns and the slow discovery of what a successful departure left unfinished. The hall has no authority over the whole faction. It earns repeated use because people can usually find someone there who remembers what was agreed.

Vera Hexen handles commissions for one of the crews. She has a strong reputation for finishing difficult work and a growing reputation for asking questions patrons would prefer to skip. She once believed a clear task and a clear price were enough. Repeated disputes over aftermath taught her that a contract can describe the client's desired outcome precisely while saying almost nothing about the people affected by it.

Noll Bittern maintains the crew's ordinary equipment and teaches newer workers. Noll is less visible in public accounts than Vera, but can usually identify which celebrated success consumed the spare parts another job now lacks. Their partnership works because Vera can bring work in and Noll can say when the hall cannot responsibly take more.

The Unpaid Hour

The Unpaid Hour began as an argument after a commission that the patron accepted as complete. The crew returned tired, with worn equipment and several follow-up questions from people using the result. Vera wanted to distribute the payment and begin the next job. Noll insisted that the unanswered work belonged to the first one.

The disputed hour became much longer than an hour. Workers had to explain unfamiliar arrangements, help return borrowed items and correct assumptions made during the handover. None of this resembled the dramatic task the patron had paid to celebrate. The cost came out of the crew's share, provoking an argument about whether Noll's standard made successful work impossible to finish.

The crew eventually separated completion from release. Completion meant the promised result had been delivered. Release meant the crew had accounted for the ordinary obligations it had created in doing so. The local distinction did not bind every patron or magically add money to a payment. It changed what Second Shift Hall was prepared to call its own success.

Learning without becoming disposable

New workers at the hall first assist with maintenance, explanations and low-stakes tasks whose consequences can be understood. Some resent the absence of spectacle. Noll answers that a person who cannot explain ordinary work is not made trustworthy by being given a more impressive tool. The lesson values competence without treating every beginner as a future champion.

Apprentices are paid for useful contributions where the crew has agreed to pay. Training cannot be used indefinitely as a reason to obtain labour without responsibility. Vera has had to turn down work because a patron's price assumed several people would contribute for exposure. That refusal protects the crew while leaving its own income uncertain.

The First Witness craft reading treats rest as maintenance and knowledge as something that should be passed on. At Second Shift Hall, a retiring worker leaves a modest unfinished task with enough explanation for another person to continue. The custom matters more than the object's importance. A brilliant specialist who makes everyone dependent on their permanent availability has created another fragile system.

The Exit Bell dispute

Exit Bell is a small bell near the hall's work table, first used when a tired apprentice wanted to leave a discussion that had become an unofficial loyalty test. Nobody had ordered the apprentice to stay. Every attempt to depart, however, was greeted with another question about commitment. The room's celebration of freedom had become difficult to distinguish from pressure.

Noll rang the bell to end the meeting. Vera objected that important decisions were unfinished, then accepted that a decision made by exhausting someone into agreement was unlikely to be durable. The crew later adopted the bell as a way to request a pause when continued participation was being mistaken for consent.

It does not cancel a person's obligations or guarantee that everyone will agree about the right time to stop. A departing member still has to return shared equipment and explain unfinished work. The custom protects the possibility of departure while preserving responsibility to the people who remain. That balance is closer to the Punks' Fork reading than an exit that leaves every cost behind.

The Broken Handrail job

The Broken Handrail job was an unglamorous neighbourhood commission to make a shared access arrangement usable again after several parties had stopped recognising one another's permissions. The name came from a physical rail beside the meeting place, damaged during the same period and repeatedly ignored while everyone debated the more abstract dispute.

Vera's crew helped the parties describe a smaller arrangement they could actually maintain. Noll insisted that the handrail be repaired as part of the work rather than left outside the technical brief. A resident with limited mobility had been unable to use the meeting place where the decisions about access were being made. The omission had shaped the agreement before any celebrated technical problem was addressed.

The job brought little status and less profit than the crew hoped. It also provided a durable example of executable dissent. People gained an alternative they understood well enough to question, while the makers accepted that usability included the route to the room. No single change abolished conflict, but fewer decisions depended on an absent authority explaining them.

The High Hats, the city and the paying side

The dedicated account names High Hat and Crypto Moongirl commissions and places the Punks against rebel networks and commercial rivals. Those relationships complicate the older image of automatic anti-authoritarian heroes. A patron can buy technical independence for itself while paying the same crew to restrict someone else's choices.

Crews respond differently. Some maintain that a clearly settled payment ends the moral discussion. Others restrict whom they will work for or what consequences they will accept. Vera's crew has no universal blacklist that resolves every case. It asks who will depend on the result, who can challenge it and whether the stated task conceals an obligation the patron wants somebody else to bear.

That practice can cost contracts. A crew refusing a powerful client may lose access to equipment or future references. Yet acceptance has costs too: neighbourhoods remember who arrived on behalf of an authority, even when the workers describe the engagement as purely technical. Reputation cannot be fully assigned by the person paying the invoice.

The Alchemists and competing kinds of expertise

The Code Alchemists are professional rivals in the main Punk account. The Alchemists regard destructive efficiency as crude; the Punks regard elaborate memory transformation as slow and self-important. Their argument is partly about technique and partly about who gets to define the result worth paying for.

Other archive war narratives put them on the same side, building extraordinary machines together. Those accounts do not erase the rivalry. They show that two schools can need one another without reaching agreement about the world's nature. A narrow collaboration also does not settle the Genesis Kernel's ontology or make a completed campaign victory part of the present history.

Noll respects a specialist who can say what was changed and what remains uncertain. Vera is impatient with an explanation that cannot identify when work will stop. Both criticisms can be useful; both can become unfair when directed at a problem neither understands. Second Shift Hall keeps those disagreements close to the work instead of treating faction pride as evidence.

Rivals, suppliers and the outside Kids

The Punks' archive account disdains the Rugpull Miners because fraudulent promises undermine the market for paid expertise. The distinction matters to them: a hard bargain that is openly stated differs from a promise deliberately misrepresented. The First Witness honest-scales reading adds a challenge they cannot avoid. An agreement reached under extreme need may still exploit the person who had little room to refuse.

The Ducky Boys and Salvagers supply repair knowledge and material support that the Punks' reputation for speed can conceal. A delayed part can decide whether a commission is possible. A damaged workroom can outlast the engagement that caused the damage. Dependence on these workers is an ordinary fact, not an embarrassment to be erased from the crew's story.

The dedicated account treats Alfie's escaped Bitcoin Kids as useful sources of information or distractions. That is a mercenary perspective, not the whole relationship or a right to use people without regard to consequences. The Bitcoin X Kids remain the distinct inside generation. Describing them as irrelevant to profit does not describe the value of their lives.

Contract Storms, campaign ritual and consequences

The source names Contract Storms and Fork Bombs as the Punks' feared forms of code warfare. They belong to the saga's language of damaged infrastructure and temporary fractures in shared reality. The important consequences are loss of service, displaced work, interrupted agreements and people forced to live with a result they did not commission. Their names are not instructions for attacking a present system.

The Calling tradition gives the Punks an arena trial before collective HODL defence. The same archive also contains proposed game missions. Ritual imagery is not proof of a live membership requirement, and a battle account does not establish that every Punk always answers without dissent. Forty remains the civilisation model without filling its archival gaps from every campaign roster.

HODL Warriors are the collective-defence tradition; HODL X Warriors remain distinct. A crew that contributes during an emergency still owes explanations for ordinary harms. Shared danger can create solidarity without cancelling an unpaid balance, a damaged home or a person's reason to mistrust the crew now asking to be welcomed as protectors.

NULL and the failure of a final guarantee

NULL threatens the Punks' defining bargain: skilled work in return for a verifiable settlement. The dedicated account imagines a completed mission followed by a corrupted payment record. It calls the faction's ultimate ambition the Final Contract, a promise to preserve paying clients through the Final Fork. The promised outcome remains unresolved, as does the faction's ability to control the threat it studies.

A payment crisis can expose disagreement over what loyalty meant all along. Some workers understand it as nothing beyond the last settled task. Others recognise years of shared tools, care and risk that a damaged record cannot fully cancel. Second Shift Hall keeps ordinary testimony beside formal accounts because neither payment data nor personal memory alone explains the whole relationship.

Vera's most difficult statement to a patron is sometimes that the crew cannot guarantee the requested result. It can cost more status than losing an argument to a rival. Noll considers the admission a mark worth keeping: a limit recognised in time leaves people able to decide what risk they actually want to share.

What remains after the client leaves

The hall's most valued object is not a trophy from a famous engagement. It is a patched work table bearing notes from several crews who no longer use the room. Some are instructions, some corrections, and one is a blunt request that the next user stop leaving damaged equipment for somebody else. The table keeps a history of competence less flattering and more useful than a row of victories.

Vera wants the crew to become wealthy enough to refuse bad work without making every refusal a household crisis. Noll wants enough trained colleagues that rest does not mean abandoning essential knowledge. Neither ambition ends the faction's mercenary problem. They make visible the ordinary needs behind the posture of limitless technical independence.

The First Witness calls the Punks' virtue permissionless building and their danger consequence-free escape. Second Shift Hall, Unpaid Hour and Exit Bell are local attempts to live between those extremes. The future remains unsettled. A meaningful alternative is one that people can use, question and leave while still recognising the people who built and maintained it.

Sources and continuity

The main account is W81 w36, The EVM Punks: Aggressive Digital Mercenaries. W69 repeats its broad profile; w72 supplies Calling and initiation imagery alongside proposed game systems; w25 offers a contrasting joint war effort with Alchemists. W29 and w30 contain other technical and cosmological battle accounts, whose absolute capabilities are not treated as verified real mechanisms.

The First Witness faction commentary, work and craft reading and honest-scales reading provide the ethical frame. The W81 evidence and migration ledger and legacy article preserve first-contract, Ether Forge and unshackling legends. They do not supply a real developer biography, live offensive capability or a completed Final Contract.

The Bright Mouth contract and the profit that stays

For Split Current Night, Hard Smile has a limited contract to maintain Bright Mouth's protected supply booking. An ordinary distribution failure leaves other service-quarter rooms dark. Dax learns that keeping his client's allocation intact will extend the neighbours' wait. He keeps it intact. The cruelty lies in the selected priority, the bargaining and the knowledge of who will bear the delay.

Tessa Brunt, 29, works with Dax and wants the gold jaw on her own coat. She hears downstairs workers asking for their stored food and suggests opening a temporary shared interval. Dax tells her the interval belongs to the booking they were hired to preserve. She stays, completes her task and accepts the fee. When the crew is praised the next week, she enjoys hearing her own name. She has not been deceived about the neighbours' loss. The job gives her money and pride alongside something she cannot make sound admirable.

Vera confronts Dax afterwards. He answers that her own customers will still need his parts when their righteous systems fail. She says that is why she dislikes him. Neither persuades the other. Ilva pays Hard Smile; another well-funded host hires the crew after seeing how firmly it defended the first booking. Some workers refuse its future work, but the refusal does not empty Dax's schedule. Tessa starts sending part of her earnings to a relative and defending the job more aggressively whenever anyone calls it ugly.

Hard Smile's scar in the local story is a protected bright room beside an injured porter and spoiled dinners. Its reward is a larger client list. The EVM Punks now contain a visible contest over what successful work means, rather than a single faction temperament. The paying side can win for a while. A builder's freedom to choose a contract also exposes the people who never got to choose its consequences.

Every participant in the new adult scenes is over 21. The account leaves the Final Fork, disputed origins and the six unnamed faction positions open.
