⚔️ The BALLY BOYS

⚔️ Lore 📅 Last updated: October 2026 📂 Lore

The Bally Boys are the brokers of the fractured Year 3008 economy. They connect buyers who cannot safely meet, find value in abandoned components and charge for the dangerous distance between a promise and its delivery. Their territory runs through the markets of the Street Kingdoms and the trading fringes of Block Topia. Masks, street leverage and a reputation for violence surround their work, but commerce is its centre. Their gift is making exchange possible where institutions have failed; their corruption is believing that every human obligation can become a transaction.

Adult lore — 21+: The Red Ledger Night sections contain strong language, adult sexual relationships, sex work, drugs, coercion and lethal violence.

A faction held together by transactions

The Bally Boys belong to the Forty Paths, the wider civilisation of forty faction cultures. They have no established right to speak for every merchant, and the archive supplies no complete membership list. A Bally desk may be a permanent stall, a travelling broker or a small group sharing risk. Independence means freedom to negotiate with opposed factions, not freedom from contracts, neighbours or the consequences of a broken bargain.

In the First Witness commentaries, they read Keeper and Chain through street leverage, status and transaction. They are quick to expose a ruler who demands sacrifice while protecting private reserves. They are slower to notice the same behaviour in a broker who calls an inflated emergency price the natural order of things.

Sera and the mask that pays for supper

Sera's commission after Red Ledger Night remains paid. At Margin House, adult brokers tell the story as proof that she can survive an ugly room without losing the deal. Her patched supper mask has become a small local icon: one bright side, one plain side, both recognisable above a customer's cup. Cheap imitations circulate with jokes about which side is speaking. She enjoys the copies and hates having the joke used when she is trying to look trustworthy.

Bally power is the meeting of people who would otherwise fail to trade. It is also the fee extracted for being the person they cannot easily bypass. Sera can supply a real scarce component and arrange a favour no official notice could obtain. A merchant who depends on her may find that every later buyer is somehow still her introduction. At one new adult supper, she gives a struggling carrier a meal and asks for a future commission before the dishes are cleared. The carrier takes the meal, negotiates a smaller share and spends the night hating how grateful he feels.

Other brokers try to borrow her look. One claims an introduction Sera actually made; she ruins his next deal by announcing the claim in front of a buyer. Two workers lose expected carrying pay when the deal collapses. She calls it necessary defence of her name. The Bally Boys stand out through glamour made portable: a mask, a table, a scarce thing somebody suddenly needs. Their idol is the broker who can get anything. The hidden question is whose access must become expensive for that reputation to keep paying.

The stable outline

The dedicated W81 account identifies arbitrage and influence brokerage as their defining trades. They move forgotten pre-Fork data, scarce computational capacity and specialist components between places that value them differently. They arrange exchanges between defectors and the GraffPUNKS’ Punk Net, supply the Games economy and lend to ambitious candidates. Their relationships with the Finance Guild, Blockchain Furies and Rugpull Miners alternate between conflict and narrowly useful cooperation.

The old street-fighter portrait preserves their masked appearance, anonymity pact and association with unrest. It does not establish that every Bally Boy is an assassin, that they command Block Topia’s alleys or that taking off a mask automatically destroys a person. The economic account explains why they might arrive before trouble: unstable districts generate both urgent need and profitable information.

Commerce after the World Chain

The World Chain Triple Fork of 2880 broke the shared systems through which ownership, money and identity had been recognised. The Bally origin account imagines a collective of entrepreneurs, hucksters and resourceful deal-makers answering that collapse. Its language of fused consciousness belongs to the archive’s mythic account; it is not a verified genealogy for every living member.

What survives across the versions is a practical opportunity. One district had usable parts but no buyers. Another had urgent need but no trusted route. A third could recognise a fragment nobody else understood. Brokerage grew in the gaps. By 2930, fewer than a dozen secure regions remained and the Queens stronghold underlay the future Block Topia. In 3008, Bally networks trade across that uneven geography without ruling it.

The Market of Fragments

For a Bally trader, value depends on context. An obsolete connector may be scrap until a pumping station needs exactly that fitting. A partial map may become valuable when its missing section is supplied elsewhere. A fragment claimed to descend from the Genesis Kernel may attract wealthy collectors while remaining useless to a repair crew. The same object can carry incompatible prices without one of them being a universal truth.

The Market of Fragments therefore includes translation work: describing condition, explaining provenance and finding somebody who can use the thing being sold. Its ugliest deals exploit the same uncertainty. Buyers pay a discovery premium only to learn that the broker withheld an easier source. An honest delivery can coexist with an exploitative price; the faction’s rivals rarely let it forget the difference.

Desks, runners and the Split Till

A local arrangement called the Split Till divides a deal’s proceeds among the person who found the goods, the carrier, the recogniser who inspected them and the desk that advanced the cost. It is a working custom, not a constitution binding every Bally member. More powerful desks try to count introductions as permanent ownership of a relationship. Runners argue that a single introduction cannot entitle somebody to a share of all future labour.

Sera Clasp, a broker who manages a small rotating desk, insists that the carrier’s share be written before departure. She is neither the faction’s leader nor its conscience. She still charges heavily for urgent work, and her refusal to surrender a commission has ended friendships. Her authority lasts only while enough people believe her desk will honour the next settlement.

Margin House and travelling premises

Margin House is the name of a shared Bally counting room in an outside market district. It contains more storage benches than grand offices: disputed parcels, empty containers awaiting return, folded awnings and cups borrowed from neighbouring stalls. Its physical address changes when tenancy, weather or local agreements fail. The name travels with the desk association rather than guaranteeing a permanent fortress.

Other brokers work near crossings and Games service yards. Such places are useful because people must pause there, but the Boys do not own every approach to the city. At the rotating Slate Market, established stewards can challenge their weights and close a stall. A trader’s feared reputation may improve bargaining power; it cannot make a broken bridge passable or force a thirsty neighbourhood to keep trading.

Masks and the cost of a hidden name

Balaclavas allow a broker to be publicly recognisable without making a household easy to trace. Marks, mannerisms and repeated performance become working identities. The anonymity pact makes sense in trades that connect enemies: a revealed intermediary can become a convenient substitute for the buyer whom a faction cannot reach.

That protection creates a recurring argument over accountability. Sera’s desk accepts hidden names but requires a continuing trading mark and a known witness to a disputed obligation. Other desks denounce this as unnecessary exposure. Their critics answer that a mask should protect a person from retaliation, not make every debt somebody else’s problem. No single solution is accepted across the faction.

Three Receipts

Oren Tally, a Bally settlement clerk, keeps the local custom called Three Receipts: what was promised, what arrived and what the recipient accepted. The records are compared because those moments regularly differ. A container can arrive intact and still hold the wrong grade of part; a household can accept a delivery under pressure while contesting the bill.

This practice is distinct from the Sacred Chain’s Three-Custody Rule. Oren is describing stages of a trade, not independent archival storage. His records make him unpopular with brokers who prefer one triumphant completion notice. They also protect sellers from buyers who use a minor defect to deny an entire delivery. Oren’s talent lies in tolerating incomplete settlements without pretending they are already closed.

Credit at the Hard Fork Games

W81 places Bally shops around the outer ring during the Hard Fork Games and Feast Week. The faction account emphasises vendors rather than competitors. That describes their economic role without making a universal claim that no member ever enters a trial. They sell scarce components and acquire the GraffPUNKS’ fictional Glitch-bombs for resale; their catalogue changes with the conflict.

Candidate loans are the trade’s most bitter legacy. A person seeking Block Node Defender status may mortgage equipment or future earnings to remain in contention. Failure leaves households facing claims against goods they need for ordinary work. A broker can accurately say the terms were accepted while ignoring how hunger, prestige and the city’s selection system narrowed the choice. Those disputes persist after the crowds leave.

The Cold Component dispute

During one Games season, Sera finances a cooling component for a candidate’s support crew. The part arrives, but the proposed installation never receives a service slot. The candidate withdraws before using it. Margin House demands the full premium, arguing that delivery ended its responsibility; the crew argues that the broker sold readiness, not a sealed box.

Oren’s Three Receipts reveal that both claims overstate the original agreement. The delivery is paid, the readiness premium remains disputed and the unused component returns to sale with its inspection history intact. Sera loses expected profit and a runner waits too long for wages. The case changes her desk’s wording, not the entire faction’s morals. It also leaves the withdrawn candidate outside the Games hierarchy without a newly invented champion title.

Work behind the bargain

The public remembers the broker’s final price. Behind it are packers, translators, repairers, cooks and people who spend hours waiting for a promised handover. Bally households frequently combine trade with other work. A courier may carry independent parcels, a recogniser may teach apprentices and a stall keeper may share childcare with neighbours who distrust the faction.

Newcomers learn to describe an object before pricing it. They must distinguish an observed fault from a rumour, a lender’s claim from possession and a copy from an original. More cynical mentors teach the opposite lesson: let the customer hear the interpretation that closes the deal. The conflict runs through apprenticeship because both approaches can produce short-term success.

The Empty Purse supper

At the local Empty Purse supper, a desk closes its books for the evening and pools a modest meal. Participants tell the story of a bargain they misunderstood. The funniest account often concerns a supposed worthless object that proved useful to someone else. Prestige comes from admitting a mistake convincingly enough that another trader can avoid it.

The custom is unevenly observed. Rich desks sometimes turn it into a display of losses they can easily afford, while carriers remember unpaid expenses that never become jokes. Sera’s supper keeps a place for anyone still owed wages by the desk. It makes the meal awkward. That awkwardness is why Oren considers it more valuable than a ceremonial pledge of fair dealing.

Furies, Miners and the Finance Guild

The Blockchain Furies challenge fraudulent trades, seek restitution and threaten profits the Boys consider legitimate. Their shared hostility to the Rugpull Miners can produce temporary cooperation. Bally brokers dislike scams that destroy the minimum trust on which repeat commerce depends, even while defending their own predatory terms as business.

The Finance Guild wants reliable settlement and access to the Boys’ market knowledge. Its audits constrain Bally desks but can also give wealthy actors more power over weaker traders. The relationship is mutual use rather than permanent alliance. A Guild finding may close a particular account without proving the whole grey market guilty or innocent.

Repairers, soldiers and city customers

The Ducky Boys turn speculative descriptions into uncomfortable practical questions: does it fit, does it work and who will maintain it? Brokers need them, and repairers need access to parts. Their arguments are usually about condition, priority and payment. The older tale of an all-consuming Bally–Ducky street feud is not the defining relationship in W81.

The Tuskon OGs are difficult customers because loyalty to the Aztec Raiders can outweigh an attractive finance offer. City officials can buy specialist supplies while treating the seller as suspect. During Feast Week, the Squeaky Pinks obstruct black-market traffic. The Boys answer these pressures through bargaining and shifting relationships, but the archive does not justify universal access to every city system.

Trade with the outside Bitcoin Kids

W81 portrays Alfie Blaze’s rebels as risky counterparties with valuable recent intelligence. A broker may prefer an old, easily recognised component to a newly issued promise whose value depends on a dispersed community. The outside Bitcoin Kids resent paying a premium for risks partly created by the people who control access to supplies.

The Bitcoin X Kids inside Block Topia remain a distinct engineered generation. The source’s “99% compliant” and “1% rebel” language is inherited faction rhetoric, not a new census or licence to collapse those identities. Bally caution around the city reflects surveillance and capture risk, not a claim that every inside child has the same wishes or life.

Brokerage behind the reopened Exchange

At the White Shutter, a Bally broker brings the workshop request, city stock and a Guild-backed advance into one bargain. The service is useful because each party accepts a different promise. The city requires an inspected lot, the carrier requires payment for a receiving shift, and the lender requires a bounded reservation on future accepted workshop output. Style gets the broker heard; specific terms get the load released.

The broker's commission is included in the advance account. Mina Patch consequently buys less stock than the headline sum suggests. The Margin House defends the charge as the price of making the arrangement possible. Mina accepts it because she cannot wait for another route and refuses to call it generosity. The bargain works without making everyone equally pleased or concealing which future hours have paid for the present relief.

A missed clinic receiving shift remains a dispute between the intervention, carriage and receiving arrangements. It is not silently folded into the borrower's stock as though everything purchased had arrived usefully. After the compact ends, the broker wants to service the independent escort too. Hosts consider the offer separately; the earlier deal gives no automatic right to their next contract. A successful intermediary can gain a returning customer and still be refused the wider loyalty he thinks his success deserves.

Street legends and competing portraits

The slashed balaclava, Shadow Alleys, mysterious curses and a leader known only through messages belong to the old masked-enforcer tradition. The most extreme versions make identity erasure inevitable on unmasking and place invisible traps in every refuge. These are rumours about Bally power, not established technical properties of their clothing or homes.

Such stories can benefit a broker: a frightening reputation reduces casual interference. They can also trap ordinary members in a role they never chose. Someone delivering a filter may be accused of preparing an uprising simply because a wall nearby carries the faction’s mark. The Boys’ reputation for noticing unrest is sometimes insight, sometimes profitable theatre and sometimes retrospective storytelling.

The Last Price

NULL and the Antichain threaten the distinction on which every trade depends: that a thing can be identified, recognised again and meaningfully exchanged. The Boys call the feared moment when this fails the Last Price. It is a market image, not a scheduled event. Nobody knows what a reserve is worth when the evidence of its existence becomes contradictory.

A few desks maintain a Closing Reserve of verifiable records and repairable necessities. Others hoard fashionable claims that will be worthless if nobody survives to honour them. W81 describes their hope that verified data will command the future’s highest price. That hope remains speculative. The Final Fork has no settled outcome, and the Boys have no guaranteed place in whatever follows.

The supper booking and the commission Sera keeps

Margin House brokers part of Red Ledger Night's hire: performers, table workers and adult companions. Sera Clasp, 43, writes the room's work limits plainly because a patron can mistake an expensive booking for ownership. Conversation hours, music and adult companionship are separate from any later private sexual arrangement, which the individual worker may accept or decline. The desk's fee is for the work actually booked. It cannot sell a person's continuing loyalty or promise that a guest will be desired.

Ione Vey chooses the dinner job knowing that Daro Sile is obnoxious and pays on time. A better choice would require another buyer, not a lecturer. Sera tells her which part of the fee remains due if the supper ends early. Ione asks if Sera will waive the introduction share because she knows several guests already. Sera refuses. 'Then put it on the page,' Ione says. 'I'm not buying the charming version.' They sign a bounded hire and dislike each other slightly more than before.

The companions' work includes performances of effortless attention. A guest tells the same story three times; a worker laughs twice and changes the subject on the third. A private patron wants somebody to remember a preference without calling it service. Others are lonely and surprisingly easy to like. Ione can enjoy a conversation and still be working. The room's corruption is not that every smile is false. It is that the guests can afford to confuse a paid interval with unlimited permission.

Oren Tally, 48 in this account, counts a break for the workers that Daro's steward initially tries to shorten. Daro mocks the desk for costing a party as if it were a freight shift. Sera says, 'They get tired in expensive rooms too, you cheap bastard.' She wins the full interval and keeps her original commission. Her firmness is useful; it does not make her generosity limitless or turn Margin House into a present-day booking business.

A dirty fee and an honest shortage

When the stolen private record becomes known, Ione asks Sera to return the introduction share. The desk did not sell the sound case, but the job introduced her to the room in which the loss occurred. Sera returns a part and holds the rest against completed arranging work and payments to others. Ione calls the calculation obscene. Sera thinks it is accurate. Accuracy gives neither of them the money needed to replace the bookings Ione has lost.

Sera goes with Ione to Third Ear because the visit needs someone who will remember exactly what the office promises. She does not demand control of Ione's claim in return. On the walk, Ione asks whether Sera would have refused the party if she had known what Daro would buy. Sera says she would have required another steward and no retained private record. Ione laughs without amusement. 'So you'd have sold the safer fucking night.' Sera has no splendid answer waiting.

The death of Jex Sable changes what remains payable from the later hire. He had been a 41-year-old porter and door worker, paid through the event account rather than a permanent member of the patron's household. His agreed wages and retained break money are identifiable. Daro wants to count his last interval as interrupted work. Oren refuses to subtract it. Collecting wages already earned does not value Jex's whole life or settle the compensation his partner seeks.

Sera releases the worker amounts her desk holds and waits for a separate unpaid contribution from the patron. That wait squeezes another small hire she had expected to finance. She has to tell those workers early rather than perform confidence with money she no longer has. One accepts the delay; another books elsewhere. The criminal case and the wage account overlap in people, not in a magical common reserve that can repair everything at once.

The Last Price after the party

The Empty Purse supper after Red Ledger Night is quieter than its regulars want. Someone jokes that the room finally found a commodity worse than a broken valve: an honest guest. Ione is not there to become the punchline. Oren stops the story before its teller gives her name. He then tells one about buying twelve matching cups and discovering that eleven only matched while empty. The laugh is smaller and deserved.

Sera is offered a further commission by Daro through a different steward. The hire is real work and would ease her shortage. She refuses to take his private-record terms, agrees to arrange a limited delivery without companions and charges more. Oren thinks continued trade gives Daro an undeserved appearance of ordinary respectability. Sera thinks refusing every job from every rotten buyer would close the desk before it punished a single patron. Their disagreement is not resolved by the larger fee.

The delivery happens and the workers are paid. Daro photographs the completed table without Sera's objection appearing in it. The picture makes him look generous. He has bought something ordinary and made it say more than it proves. Sera later sees the picture and swears at an empty room, which improves nobody's account but briefly improves her temper.

These local dealings sharpen the Split Till tradition without changing the earlier Open Crate advance. Mina's reserved output, the disputed component commission and the carrier shares remain as recorded. Bally brokerage keeps goods, wages and people moving; it can also give a powerful scoundrel a convenient route around the people who refuse him. The faction's hustle has a social cost even when the arithmetic is correct.

The Kiss Before Commission story

Margin House remembers an adult engagement supper at which two brokers agreed to stop charging each other for introductions. Sera admired the romance and predicted the bookkeeping would become ugly. She was right about the bookkeeping and wrong about the relationship: the couple remained affectionate while arguing fiercely about which of their customers had become common friends. Their private agreement did not oblige other brokers to waive fees or visitors to treat every invitation as a commercial lead.

A joke from the supper became the Kiss Before Commission: whoever admitted affection before calculating advantage supposedly owed the other person a free introduction. Adults repeated it during consensual flirtation, mostly because catching someone being tender was amusing in a room proud of its sharpness. Sera claimed never to have lost. A former lover produced a remembered sentence so embarrassingly gentle that she bought his dinner to stop him repeating it. He repeated it after dessert. She paid the bill and called him an arsehole with unmistakable affection.

Taste that makes a stranger useful

Bally style is improvised richness: a glorious sleeve on a worn coat, one excellent dish among cheap plates, a mask whose repair tells a better story than an untouched surface. Brokers want an object with provenance interesting enough to become another introduction. They can make a boring room feel like the precise place a person should have been waiting to enter. Their private dread is a successful exchange taking place without needing them.

Sera keeps names attached to favours long after the favour's recipient believes gratitude has finished its work. A generous meal may become the opening scene of a commercial relationship neither side describes as a debt. Younger brokers try to distinguish hospitality from a claim on future earnings, then discover how hard it is to fund a supper where nothing afterwards can be billed. A carrier can genuinely enjoy the evening while calculating whether staying for the next drink will cost more than leaving.

Bally loyalty attaches to a person who has made things happen, rather than to a uniform office. That creates glorious access and humiliating dependence. Sera's rivals can copy the mask but cannot copy every remembered introduction. She knows exactly why the room needs her and occasionally mistakes that knowledge for a right to decide how grateful it should sound.

Anonymity charged twice, collateral taken after death

Digital Entrepreneurs of the Grid makes anonymity a commodity sold twice. In its trades between Block Topia defectors and the GraffPUNKS’ Punk Net, both sides pay a premium for the promised guarantee. A broker can earn from the fear each party has of exposure without sharing either party’s cause. The guarantee belongs to the broker’s offer; it does not establish that every exchange remained secret or that the paying defector escaped the city’s attention.

The same account is explicit about candidate loans: failure or death triggers immediate seizure of collateral. Death is not a pause in collection. The dream of Block Node Defender status gives the lender a market, and the household left behind supplies the goods with which the failed dream is settled. The source also resells used GraffPUNK Glitch-bombs at a heavy markup. Its traders finance other people’s contests rather than needing to enter those contests themselves.

Fury scrutiny and Reversal Protocols force the brokers to spend on obfuscation. Their occasional cooperation against the Rugpull Miners protects the minimum trust a profitable market requires; it is not a conversion to restitution. Hoarding verified truth for a Final Arbitrage carries the same calculation into the feared ending. The value of that reserve after the Final Fork remains a hope, not an observed result.

Oren’s refusal to subtract Jex Sable’s earned wages remains a particular refusal, not a faction-wide repeal of predatory collection. Ione’s stolen private record, lost bookings and dispute over Sera’s introduction share remain unresolved costs of the supper hire. Sera can insist on breaks, enjoy a lover’s embarrassing tenderness and still sell the next bounded arrangement to a rotten patron. Neither a fair entry nor a dirty fee cancels the other. The older source supplies a harsher commercial norm beside these specific adult choices.
