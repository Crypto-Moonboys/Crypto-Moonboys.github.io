Addiction, Intoxication and Compulsion

First WitnessSelf-governance tradition · Section 75Last updated: October 2026

A tradition obsessed with external dependency eventually turned the same question inward: what happens when the gate you cannot leave is inside your own habits?

Use and captivity

The tradition distinguishes use from dependence rather than pretending every pleasurable behaviour is identical. The key question is whether a person can still choose, function and keep obligations without the substance or behaviour controlling the terms.

Shame rarely repairs dependence

Public humiliation may temporarily stop visible behaviour while deepening secrecy. Recovery schools favour honest records, practical boundaries and support over moral theatre.

Intoxication and consent

Impaired judgement creates special concerns around driving, violence, contracts and intimacy. Freedom to intoxicate does not erase responsibility for predictable risks imposed on others.

Digital compulsions

Later schools expand addiction language to gambling loops, feeds, games, markets and reputation systems deliberately engineered to capture attention.

Recovery as succession

A recovering person often needs to build routines and relationships capable of carrying them when willpower is weak. The Keeper becomes internal infrastructure.

The test

If stopping feels like losing yourself, ask who taught the habit to carry your identity.
