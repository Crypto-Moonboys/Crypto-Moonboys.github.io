The difference between a rumour and a witness

Danny Rankz, forty-two, remembers the first time a room decided somebody was guilty because they were easy to find. His mother worked the till at a bridge cafe. One night a collection tin went missing. Dad put a hand on the shoulder of the new dishwasher and asked her to empty her bag. Two or three customers got up to watch. The tin was found in a delivery crate the next morning. The dishwasher returned for her wages with her sister and never worked there again. The memory Danny carries is how quiet the regulars went when his father asked who had seen it happen.

He took a junior job checking cargo claims and learned that a list can make an argument long before anyone speaks. A warehouse manager used to sort the oldest cases to the bottom. Danny put them in order of who was having to pay out of pocket and got told he was ranking people instead of paperwork. Within a week the loading crew was calling him Rankz. He found out who was keeping a copy of the list and paid that person for their time. The money came out of his own shift.

The Furies’ uncomfortable trade

The Blockchain Furies pursue evidence and accountability across a world full of broken records, saleable outrage and hidden patrons. Danny relishes the moment a powerful person has to answer an ordinary question. He also knows a Fury can become addicted to a good accusation. As a young investigator he released a summary early. It made a pair of honest storekeepers look as if they had taken money for something that arrived after they had left. The correction circulated among professionals. The accusation remained pinned in a bar after the correction had gone into the bin.

His current rule is to let the injured person hear what will be made public and to seek repair even when the crowd wants a spectacular confession. Some Furies think this is a skilful way of hiding compromises. Danny usually responds by showing the repair terms, while keeping private details with the people who supplied them. He remembers the scratched table in his parents’ cafe any time somebody insists the case will be easy.

The hearing at the fish counter

After the double tide Danny took statements at a fish counter lent by its owner, who insisted he clear it by noon. The angry version of events said one operator had opened the waterfront to a flood for money. Danny had separate evidence of a stale reading, poor inspection practice, an unpaid work order and late closure. He asked ZARAH8 who had signed the inspection. He asked Jimmy Rugpull which shifts he could prove had been worked. He asked Chada Boi exactly what the bell recording established. The owner charged him for ice because one statement ran past noon.

His report attached costs to actual decisions. Workers received the balance of their wages. A stallholder got part of her equipment loss covered. Two households received no compensation for ruined goods because the agreed fund ran dry. Danny made sure the names of the two households could stay private and printed the shortfall in full. Some people still say he traded justice for orderly minutes. He sometimes wonders what he could have secured if he had let the crowd shout longer.

Hobbies that refuse to sit neatly

Danny grows hot peppers and labels them with the names of ships he has missed. He owns three second-hand coats identical enough that nobody can tell which one his cat has ruined. He plays dominoes in a cafe where they put a chipped tile at the end of the table for the next player and do not let him keep score. He enjoys a properly unkind joke if the target has enough money to answer it. He opens a window when taking a difficult call, even in winter.

Jack the Bull appreciates Danny’s insistence on a visible result and hates how slowly he gets there. Eddie Stax fights him about what a witness ought to be able to take back after publication. He and Teddy Boi Jones share a taste for old radio plays. Danny likes when a character is allowed to talk themselves out of the audience’s affection. Teddy likes giving them a second scene.

The part still owed

Danny sits at the Ninth Berth on two evenings a week. His table always has a vacant chair facing away from the crowd for people who need to speak quietly. He would like a compensation fund with enough money to answer a real disaster. He also insists any fund should allow a claimant to challenge him without having to confront him personally. He is looking for someone who could chair such an appeal and has ruled out three candidates for disagreeing with him in ways he found irritating. He has started to notice the problem.

When pushed for an opinion he gives the part he can trace and says where he stopped. As a friend he can listen to a story for much longer than the teller intended and then ruin the effect by asking about the end. “I’m with you as far as that door. What happened after it closed?”

When the evidence bought the wrong thing

Danny once paid a yard clerk for his time in assembling evidence of duplicate fuel charges. He explained the arrangement at the hearing. The patron whose account was at issue said Danny had bought the testimony. The clerk asked for his statement to be withdrawn altogether. It was the first time Danny understood what it cost to be publicly described as somebody who could be bought.

They kept the claim open and found the two charge slips through separate customers. The difference amounted to less than the cost of assembling the evidence. Danny has never been sure how much his persistence was about recovering the money and how much was about beating the patron in public. He keeps both slips between the pages of a detective novel. He has read the first half three times and continually loses his place where someone charming does a stupid thing with a cigarette.
