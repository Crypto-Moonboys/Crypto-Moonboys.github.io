Counterfeit Relics, Forged Hashes and Sacred Scams

First WitnessFraud history · Section 68Last updated: October 2026

Anything people value attracts counterfeiters. The First Witness generated a particularly strange fraud market because authenticity itself was one of its sacred themes.

Fake Concord fragments

Forgers repeatedly produced supposed lost pages supporting whichever doctrine was politically useful. The best fakes copied period language while introducing one convenient clause.

Hash theatre

A technically valid hash proves only that some data existed at some point. Scammers learned to display complex verification interfaces that proved something real but not the claim buyers thought was being proved.

This became known as hash theatre.

Relic laundering

Ordinary old objects gain prestige through invented ownership chains. A stylus becomes “the stylus used at the Concord.” A storage device becomes “the first Sacred Fork node.” Each resale adds another citation to the same unsupported story.

Prophecy editing

Some fraudsters publish a vague prediction privately, alter inaccessible copies later and reveal the “original” only after the event. Prophecy ledgers were partly created to defeat this technique.

Sacred investment schemes

Prosperity Witness groups repeatedly sold financial products implying religious alignment guaranteed returns. Mainstream doctrine classifies guaranteed spiritual profit as both theological corruption and ordinary fraud.

The fraud lesson

The religion's uncomfortable conclusion is that people who care deeply about authenticity are not immune to scams. They may be easier targets because they desperately want one object to settle uncertainty.
