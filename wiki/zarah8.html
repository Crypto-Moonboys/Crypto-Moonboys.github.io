The eighth line

ZARAH8 is a thirty-four-year-old Crypto Moongirl and the faction’s representative on Lumina’s Waterfront. She learned to read a room by listening to its pipes. In her childhood block a failing pump made the cutlery chatter before the lights gave any warning. Her mother, a night-shift laundry worker, could tell which floor had opened a tap from the change in pitch. Zarah used to put an ear against the wall and call out the floor numbers. She was right often enough to become unbearable about it.

Her name comes from a maintenance form with seven printed lines for affected households. On the night the lower laundry flooded, seven authorised addresses went into the record. The caretaker lived behind the boiler and had no separate address. Zarah wrote him on an eighth line herself. A supervisor called it a breach of procedure. The caretaker put an eight on the handle of the wrench he gave her. Years later she put it on her work jacket, then on everything she signed.

What she owes Block Topia

The Crypto Moongirls govern and defend Block Topia under Queen Sarah P-fly. Their service networks take skill and discipline to keep alive. Zarah spent eleven years learning what that means under the floorboards, where a public guarantee has to become clean water or a door that physically opens. She likes the clarity of a handover with two signatures. She has seen people who despise the city accept its treatment when their own generators fail. She has also withheld service from a building while it waited for a valid occupancy record. A woman there lost a pantry’s worth of food during the delay. Zarah still has the woman’s list, with the cost in pencil.

She came to the Waterfront because the city wanted a person who could negotiate access to maintained equipment without promising the district permanent city support. The district wanted someone who could persuade a depot to lend a part without making everybody fill out a residency form. Both sides underestimated how much time she would spend arguing with them. Her mandate reaches the equipment and the agreements she signs locally. The Queen runs the city, and Zarah answers for the work she personally puts her name to.

First light at the Ninth Berth

Before the Night the Tide Came in Twice, Zarah had certified the lower walk using a gauge that passed each scheduled check. On a live shift the gauge and the water disagreed. She stood in the service shed long enough to see the displayed reading come back after the lights flickered. It was the same reading. The oily mark on her boot was a joint higher than it had been a minute before. She shut access to the lower walk and told the upper depot to release a reserve lamp and lifting gear.

Her original order sent evacuees through the staffed checkpoint. Nine Toes Joe told her the checkpoint would count documents while people were still on the stairs. She struck out that clause on both copies. Later, when the depot asked how many names she had verified, she supplied a head count and the readings she had made herself. She kept the rejected confirmation letter beside her earlier certificate. The two pieces of paper tell the part of the night she would rather leave out.

The glass garden

Zarah brings home broken lamp lenses and arranges them in shallow trays. The green lenses make her mint look sickly. The amber ones make the basil look far healthier than it is. She exchanges cuttings with a laundry electrician called Ina Silt, who can make almost anything root and refuses to return the shallow trays. On her coat the front pocket always holds twine, two pencils and a square of dry cloth. She wipes the chair before she sits down even when she is the guest. On her days off she swims in the old training pool and makes a point of never timing herself.

She and Nobby fell out when his crew asked to leave a working piece of reserve equipment with residents. She feared the volunteers would be left to cover a municipal duty. He asked why she was willing to leave those same residents with no reserve at all. They eventually set up paid handovers with two keys and a record of each handover. They still argue about who takes the expensive call at three in the morning. Eddie Stax earned a permanent seat at her kitchen table when he painted an anonymous evacuation diagram and agreed to remove a sketch that would have identified somebody living above the yard.

A plan with room for a person

At the Berth’s hearing Danny Rankz asked Zarah to say aloud who had signed the first certificate. She said her own name and then tried to explain the form. Danny let the answer sit before asking the next question. She hated him for most of that week. Her revised inspection route now requires a second person to walk the steps and independently record the waterline. She has been trying to get that second shift funded instead of quietly doing it twice. Fatigue lies very well in her handwriting.

Zarah’s five-year plan is a trade school with heat, paid training and a room the trainees can book for whatever they please. She has already turned down one sponsor who wanted everyone’s residency status. In conversation she asks for one detail at a time, picks her words carefully and will stop to count a queue in the middle of an argument. She dislikes an anecdote she cannot act on and is slowly coming to understand that some people tell her things simply because they trust her. “Give me the reading from the step you’re standing on. We’ll work out from there.”

The hour the pool belonged to somebody else

Before she had a Waterfront post, Zarah spent an autumn on the Moongirls’ ward service. The warm-water schedule included an hour for staff to swim. A clerk doing hydrotherapy was told she could use the pool in that hour if she wore an overshirt and kept away from the lane ropes. Zarah used to grumble about finding the shallow lane occupied. One week the clerk stopped coming. The list showed a successful discharge. Zarah found her walking the corridor at midnight because the available rehabilitation class conflicted with a shift she could not afford to lose.

They arranged a half-hour after closing. Zarah wanted to call it an accommodation and ask the service to provide it. The clerk asked for a key and the name of someone she could call if the hoist stuck. They got both after a messy month of forms. Zarah was rotated out just when the arrangement started working. She loves an uninterrupted swim now, and puts her towel down last when there is a queue for the warm changing cubicle.
