The Satoshi Scroll

🌙 Community Lore 📅 Last updated: October 2026 📂 Lore

The Satoshi Scroll is the HODL Wars’ sacred text tradition inspired by the Bitcoin whitepaper. Carried as a blueprint by the Bitcoin Kid Army and interpreted by successive communities, it has become an object of study, dispute and practical care. Its history lies in copies, margins and the people who make time to read together, as well as in the legendary authority of its absent author.

The real whitepaper and the fictional Scroll

Bitcoin: A Peer-to-Peer Electronic Cash System, published under the name Satoshi Nakamoto in 2008, describes a peer-to-peer payment system and its approach to the double-spending problem. The historical whitepaper is a technical document. It is not a book of predictions about Block Topia, a command to hold an asset forever or a promise that every participant will profit.

The Satoshi Scroll is the HODL Wars’ literary transformation of that document into a foundational object. In W81’s subject register, it is a blueprint carried by the Bitcoin Kid Army. The older wiki tradition imagines warriors reading it before a Moon Mission. Those descriptions establish its cultural role without proving that every recruit has read an identical copy or that the surviving Scroll has a single uncontested text.

The three teachings and their later interpreters

The older Scroll tradition names decentralisation, self-custody and HODLing as three sacred laws. In the fictional world, each has acquired meanings beyond a payment system. Decentralisation asks who can make a decision and who can survive their absence. Custody asks what it means to hold something for oneself or another person. HODLing becomes a language for endurance through uncertainty.

These are later interpretations, not quotations of three numbered laws in the whitepaper. They can conflict in practice. A community may distribute authority and still depend on one exhausted organiser. Someone may insist on personal custody while leaving a dependent household without a workable handover. Endurance can preserve a promise or prolong a harmful commitment.

The Scroll’s usefulness lies partly in making these arguments possible. Treating it as an answer that forbids questions would place its interpreters above the scrutiny they claim the text demands.

An object made for carrying

In the outside world of 3008, a foundational text must survive movement. Copies travel with people who have other loads, other deadlines and limited room for possessions. A beautiful edition may be too heavy to carry; a small edition may omit the explanation a first reader needs. Damp, wear, hurried copying and unfamiliar language change the experience of a supposedly stable text.

The Folded Page room keeps examples of these practical compromises. It is a small teaching and repair room serving travellers in the Street Kingdoms, not the sole repository of an original. A stitched copy has wide margins because its first owner wrote questions there. Another has replacement covers made from a retired notice. Neither becomes less worth reading because its material history lacks grandeur.

Reading also uses fuel, light and somebody’s patience. A visitor who listens for an hour has postponed another task, while a custodian who prepares the room has done work that disappears from most accounts of revelation. Mara keeps a small repair basket beside the readings so that willing listeners can mend ordinary bindings while they listen. Participation is optional: someone with tired hands may simply sit. The room’s welcome is diminished whenever useful work becomes an unspoken fee for being allowed to learn.

The room’s custodians distinguish the inherited wording, a later explanation and a reader’s addition. The distinction does not silence the margin. It lets a future reader know which voice they are hearing.

Mara Fold and Ivo Parch

Mara Fold repairs the room’s worn copies and schedules readings around the work of people who use it. Ivo Parch helps readers compare versions. Mara learned that a reading held at an impressive hour can exclude everyone receiving late deliveries. Ivo learned that correcting somebody’s pronunciation in public can prevent them from asking the more important question they brought.

Their first disagreement concerned an annotated copy whose owner had crossed out a passage from a later commentary. Ivo wanted the commentary restored in a clean hand. Mara asked whether repair meant restoring a book to the form its teacher preferred or preserving the use the owner had made of it. They eventually supplied a separate replacement sheet, leaving the crossing-out visible.

The solution was small, but it shaped the room’s habits. A reader could request help without surrendering the history of their disagreement. Ivo still explains why he thinks the omitted passage matters. He no longer describes his preferred version as the book before damage.

The Two Bindings reading

The Two Bindings reading began when travellers brought copies with different endings. One ended after a discussion of responsibility; the other carried a confident WAGMI declaration. Several listeners assumed the shorter copy had been deliberately weakened. Others thought the longer one had been altered to serve a recruiter’s enthusiasm.

Mara placed the books beside each other and asked their carriers what they knew of the copying. Neither could supply an unbroken history. Ivo read both endings, then the earlier passages on which both groups agreed. The meeting did not authenticate an original. It established that the disagreement was real and that neither carrier should be accused on the strength of an assumption.

Afterwards, the room retained a description of the difference with permission from the owners. A later visitor asked which version had won. Mara pointed to the two spaces on the shelf. The room had kept a comparison available; it had not acquired authority over every Scroll in circulation.

Custody, dependence and a missing afternoon

A teaching about personal responsibility sounds different to a traveller with few belongings and to a carer holding documents for several people. At one Folded Page session, an enthusiastic reader argued that dependence itself was a failure. A visitor left before the discussion ended. They had already missed part of a care shift and could not spend another hour defending why another person depended on them.

Mara changed the next session’s arrangement so the visitor could attend briefly and receive a written account of the rest. The change did not solve the philosophical dispute. It showed that the room had been demanding a kind of independence that its timetable made possible only for some readers.

This local lesson sits beside First Witness custody practices without rewriting them. Three-Custody concerns independent failure arrangements; it is not simply a demand for three copies in the same room. The Copy Ledger records attribution, not ownership of a person. A sacred vocabulary does not excuse careless use of either distinction.

The Scroll and the Bitcoin Kid Army

The archive’s description of the Scroll travelling with the Bitcoin Kid Army places it beside movement, service and argument. A carried blueprint can remind a group why it formed. It does not certify every command issued by someone holding the book. A recruit may know the teaching through a reading, a borrowed copy, a remembered line or a dispute they heard while working.

The outside Bitcoin Kids are the escaped generation, distinct from the engineered Bitcoin X Kids living inside Block Topia. Nothing in the Scroll account completes an inside mass awakening or gives the outside Army automatic authority over every child. A text carried by one group can speak to another without absorbing its separate history.

Mara occasionally reads with returning travellers who want the room’s warmth more than a lesson. She allows them to listen without being introduced as evidence that the Scroll has converted anyone.

The Open Hinge annotation

The Open Hinge is a local annotation written after a traveller returned a copy with several leaves damaged. The traveller apologised as if returning an imperfect book were worse than not returning it at all. Ivo began to lecture on care. Mara asked how the copy had been carried and learned that it had shared a bundle with tools needed for urgent repair work.

They repaired what could be repaired and kept a note of what remained unreadable. Ivo wrote the new annotation separately: a teaching that cannot survive meeting ordinary obligations has become too fragile to guide them. He did not sign it with the name of Satoshi or place it among the historical whitepaper’s claims.

The annotation acquired readers because it answered an experience they recognised. Some disliked it, arguing that the room had made negligence too easy to excuse. Their objections stayed with it. The Open Hinge became a conversation about care, not a universally accepted fourth law.

Additions, covenants and a borrowed authority

The Rug Pull Wars tradition attaches an Anti-Rug Covenant to the Scroll. It asks readers to take promises seriously enough to question them and to answer when their assurances have helped cause harm. The WAGMI tradition adds a declaration of collective hope. These later layers can be meaningful without being attributed to the historical author of Bitcoin.

Neither addition replaces the Great Concord of 2030 or the Covenant preserved by First Witness. Shared religious language does not make every covenant the same event. A copied supplement can borrow prestige from the word original; careful readers ask which original, whose copy and what history of transmission is actually known.

At Folded Page, later additions have their own headings. A donor once offered better bindings on condition that the headings disappear into one seamless text. Mara declined the condition and accepted fewer repaired copies that season. The cost was real: some readers waited longer for a book.

The Lost Satoshi and the wish for an answer

Satoshi Nakamoto is the name used by Bitcoin’s creator; the identity behind it remains unestablished in this account. The Scroll’s mythology turns absence into a source of authority. If the author cannot return to object, every faction can imagine that its preferred interpretation would have received approval.

The older page went further, treating a supposed holding, silence and motives as certain evidence of perfect virtue. Those claims are not needed to explain the legend. Neither an estimate about early coins nor a story about withdrawal from public life proves a person’s motives, present ownership or future actions. The fictional patron figure is a literary construction around an unresolved historical identity.

Ivo’s introductory readings leave a blank space where some editions print a portrait. Readers can discuss why they want a face there. The blank is not presented as a recovered authorial instruction; it is the room’s way of preventing an invented likeness from silently becoming a fact.

Who may interpret a sacred text

Chain Scribes value the Scroll as an object with a transmission history. Code Alchemists may be drawn to damaged layers and difficult reconstructions. GraffPUNK readers care about what a teaching means when carried into streets whose lives exceed official records. These interests can cooperate and compete. A spectacular reconstruction may attract attention while a plain correction reaches the person who needs it more directly.

A local reading group has authority over its meeting, not every interpretation in the world. At Folded Page, Mara chooses how long a session lasts and who can use the repair table; those are powers that affect access. Ivo chooses which comparison to begin with; that can shape the disagreement before anyone has spoken. Their claim to openness is tested by how they use these ordinary decisions, rather than by the loftiness of the text on the table.

Prophecy without a settled ending

The Scroll’s later legends look toward a Moon Mission, WAGMI and a world where shared memory survives its enemies. Hope does not establish the route or guarantee every arrival. A tale in which all readers become prosperous is not evidence that the same outcome has already occurred, in fiction or in a real market.

The wider chronology preserves the World Chain Triple Fork in 2880, the fewer than twelve secure regions by 2930 and the Queens foundation of Block Topia, with 3008 as the current setting. Sacred or Aether-Chain, World Chain and True Bitcoin Fork remain distinct. The Scroll is a text tradition, not an additional chain or a device that reconciles their histories.

The Kernel’s exact dating, the Sacred Fork’s chronology and the Final Fork’s outcome remain unresolved. Later readers cannot settle those questions by attributing a convenient prediction to an ancient author.
